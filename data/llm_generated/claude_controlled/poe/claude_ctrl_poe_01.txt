The stranger came at that hour when the last honest light has withdrawn from the world and the darkness that remains is not merely the absence of day but the presence of something older and less indifferent. He came on foot, which was itself remarkable, for the road that led to our town from the outer world was not a road that invited walking; it was a road that invited the kind of haste which puts distance between a man and whatever he has left behind. He walked without haste. He walked as a man walks when he has nowhere he is more urgently needed than where he is going, and nowhere he is going matters more than the fact of his arrival.

I watched him from the window of the inn, where I had been sitting for an hour in the particular species of restless inactivity that afflicts a man who has finished his supper and has not yet found sufficient reason to retire. The night was clear and cold, and the stranger moved through the pale illumination of the moon with a deliberateness that I found, even at that distance, and even through the imperfect medium of glass and shadow, somehow unsettling. It was not his pace that disturbed me, nor his silence, nor the absence of any companion. It was something in the quality of his attention. He looked at our town—at its modest houses, its closed shutters, its single street lamp that flickered in the wind with the persistence of something that refuses to admit defeat—with the expression of a man who has been given a description of a place and is now verifying each detail against some internal record.

He stopped before the door of the inn and stood there for a moment that extended itself, in my perception, well beyond its actual duration. I had the sensation—irrational, I acknowledged even as I experienced it, and yet insistent—that he was not deciding whether to enter, for that had been decided long before he arrived at our street, long before he set foot on our road, perhaps long before he was born into the particular chain of events that had deposited him, on this particular evening, before this particular door. He was pausing, rather, in the manner of a man who has arrived at a conclusion and is allowing himself a moment, the last such moment he will have, to appreciate the simplicity of what comes next.
