I have set myself the task of recording, with as much fidelity as my faculties permit, the events that followed the arrival of the stranger; and I find, having set myself this task, that the greatest obstacle to its completion is not the inadequacy of my memory, which is, on the contrary, perhaps too precise, but the inadequacy of the medium in which I am compelled to work. Language was designed for the communication of ordinary experience; it was built for transactions between people who share a common world and require only the most efficient means of conveying information about that world from one consciousness to another. For the communication of experiences that do not belong to the ordinary, it serves badly, and the worse the experience departs from the ordinary, the worse language serves.

His arrival was, in the most literal sense, unremarkable: a man, alone, on foot, at night, approaching a town. There is nothing in this description to which even the most impressionable reader could object, nothing to which any sensible person could attach a significance beyond the ordinary significance of such events. And yet—I write this with the full awareness that I am describing something for which I have no satisfactory explanation, and with the resignation of a man who has learned that the absence of explanation does not constitute the absence of the thing to be explained—there was nothing ordinary about it. There was nothing ordinary about the way the air seemed to change quality as he approached, to become at once heavier and more transparent; nothing ordinary about the way the sounds of the night—the wind in the remaining leaves, the distant complaint of some nocturnal animal—seemed to organize themselves, as he drew nearer, into something that was almost but not quite a pattern.

He stopped at the edge of the square and looked at our town, and our town, as I have said, looked back at him—or so it seemed to me, watching from my window with the particular clarity of perception that comes in moments when one understands, without yet understanding why, that something is ending.
