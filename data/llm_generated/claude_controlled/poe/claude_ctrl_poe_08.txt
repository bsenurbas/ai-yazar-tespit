There is a species of dread which is distinguished from ordinary fear by the absence of any identifiable object; ordinary fear has a name, a face, a set of circumstances which, being named and faced and understood, can at least be confronted and, if not defeated, at least engaged. This other dread is different; it has no name, no face, no circumstances specific enough to permit engagement; it exists as a quality of the air, a property of certain silences, a characteristic of certain kinds of light—or certain kinds of darkness—that announces itself to the nerves before the mind has been given any information adequate to justify the announcement. It is this dread that I felt, standing at my window, watching the stranger cross our square.

He had come a long way. I knew this not from any external evidence—his clothing showed no unusual wear, his bearing showed no unusual fatigue—but from the same source from which I knew everything about him that I knew, which is to say from a source I cannot identify or name, from that region of apprehension which precedes and underlies the ordinary processes of perception and reason. He had come a long way, and he had been coming for a long time, and our town was not an accident on his route but a destination, chosen or perhaps assigned—I could not have said which seemed more terrible—long before he set out.

The square was empty at that hour except for himself and, invisible to him, myself. The lamp above the inn door cast its yellow light in a circle of moderate radius, and he walked through the darkness toward this circle with the unhurried certainty of a man who knows that the light will be there when he arrives, because it has always been there, because everything he needs has always been there, arranged in advance by whatever arranges such things. When he stepped into the light I saw his face for the first time—and I will not describe what I saw there, not because it was terrible, but because it was, in the deepest and most disturbing sense, familiar.
