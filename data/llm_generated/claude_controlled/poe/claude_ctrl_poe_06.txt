The soul has its seasons, as the year has its seasons, and there is a season of the soul that corresponds to the late autumn night on which the stranger came to us—a season not of death exactly, but of the preparation for death, of the stripping away of everything that is not essential, so that what remains is exposed to the cold air in its full and unprotected particularity. I had been in that season for some time before he arrived, and his arrival seemed to me, at the time, not so much an intrusion into my solitude as a fulfillment of it; as though my solitude had been a form of waiting, and his arrival was what I had been waiting for.

He came from the direction of the old road, which surprised me, for the old road had not been maintained and was not, by common consensus, passable at night. Yet he came from that direction with no more difficulty in his bearing than a man who has walked a path so many times that its obstacles have ceased to register as obstacles, becoming instead features of a familiar landscape, as intimate and unremarkable as the furniture of one's own room. He walked through our town with the same ease, the same unhurried proprietary familiarity, the same sense of a man moving through space he has long since claimed by right of some understanding that predates and supersedes legal title.

What I remember most clearly—more clearly than his face, which the darkness partly concealed, more clearly than his voice, which I did not hear until later—is the quality of his stillness when he finally stopped. He stopped before the inn and stood there with a stillness so complete, so absolute, so different from the ordinary stillness of a man pausing to consider his next action, that it seemed less like the absence of movement than the presence of something—some force or intention or awareness—that had temporarily taken the form of stillness as a matter of convenience, and might at any moment resolve itself into something else entirely. Into something for which our language, designed as it is for the ordinary commerce of ordinary experience, has not yet found an adequate name.
