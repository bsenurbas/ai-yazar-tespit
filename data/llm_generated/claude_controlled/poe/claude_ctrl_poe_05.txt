He arrived on a night of extraordinary silence—the kind of silence that is not passive but active, that presses against the ears with a weight one does not notice until it is suddenly, without warning, interrupted. The interruption, when it came, was the sound of footsteps: steady, measured, neither hurrying nor reluctant, the footsteps of a man who has settled the question of his destination far enough in advance that the walking itself requires no further decision. I heard them for a long time before I saw him, and in that interval—during which I remained at my desk with my pen suspended above the paper on which I had been attempting, without success, to write—I formed an impression of the approaching figure that was, I later reflected, more accurate than any visual observation could have been.

He was a man who carried a great weight that was not physical. This was the impression I received from those footsteps, measured and deliberate and relentless as they were, without the drag of fatigue or the lightness of relief, but with a consistency that suggested something beyond either: the gait of a man who has made his peace with the necessity of his errand without having made his peace with the errand itself. When I finally rose and looked from my window, I saw a figure that confirmed this impression in every particular; tall, dark-coated, moving through the moonlit street with the unhurried certainty of something that cannot be stopped and knows it.

The town received him in the way that towns receive what they have long feared and long expected: with a silence that was somehow different from the silence that preceded his arrival, heavier, more deliberately maintained, the silence of collective breath held. Doors that had been open a crack were gently, quietly closed. The few lights still burning in windows were extinguished, one by one, not in panic but in the orderly, almost ceremonial manner of people who have rehearsed this response many times and are now, at last, performing it in earnest. Only I remained watching, unable to perform my own part in the general withdrawal, held at my window by a fascination that I recognized even then as not entirely my own.
