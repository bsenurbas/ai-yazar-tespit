Looking back on it now, with the advantage of knowing what we subsequently learned, the arrival of the stranger seems inevitable in a way that arrivals rarely seem, even retrospectively. At the time, of course, it seemed like nothing at all—a man checking into an inn, ordering a meal, going to bed. The ordinariness of it was, I now understand, part of its logic. What was coming did not announce itself. It checked in quietly and ordered the fish.

His name, he said, was Aldous Reeve, and he gave his occupation as surveyor. This was plausible. Surveyors did occasionally come through the region, and they had the kind of peripatetic, solitary professional life that made a man difficult to verify and easy to accept at face value. He had the right kind of equipment—I saw it later, when he had established himself—and the right kind of manner: methodical, precise, given to looking at things for longer than ordinary people look at them and then writing something in a small notebook.

What he was actually surveying, we did not understand for some time.

The clues were there, if one had known how to read them. His questions, which he put to various residents over the first several days, followed a pattern that was not the pattern of someone assessing land or property. He wanted to know about water: the depth of wells, the behavior of the river in different seasons, the location of springs. He wanted to know about the soil at depth—not the agricultural properties of the topsoil but what was beneath it, how far down the bedrock lay, whether there had ever been any unusual findings during construction or excavation. He asked these questions with the systematic thoroughness of someone working from a checklist, and he noted the answers with the same careful attention he gave to everything else.

Nobody thought much of it. Surveyors asked all kinds of questions.
