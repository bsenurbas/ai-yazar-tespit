The stranger arrived on a Thursday evening in late October, and the manner of his arrival was, at first glance, entirely unremarkable. He came on foot, from the direction of the main road, carrying a single bag of moderate size, and he went directly to the inn and took a room for the night. These are the facts as they were observed by several people independently, and they are not disputed. What is disputed—or rather, what proved, upon closer examination, to be more complex than it appeared—was the question of where he had come from and why.

The village of Merton Cross had, at that time, a population of approximately four hundred persons, and it was not the kind of place that attracted visitors without reason. It lay at the end of a branch road that led nowhere else, and its single industry—the manufacture of a specialized agricultural component—was not of the kind that brought commercial travelers or buyers from the cities. Visitors came for specific purposes, and their purposes were generally known in advance. The stranger's purpose was not known, and this was the first source of interest.

The second source of interest was more difficult to articulate. Several people who encountered him in the first day or two noted, independently and without apparent consultation, something in his manner that they described in various ways but which amounted, when the various descriptions were assembled, to the same thing: the impression of a man who was paying attention to something that other people were not paying attention to. He would pause in the middle of a conversation and look at something—the sky, a wall, the surface of the road—with an intensity that seemed out of proportion to what was there. When asked what he was looking at, he invariably said "nothing" and returned his attention to the conversation, but the pause had happened, and it was the kind of pause that stays in the memory.
