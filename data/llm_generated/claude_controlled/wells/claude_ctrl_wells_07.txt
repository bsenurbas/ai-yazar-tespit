What we knew about him, in the end, was this: he had arrived on a Thursday, he had stayed for eleven days, he had paid his bills promptly and in full, he had been polite to everyone he encountered, and he had left without warning on the morning of the twelfth day, before most of the town was awake. He had taken everything he brought with him and left nothing behind except the impression he had made on various people, which was, when those impressions were compared, considerably more consistent than one might have expected given how little direct information any individual had gathered.

The consistency was itself informative. When multiple people, having had limited and different contact with the same individual, form the same impression of that individual, it generally means one of two things: either the impression reflects something real and therefore consistent across observers, or the individual has been very deliberate about the impression he created and has managed it carefully enough to achieve the same effect from multiple angles. Both possibilities said something about the stranger, and both were worth considering.

The impression, in this case, was of someone who was conducting a scientific investigation of some kind. Not scientific in the loose sense of careful and methodical, though he was both, but scientific in the more specific sense of someone who has a hypothesis and is testing it against observable reality, someone for whom the data of daily life—the behavior of water in the pipes, the settling of foundations, the growth patterns of the vegetation in the untended lots at the edge of town—were not background but foreground, not context but evidence.

Evidence of what? That was the question that remained after he had gone, and that remained unanswered for longer than was comfortable, and that was eventually answered in a way that made us wish, retrospectively, that we had asked it sooner.
