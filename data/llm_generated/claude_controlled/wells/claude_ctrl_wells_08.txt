The economic disruption caused by the arrival of genuinely new technology follows a pattern that historians of industry have documented many times: initial dismissal, followed by gradual recognition, followed by a period of competitive adoption that advantages early movers and disadvantages late ones, followed eventually by a new equilibrium that accommodates the technology as a normal feature of the landscape. The human disruption caused by the arrival of a genuinely unusual person follows a similar pattern, though the timescales are compressed and the equilibrium, when it comes, is psychological rather than economic.

We dismissed the stranger initially because dismissal is cheaper than engagement and because the cost of being wrong about a dismissal is, in most cases, low. We recognized him gradually as something worth paying attention to, and this recognition spread through the town at approximately the rate that interesting information always spreads: faster than practical information, slower than alarming information. We did not reach the stage of competitive adoption, because there was nothing to adopt—he had not offered us anything except his presence and his questions, and his presence was temporary and his questions were unrevealing. We reached, instead, a period of collective puzzlement that lasted for the duration of his stay and some time beyond it.

The equilibrium came eventually, as equilibria do, not through understanding but through the passage of time. He had come, he had done whatever he had come to do, and he had gone. The town continued. The wells continued to produce water, the foundations continued to hold, the vegetation in the untended lots continued its slow and patient work of reclamation. Whatever he had been measuring, or testing, or recording, had not produced any visible consequence.

Not yet. That qualifier is important. The consequences of what he did, and of what we failed to do in response, took some time to become apparent. But they did become apparent, and when they did, they were of a kind that no one in Merton Cross had had occasion to prepare for.
