It is a fact of some interest that the human mind, presented with evidence that does not fit its existing categories, does not generally revise its categories. It revises the evidence. It finds reasons why the anomalous data point is not really anomalous, why the exception does not challenge the rule, why the thing that seems strange is, on reflection, perfectly consistent with the way things normally are. This capacity for retrospective normalization is, in many circumstances, a useful feature of cognition; it prevents us from being paralyzed by every minor deviation from expectation. But it can also prevent us from recognizing, until it is very late, that something genuinely new has arrived.

The stranger was, for most of his time among us, a beneficiary of this tendency. The things about him that did not fit were fitted, one by one, into categories that accommodated them. The hours he kept were those of a dedicated professional. The questions he asked were those of a thorough researcher. The places he visited were those of someone with an unusual specialty. Each anomaly was absorbed into a narrative that kept him legible as an ordinary man with ordinary purposes.

It was Sanderson—the pharmacist, a man with a literal cast of mind that made him less susceptible than most to the reassuring power of narrative—who first suggested, about a week into the stranger's stay, that the various oddities might not be independent features of an unusual but essentially normal person, but might instead be symptoms of something that had not yet been diagnosed. He put it in those terms, the medical terms of his training, and the metaphor was more apt than he knew.

"What if we've been asking the wrong question," he said to me one evening. "What if the question isn't what kind of man does things like this, but what kind of task requires a man to do things like this."

I remember thinking it was an interesting distinction. I did not yet understand that it was the crucial one.
