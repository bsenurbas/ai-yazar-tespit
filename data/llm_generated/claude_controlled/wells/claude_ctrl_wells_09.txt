The question of what constitutes sufficient evidence for a conclusion is one that philosophers have debated for centuries without reaching agreement, and the practical reason for this is that the answer depends entirely on the cost of being wrong. For low-stakes conclusions—whether to carry an umbrella, which road to take—the evidence threshold is low, because the cost of error is low. For high-stakes conclusions, the threshold should, in principle, be higher. In practice, it is often lower, because high-stakes situations produce anxiety, and anxiety produces the desire for resolution, and the desire for resolution produces a willingness to accept conclusions on the basis of evidence that calmer circumstances would find insufficient.

I offer this not as an excuse but as an explanation. By the time we had assembled enough evidence to reach a conclusion about the stranger, we were not in a calm state of mind, and the conclusion we reached was therefore less carefully examined than it might have been.

The evidence was as follows. He had arrived with knowledge of the town that he had not declared—knowledge of specific streets and buildings that a first-time visitor should not have possessed. He had spent eleven days conducting what appeared to be a systematic survey of the town's physical infrastructure. He had asked questions about underground geology that were more specific than any ordinary professional purpose would require. He had left suddenly, before dawn, without farewells, and had not been traced to any destination.

The conclusion we reached was that he had been sent to assess the town for some purpose we did not understand, by some organization we could not identify, for some reason that had not been explained to us and might not be explained to us. This conclusion was correct, as far as it went. Where it fell short was in its account of the purpose, the organization, and the reason. Those three gaps turned out to contain rather more than we had room for.
