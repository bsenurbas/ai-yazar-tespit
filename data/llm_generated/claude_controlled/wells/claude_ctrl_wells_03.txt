The thing that strikes me most, when I try to reconstruct the sequence of events with accuracy, is how long it took us to ask the obvious question. We accepted the stranger's presence, we noted his habits, we formed various opinions about him, and it was not until the fourth day that anyone thought to ask, in a direct and literal way, what he was doing there. This is, I think, characteristic of how communities respond to the presence of something outside their experience: they fit it into the nearest available category and then stop looking at it.

He fit reasonably well into the category of "solitary professional man passing through," and so that is what we took him to be. His actual category was something we did not have a name for, which is why we did not look for it.

What I observed, during those first days, was this: he slept little, or appeared to. The light in his room was visible until late at night and again early in the morning, and the hours in between were shorter than one would expect if he were getting anything like a normal amount of sleep. He ate regularly and without apparent appetite, in the manner of someone maintaining a physical system whose optimal function he considers important. He walked the perimeter of the town each morning, at a pace that suggested purpose rather than exercise, and he made notes during these walks in the small book he always carried.

He was measuring something. This was apparent even before we knew what. The measurements themselves were not visible—he carried no obvious instruments beyond the notebook—but the behavior of a man who is measuring has a quality distinct from the behavior of a man who is simply observing, and after a few days I had become fairly certain that this was the former.

What he was measuring, and why, and for whom, were questions that seemed, at the time, like the kind of questions one could defer indefinitely. One cannot, of course. But that understanding came later.
