The population of a small town is, in certain respects, an extremely efficient information-processing system. News travels quickly, anomalies are registered rapidly, and the collective assessment of a new situation is generally reached within twenty-four hours of the situation's arising. The collective assessment of the stranger, reached by the usual processes of conversation and inference, was that he was a geologist of some kind, probably employed by one of the mining companies that had been taking an interest in the region, and that his presence was temporary and professional and of no particular concern.

This assessment was wrong in almost every particular, but it was reasonable given the available information, and I do not record it as an example of communal stupidity. I record it as an example of the way in which the frameworks we use to interpret new information shape what we are able to see, and therefore what we are able to know.

He was with us for eleven days. During those eleven days he was consistently courteous, consistently unhelpful in the sense of never explaining himself beyond what was necessary for ordinary social function, and consistently, quietly, purposeful. He went places in the town that visitors did not normally go—the pump house at the edge of the industrial quarter, the old mill that had been disused for twenty years, the stretch of the river where the bank was eroded and the underlying geology was exposed—and in each of these places he spent time that was longer than curiosity would explain but shorter than anything that could be called work.

He was, I now believe, taking a census of something. Not of the town's population, or its economy, or its geography in any conventional sense. He was taking a census of something that required him to observe the town from many angles, in many lights, at many times of day, and to record his observations in the small notebook with the systematic thoroughness of a scientist working through a protocol.

What the protocol was, and what the census was for, I only understood after he had left.
