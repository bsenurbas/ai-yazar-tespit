The landlord of the Black Horse was a man of limited imagination but reliable observation, and when he told me that the stranger had arrived at eleven-forty on a Tuesday night, having come on foot from the direction of the quarry road, and that he had paid for his room in coins rather than notes, and that he had asked, before going up, whether the town had a telegraph office and what time it opened in the morning, I understood that these details, individually commonplace, collectively told a story worth following.

"What did he look like?" I asked.

The landlord considered. "Ordinary. Medium everything. The kind of man you'd pass in the street and not remember."

"But you remembered him."

"Well, it was nearly midnight, wasn't it. Not many ordinary men walk out of the quarry road at midnight." He paused. "And there was his hands. A man's hands tell you what he does, my father always said, and this man's hands didn't match the rest of him. Rest of him said clerk or schoolmaster or something quiet. Hands said something else."

"What did they say?"

"That he'd worked hard with them once, a long time ago, and hadn't forgot how to."

This was, I reflected, a more acute observation than the landlord probably realized. A man whose hands preserved the memory of physical labor while the rest of him had moved on to something less visible was a man with two lives, or a man who had exchanged one life for another. Either possibility was suggestive.

"And the telegraph office," I said. "Did he go?"

"First thing, before breakfast. Sent a wire and waited for a reply." The landlord met my eyes. "Reply came in twenty minutes. He read it, burned it at the candle on the counter—the girl said he just held it to the flame like it was perfectly normal—and went back to his room for his bag."

"And then?"

"And then he was gone. Before I'd finished my own breakfast."
