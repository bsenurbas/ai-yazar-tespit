My companion had said nothing for the better part of an hour—a silence I had learned to interpret as a sign that something in the situation had engaged him at a level beneath ordinary conversation—when the door of the inn opened and the stranger walked in.

"Now that," said Pemberton quietly, setting down his glass, "is interesting."

I looked at the newcomer with fresh attention. He was of medium height, conservatively dressed, and he had the kind of face that is designed, whether by nature or by effort, to be forgotten: regular features, unremarkable coloring, an expression of mild and general neutrality. He should, by every ordinary criterion, have been invisible.

"What do you see?" I asked, in the way that had become habitual between us.

"I see a man who has spent considerable effort constructing an appearance of ordinariness and has very nearly succeeded. The coat is deliberately chosen for its anonymity—mid-price, mid-color, mid-cut, the kind of coat that five thousand men in this country are wearing tonight. The boots are similarly chosen. Even the way he holds himself has been modulated—he's suppressing his natural posture, which is more upright than what he's showing us." Pemberton's eyes moved over the figure with the quick, systematic attention I had observed many times. "But the right hand gives him away. He's keeping it slightly away from his body—not much, just enough to allow for quick access to the inside breast pocket. A habit. The kind of habit a man develops when he's accustomed to carrying something in that pocket that he may need in a hurry."

"You think he's dangerous?"

"I think he's cautious. Whether that caution is offensive or defensive I haven't yet determined." He picked up his glass again. "But I intend to find out. Go and introduce yourself. You have an honest face, which is useful in these situations."
