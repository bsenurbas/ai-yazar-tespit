"The difficulty," said Crane, turning from the window where he had been watching the stranger cross the square below, "is that he knows we're interested. He's known since he walked in."

"Is that a problem?"

"It depends on why he came. If he came to be found, it's not a problem at all—it's what he wants. If he came for some other purpose and finding us here is an unexpected complication, then yes, it's a problem, because it means our next move is his move, and I don't yet know enough to predict it."

I joined him at the window. The stranger had stopped at the well in the center of the square and was drinking from the ladle with the thoroughness of a man who has been a long time without water. He was not looking up at us. He was, as far as one could tell, entirely absorbed in the act of drinking.

"He's not going to run," I said.

"No. Running would tell us too much. He'll stay and manage the situation, whatever that means for him." Crane turned from the window. "What did you make of him when he first came in?"

"Physically capable, controlled, accustomed to operating in environments where he's being observed. The way he moved when he came through the door—he'd already assessed the room before he was fully inside it. That takes practice."

"Yes. And the questions he asked the landlord?"

"Practical. Roads, distances, the schedule of the weekly market. The questions of a man building a picture of a place, not the questions of a man just passing through."

Crane nodded. "So he's here for a reason, he knows we've noticed him, and he's intelligent enough to know that pretending otherwise would tell us more than acknowledging it." He reached for his coat. "I think we should simply ask him. Directly and frankly. In my experience, that approach works best with intelligent people, because they recognize that the alternative—the elaborate dance of misdirection—costs more than it gains."
