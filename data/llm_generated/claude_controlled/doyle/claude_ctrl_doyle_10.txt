I have set down many accounts in my time, and I have learned that the most useful beginning is not the dramatic moment but the moment before it—the moment when everything is still ordinary and the observer, not yet knowing what is about to become significant, records what he sees without the distorting lens of retrospective importance. So I will begin not with the stranger's arrival but with the hour before it, when I was sitting in the inn with a glass of indifferent claret and a letter I had been putting off writing for three days, and the evening was as unremarkable as evenings in Ashcombe generally were.

The inn was moderately full. The regulars occupied their customary positions with the territorial confidence of men who have claimed their ground by right of long use. The fire was adequate. The conversation was the conversation of men who have said everything they have to say to each other many times and have achieved, through repetition, a kind of companionable shorthand. I was an outsider still, after three weeks, which was not unusual in a town like this; outsiders were assessed for a long time before being permitted inside, and the assessment was conducted not through questions but through observation.

I was, in other words, still being watched. I had grown accustomed to this and had stopped minding it, which was itself, I understood, a necessary step in the process.

It was in this state of moderate comfort and mild inattention that I heard the door open and felt, rather than saw, the change in the room's quality of attention. Conversations did not stop, exactly, but they became slightly less animated; heads did not turn, exactly, but they shifted fractionally. The newcomer had been registered, assessed in the first second of his entry, and filed under a category that the room had not yet shared with me.

I turned to look, with the deliberate casualness of a man who has learned to look without appearing to look.
