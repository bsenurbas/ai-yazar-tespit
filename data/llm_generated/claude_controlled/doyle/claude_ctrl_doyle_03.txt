I had been in Thornfield for three weeks before the stranger arrived, and in that time I had formed a reasonably complete picture of its inhabitants and their habits. It was the kind of small town whose social geography is entirely legible once one has taken the trouble to read it: the inn served as the clearing house for all information of consequence, the church provided the framework within which that information was morally assessed, and the post office supplied the raw material for both. I had, as I say, read all of this, and I believed I understood it.

The stranger complicated my understanding.

He arrived late on a Thursday evening, on foot, from the direction of the moor, which was in itself unusual since the moor road was little used and poorly maintained and offered no particular advantage over the valley road to anyone with ordinary business in the town. He was tall, spare, forty or thereabouts, and he carried himself with the economy of movement that distinguishes men who have spent time in circumstances where unnecessary motion is a liability. Military, possibly, or something adjacent to military. His coat was good but not recent, and it had been repaired at the right sleeve with thread that was a close but not exact match—a repair made in the field, by the man himself, rather than by a tailor.

He took a room, ate a meal, spoke to no one beyond the minimum required by his transactions with the innkeeper, and retired early. I observed all of this from my customary corner table, and I noted it with the attention I give to anything that departs from the established pattern of a place I am studying. He was not merely reticent, which would have been unremarkable; he was reticent with purpose, in the manner of a man who has information to protect rather than simply nothing to say. These are distinguishable states to anyone who has had occasion to observe both.

In the morning he was gone before anyone else had risen, and the only evidence of his presence was the neatly made bed—he had made it himself, with military precision—and a single muddy boot print on the threshold of his room, pointing outward, toward the moor.
