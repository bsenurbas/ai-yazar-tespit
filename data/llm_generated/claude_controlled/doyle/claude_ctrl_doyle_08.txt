There were three things about the stranger that struck me as worthy of note, and I record them in the order in which I observed them rather than in the order of their importance, which I was not able to determine until considerably later.

The first was his shoes. They were good shoes, well made and recently resoled, but the resoling had been done in a hurry and by someone less skilled than the original cobbler; the stitching on the right heel was uneven in a way that would have been invisible in ordinary light but was apparent in the low, raking light of the inn's fireplace. A man who has his shoes resoled in a hurry is a man who has had to extend a journey beyond its planned duration.

The second was the newspaper he carried. It was a London paper, two days old, and it had been read with great thoroughness—I could see from across the room that it was heavily annotated in pencil, with sections underlined and marginal notes added in a small, precise hand. A two-day-old paper that has been read with that degree of attention is a paper someone has been carrying with them for a purpose, consulting and reconsidering as they travel.

The third was the way he ate. He ate quickly and without apparent pleasure, in the manner of a man who regards food as fuel rather than as an experience, but he ate everything on his plate and then sat for a moment looking at it with the expression of a man who is calculating whether he needs more. He decided he did not, and this decision seemed to be based on something more precise than appetite. He was, I concluded, managing his resources with the care of someone who does not know exactly when he will next have the opportunity to replenish them.

Taken together, these three observations suggested a man in the middle of an operation whose duration had proved longer than expected. Whether the operation was his own or someone else's was the question I had not yet answered when he looked up and met my eyes across the room.
