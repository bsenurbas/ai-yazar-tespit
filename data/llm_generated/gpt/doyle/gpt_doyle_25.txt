The matter of the blue chalk marks began with a pawnbroker who came to us in a state of considerable agitation. “Someone has marked my door three nights running,” he said. “Blue chalk. A cross, then a circle, then a line.” Holmes listened with his eyes closed. “Not thieves’ signs,” said he. “Too theatrical. Who owes you money?” “Half of London,” said the man. “A large field, but not infinite.”

Holmes asked for the man’s ledger, then for his hat, then for his left boot. The pawnbroker protested, but complied. “You have recently taken in a violin, a military medal, and a woman’s brooch with a cracked clasp,” said Holmes. “Which of these brought a caller with blue-stained fingers?” The pawnbroker hesitated. “The medal, perhaps.” “No. Chalk marks are made by one who handles billiard cues, not school slates.”

That evening we visited a billiard room off the Strand. Holmes stood by the green table and pointed to a young man with a bandaged wrist. “Your marks were not signals to accomplices. They were measures. You were testing the watchman’s line of sight before breaking in.” The fellow laughed weakly. Holmes held up the brooch. “You sought this, not money. A love token is a poor reason for burglary, but an excellent reason for stupidity.”
