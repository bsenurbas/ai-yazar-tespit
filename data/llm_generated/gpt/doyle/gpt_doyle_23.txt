The case of the painted cane was first presented to us by a young actor whose distress seemed genuine, though Holmes later observed that actors are professionally sincere. “My friend has disappeared,” said he, “and left this cane behind.” It was a handsome stick, black lacquered, with a silver top recently painted over in brown.

Holmes rubbed the handle with his thumb. “Your friend was not its owner.” “But he carried it every day.” “Borrowed symbols are common in young men. The cane belonged to an older person, probably a physician. There is carbolic in the grain and a dent made by a hospital stair.” The actor stared. “He said it was his father’s.” “He lied. The question is whether he lied for vanity or survival.”

We traced the cane to a retired surgeon in Kensington, who admitted that it had been stolen with certain letters. Holmes found the actor’s friend in hiding, not guilty but terrified. “The paint was your salvation,” Holmes told him. “Had you left the silver visible, the blackmailer would have found you first.” He turned toward the surgeon’s valet, who had been listening at the door. “And now that he has found you, he may explain why a valet keeps surgical wax in his pocket.”
