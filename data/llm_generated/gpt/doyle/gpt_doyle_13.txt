The case of the yellow glove was brought to us by a cabman whose face had the grey, pinched look of a man who has seen too much before dawn. “Found it in my hansom, Mr. Holmes,” said he, laying a lady’s glove upon the table. “And there was blood on the step.” Holmes lifted the glove by the tip. “Paint, not blood. Oil-based. A woman entered your cab, but a man left it.”

“How can you say that?” I asked. “Because the glove is new, yet stretched across the knuckles. It was worn by a larger hand. The perfume is lady’s violet, applied too heavily by one who guessed rather than knew. The paint is chrome yellow, used by carriage makers, not portrait painters.” He turned to the cabman. “You drove from the Adelphi to Lambeth, stopped once near the bridge, and were paid with a sovereign.” The man gaped. “True, sir.”

In Lambeth, Holmes found a carriage workshop, a locked storeroom, and a missing apprentice. “The glove was a disguise for the hand that signed the false receipt,” said he. “A lady was meant to be blamed for taking the plans, but no lady leaves axle grease under her fingernails.” He faced the foreman, who had grown suddenly still. “You dressed as the woman you meant to accuse. A bold stroke, but your trade followed you into the cab.”
