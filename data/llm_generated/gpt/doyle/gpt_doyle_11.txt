The adventure of the copper thimble reached us upon a morning so dark that the lamps of Baker Street still burned at ten o’clock. Holmes had taken no breakfast and was seated among the papers, his long fingers stained with tobacco ash. “Watson,” said he, without looking up, “a seamstress has been frightened by a gentleman.” “A bold conclusion,” I replied. “A simple one. Her note is written upon trade paper, but the envelope bears a club seal. The matter concerns class, secrecy, and a hand accustomed to silk.”

The young woman who entered wore a plain black dress and carried herself with more courage than ease. “Mr. Holmes, I mended a coat yesterday. In the lining I found this.” She placed a copper thimble upon the table. Holmes examined it with his lens. “Not yours,” said he. “Too large, too smooth at the rim, and marked with resin. It belongs to no seamstress, but to a violinist who has wished to disguise his fingers.” She looked astonished. “The gentleman told me to burn the coat.” “Then he feared the lining more than the stain.”

By evening we had traced the coat to a music-room in Mayfair, where Holmes stood before a cabinet of instruments and gave a dry little laugh. “The missing treaty was folded into the sleeve, not the pocket. Our thief chose a coat because no one searches a tailor’s repair basket.” He turned toward a pale young baronet by the piano. “Your violin betrayed you. Resin dust upon the thimble, ink upon the cuff, and a tear too neat for accident. You may play with feeling, sir, but you steal without method.”
