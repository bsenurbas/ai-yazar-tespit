The case of the silver fishhook began with an elderly fisherman from Richmond, who appeared in Baker Street smelling strongly of river water and tobacco. “They say my brother drowned,” he said. “But he feared deep water and never went alone.” Holmes took the fishhook from his palm. “This hook was not used in fishing,” said he. “It was used in lifting.”

“Lifting what?” I asked. Holmes showed me a trace of red sealing wax caught in the barb. “A document from a narrow space. Perhaps a letterbox. Perhaps a drawer.” The fisherman frowned. “My brother kept letters in a locked cabinet.” “Then someone without a key found another method.” Holmes’s face sharpened. “Who wished to read them before they vanished?”

Near the river cottage, Holmes knelt by the cabinet and inserted the hook through a crack at the back. “So,” said he. “The letters were drawn out one by one. Your brother discovered the theft, followed the thief, and met his death by the bank.” He turned to the neighbour, a smooth man with polished boots unsuited to mud. “You stole secrets with a fishhook. The river merely covered the less original part of your crime.”
