The adventure of the cracked opera glass occurred during that strange season when all London seemed to attend the same scandals with more interest than Parliament. Holmes and I had returned late from dinner when a small parcel awaited us. “No note,” said I. “On the contrary,” Holmes replied. “The absence of a note is itself eloquent.” Inside lay an opera glass with one lens fractured in a spider’s web of fine lines.

“A quarrel at the theatre?” I suggested. “A quarrel, yes. At the theatre, no. Observe the dust in the hinge, Watson. Country dust. The crack was made by heat, not impact. The owner watched something through this glass while standing near a fire.” The next morning brought Lady Marwell, who had lost a diamond clasp during a private concert. “I saw no thief,” she said. “Only a flash in the mirror.” Holmes smiled. “Then we shall trust the mirror before the guests.”

At Marwell House, Holmes placed the broken glass near the drawing-room hearth and nodded. “The thief used this not to watch the singer, but the reflection of the jewel case behind him. The lens cracked when he dropped it upon the hot fender.” He turned to the blind pianist’s attendant, a mild man with white hair. “Your master saw nothing, but you saw too much. The mirror gave you the clasp, and the fire gave me your instrument.”
