The adventure of the red ribbon was one of those trifles which Holmes valued because it opened into darker country. It came by post, tied about a single lock of grey hair. Holmes sniffed it, touched it to his tongue, and gave a little nod. “Seawater, Watson. A woman’s ribbon, an old man’s hair, and a young criminal’s impatience.” “You draw much from little.” “I draw nothing. I observe what is offered.”

Our client was a solicitor from Wapping, much disturbed by the disappearance of an elderly shipowner. “His daughter received that ribbon this morning,” said he. “No demand for money?” asked Holmes. “None.” “Then the message was not ransom. It was proof of command.” The solicitor frowned. “Command over whom?” “Over the daughter, I suspect. Let us ask what she has refused to sign.”

At the docks, Holmes found the answer in a warehouse smelling of tar and damp rope. “The old gentleman is alive,” said he, pointing to fresh boot marks near a crate. “He was brought here to frighten the daughter into surrendering the shares.” He turned to a young shipping clerk whose sleeve bore a trace of red silk. “You cut the ribbon yourself, but not the hair. That was taken from his brush, not his head. A clever economy, but too domestic for a dockyard kidnapping.”
