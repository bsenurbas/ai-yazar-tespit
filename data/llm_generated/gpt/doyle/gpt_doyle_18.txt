The adventure of the empty decanter occurred on a night of heavy fog, when Lestrade came to us with his collar turned up and his temper much disturbed. “A man is dead in his dining-room,” said he. “No wound, no poison in the glass, no sign of entry.” Holmes looked up from his pipe. “And yet you bring me the decanter.” “It is empty.” “An empty vessel may be more talkative than a full one.”

He held the decanter to the light. “Port wine, but rinsed with cold water. That was foolish. Sugar clings where wine does not.” Lestrade blinked. “Sugar?” “A sweetened draught was used to hide a bitter powder, but the glass was exchanged. The murderer cleaned the decanter, not the stopper.” He drew the cork and showed a dark stain at the inner rim. “There, Watson, is the last inch of the case.”

At the house, Holmes examined the sideboard and questioned the servants. “The victim drank from his usual glass,” said he. “The poison was not in the glass, but upon the stopper. Each time he poured, it dissolved a little more.” He faced the dead man’s brother, who had protested too much and too soon. “You washed the decanter and forgot the cork. It is a common failing of criminals. They clean what they see and betray themselves by what they handle.”
