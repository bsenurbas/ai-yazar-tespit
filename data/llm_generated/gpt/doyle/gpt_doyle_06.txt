The adventure of the silver matchbox began upon a bleak March night, when the rain beat upon the windows of Baker Street with such persistence that even Holmes abandoned his papers and turned toward the fire. I had scarcely settled into my chair when there came a hurried knock below, followed by the heavy tread of a man who had no patience for ceremony. “A military fellow,” said Holmes. “Retired, anxious, and accustomed to command.” The visitor entered at once, a red-faced gentleman with a clipped moustache and a silver matchbox clenched in his hand. “Mr. Holmes, my son has been accused of theft from the War Office.” Holmes glanced at the matchbox. “Then your son is innocent, Colonel. The thief smokes cigars, writes with violet ink, and has a slight injury to the right hand.”

The colonel stared as though Holmes had performed some conjuring trick. “You know this from that box?” “From what is not upon it,” said Holmes. “Your son is a cigarette smoker, yet the sulphur marks are wide and uneven, such as a cigar man leaves. The silver has been polished recently, but violet ink remains in the hinge, which means the owner opened it while handling a fresh letter. As for the hand, the striker is rubbed on the wrong side; a right-handed man with two sound fingers would not use it so.” He leaned back and fixed our visitor with that cold, bright gaze of his. “The stolen paper was not taken to sell. It was taken to delay. Someone wished a certain dispatch to miss its hour.”

By midnight we stood in a narrow office off Whitehall, where Holmes examined a blotter, a coal scuttle, and the rim of a waste-paper basket with equal interest. “Here is our little drama,” said he. “The paper was removed, copied, and returned, but returned too late. The accusation fell upon the young man because he alone had the key. Yet the key was never used.” He lifted the matchbox and tapped it against the desk. A tiny brass shaving fell out. “The lock was opened by a duplicate made in haste, and the shaving lodged where only the maker’s hand could place it.” He turned toward the clerk who stood trembling by the door. “You may tell the colonel’s son that his name is clear. As for you, Mr. Prentiss, I advise plain speech. The matchbox has spoken already.”
