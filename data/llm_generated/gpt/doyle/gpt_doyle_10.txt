The adventure of the green ledger began upon a bright but bitter morning in late January, when the frost lay white upon the railings of Baker Street and the breath of the horses rose like steam in the roadway. Holmes had been seated with his knees drawn up, his grey eyes fixed upon a small account book which had arrived by post. “Watson,” said he, “commerce is often dull, but bookkeeping has its moments of poetry.” “You find poetry in figures?” I asked. “In their irregularities, my dear fellow.” At that instant our visitor was admitted: a portly merchant with anxious eyes and a gold chain stretched across his waistcoat. “Mr. Holmes, my cashier has disappeared, and with him a thousand pounds.” Holmes tapped the ledger. “No, Mr. Grimsby. Your cashier has disappeared without the money, which is a far more serious matter.”

The merchant stared. “But the accounts are short!” “The accounts are too short,” said Holmes. “A thief conceals the amount he steals; an honest man in danger leaves a signal. See here: every third entry is underlined by a pressure mark, not by ink. The page was written twice over, first in pencil, then in haste. Your cashier wished us to read the pattern.” He turned the book toward me. “Three, six, nine, twelve. The missing figures form an address, Watson, not a sum.” “An address?” cried Grimsby. “Then he has been taken?” “Persuaded first, held later,” replied Holmes. “The ink blot upon the margin is from a railway pen, and the smell of cheap cigar smoke is not your office. Your man wrote his warning at a station.”

By afternoon we were in a narrow room above a tobacconist near Waterloo, where Holmes moved from the window to the table with that quiet economy of motion which always told me the chase was ending. “The cashier sat here,” said he, pointing to a chair. “He was not bound, but watched. He altered the ledger before returning it to his employer, knowing that only an accountant or a detective would notice.” A thin man by the door made a sudden movement, but Holmes had already laid a hand upon his sleeve. “No haste, Mr. Venn. You staged a theft to ruin the cashier before he could expose your false invoices. Unfortunately for you, figures are faithful witnesses. They do not forget, and they do not lie unless a man teaches them.”
