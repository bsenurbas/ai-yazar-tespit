The affair of the silent parrot began in the dull warmth of a June afternoon, when London seemed to steam beneath its own smoke. Holmes had been conducting an experiment with cigar ash when Mrs. Hudson announced a widow from Chelsea. “A widow, Watson, and one lately troubled by inheritance,” he murmured. “Her card is black-edged, but the handwriting is firm. Grief has passed. Suspicion remains.”

“My husband’s parrot has not spoken since the night he died,” said Mrs. Larkin, a sharp-eyed lady with a trembling mouth. “Before that, the creature repeated everything.” Holmes leaned back. “And what did it say last?” “Only this: ‘Not the blue bottle.’” Holmes turned his head sharply. “Ah. A bird may be a poor witness, but a fine clock. Who fed it afterward?” “My nephew.” “Then your nephew is either kind, guilty, or both.”

At the house, Holmes studied the cage, the medicine tray, and a shallow scratch upon the window latch. “The bird was not silent from grief,” said he. “It was drugged. The blue bottle contained harmless tonic, and the clear one beside it carried the poison. Your husband warned the room, but not the right person.” The nephew protested, but Holmes lifted the cage cloth and showed a dusting of white powder upon the seed cup. “You silenced the only witness. Unfortunately, you did not silence the seed.”
