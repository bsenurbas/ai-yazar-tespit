The epidemic of honesty began among railway clerks. A man asked for the time would answer with the time, then confess the sum he had stolen from petty cash in 1898. Soon shopkeepers, judges, widows, and Cabinet ministers were speaking with the same destructive accuracy.

Physicians searched for poison. Priests claimed judgement. Dr. Mallory found the cause in a new anaesthetic vapour used widely in dentistry, which left the inhibitory centres of the brain weakened for weeks. Affected persons did not lose reason, memory, or courtesy. They merely lost the power to select.

Civilisation proved less prepared for truth than for war. Businesses failed, marriages altered, courts overflowed, and politics entered a condition too pure to survive. Mallory’s antidote was received with relief. Yet for a short season, mankind saw itself without the merciful blur of custom, and few who lived through it spoke of the old lies with quite the same confidence.
