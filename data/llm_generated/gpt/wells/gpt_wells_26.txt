The door appeared during a storm in Bloomsbury, standing upright in the middle of a wet pavement between a lamppost and a cab rank. It was made of dark wood, with a brass handle and no wall about it. Rain struck its panels and ran down them in ordinary drops. People walked round it at first, as Londoners walk round any obstruction whose ownership is unclear.

A constable tried the handle and found it locked. A locksmith opened it at noon before a crowd of perhaps two hundred persons. Beyond lay not the opposite side of the street, but a small grey room with no windows, lit by a steady light from no visible lamp. On a table in the room stood a glass vessel containing a human hand. The hand moved when the door opened, slowly, as though stirring from sleep.

The authorities closed the door and posted guards, which increased public interest. Experts examined the hinges and found that the wood was oak, but from no known species. The brass contained an isotope not then named. More strikingly, the thickness of the door changed according to the weather, being three inches on clear days and nearly a foot in rain. It seemed not to occupy space, but to negotiate with it.

On the fourth rainy night, the door opened from within. A man stepped out, clothed in a material like dull paper and speaking English with archaic vowels. He asked for the date and received it without visible surprise. Then he asked whether the hand had survived. On being told that it had, he wept with relief. It was, he explained, the first portion of mankind ever sent successfully through a direction not yet included in geometry.
