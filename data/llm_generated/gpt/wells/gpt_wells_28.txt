The Bureau was founded after the railway accident at Crewe, in which thirty-seven passengers arrived in London two hours before the train had departed. At first the matter was dismissed as a clerical confusion, but the passengers carried morning newspapers not yet printed and displayed appetites appropriate to an evening meal. One elderly woman had completed a letter during the missing interval and could not remember writing it.

Further cases followed. A solicitor lost twenty minutes between his front door and the street. A child gained an entire afternoon while crossing a nursery. In Manchester, a factory whistle blew on Thursday and was heard on Tuesday. Time, which had been treated by engineers as the most obedient servant of timetables, had begun to show defects in distribution.

The Bureau occupied two rooms behind the Board of Trade and employed mathematicians, detectives, and one retired conjurer. Citizens were invited to report lost, duplicated, or premature hours. Most reports were nonsense, but a few were measurable. In certain streets, duration leaked; in certain houses, it accumulated. Watches could be corrected, but no one knew what was being corrected.

The scandal came when the Bureau discovered that lost hours gathered in a disused reservoir beneath north London. There, in the dark, clocks ran without winding, flowers bloomed in sealed boxes, and insects lived generations between inspections. At the centre lay a smooth black sphere, not larger than a cannonball, around which time circulated like water around a drain. It was not an object from the future. It was a hole in the present, and the city had been pouring its wasted moments into it for years.
