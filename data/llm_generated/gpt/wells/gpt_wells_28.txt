The first message from the future was received on a broken wireless set in Bristol. It consisted of market prices for wheat, a list of three deaths, and a warning not to repair the northern bridge. No one believed it until the bridge fell two days later.

Thereafter messages arrived irregularly, always practical and always incomplete. They advised against voyages, predicted storms, named inventions before their inventors had conceived them. Governments seized apparatus, but the future was careless in its choice of receivers. It spoke through toys, military stations, and once through the fillings in a dentist’s patient.

The terror lay not in prophecy, but in its poverty. No message explained mankind’s fate. No voice spoke of justice, wisdom, or the purpose of history. The future, when it reached back to us, was as harried and provincial as the present, concerned with accidents, profits, repairs, and the avoidance of immediate pain.
