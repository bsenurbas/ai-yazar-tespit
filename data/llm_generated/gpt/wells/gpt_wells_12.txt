The new metal was found in a fallen star no larger than a gentleman’s watch. It was grey, soft under the knife, and yet no furnace in the laboratory could melt it. When struck, it gave no sound, but all the clocks in the room stopped for three seconds.

Dr. Pern, who purchased the fragment from a Dorset farmer, found that it had one property unknown to terrestrial substances. It did not resist force; it delayed it. A hammer blow delivered at noon made its dent at half past four, and a weight placed upon it in the evening was not felt by the scale until dawn.

The uses of such a substance were discussed with vulgar enthusiasm. Engineers imagined bridges that would postpone their collapse, soldiers imagined armour that would receive a bullet tomorrow, and financiers, with their usual genius for moral ugliness, imagined contracts in which consequence itself could be deferred. Pern alone grasped the larger horror: matter had revealed a loophole in cause and effect.
