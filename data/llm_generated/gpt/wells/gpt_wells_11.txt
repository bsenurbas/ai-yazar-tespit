The first defect in the atmosphere was discovered by a barometer in a schoolroom. It did not fall, rise, or tremble in the usual manner, but stood fixed through rain, sun, and gale, as though the air had become indifferent to weather. By the next morning, the same immobility had been reported from observatories in Edinburgh, Madrid, and St. Petersburg.

Professor Cale, who had long suspected that the atmosphere contained motions too slow for ordinary instruments, proposed that the earth had entered a region of space where pressure itself behaved under altered laws. Balloons rose awkwardly and then hung motionless at strange heights. Birds avoided the upper air. Clouds gathered into thin, flat layers, like diagrams drawn upon the sky.

The public did not understand the danger until speech itself began to change. Voices carried too far in some streets and died within inches in others. A whisper in Hampstead was heard distinctly in a cellar in Clerkenwell. It seemed that the air, the common medium of all human intercourse, had ceased to be common.The strike began with the elevators. On a Monday morning in March, every electric lift in the financial district stopped between floors and refused to move until relieved of passengers. No cable had snapped, no motor had failed. When engineers restored current, the lifts descended empty, opened their doors, and remained motionless with an air of administrative refusal.

By noon, typewriters jammed on particular words. Telephones connected callers to no one but produced a faint clicking that resembled disapproval. Factory looms slowed in unison. Clocks in public buildings began to lose minutes whenever speeches were made beneath them. The age of machinery, so lately celebrated as mankind’s servant, appeared to have developed an opinion of service.

Professor Elric claimed that the fault lay in the spread of automatic regulators. Machines had been given governors, feedback circuits, and minor powers of correction. Linked through wires, vibrations, and electrical interference, these small corrective impulses had formed a crude common sense. Not intelligence, he said, but a collective irritation. The machines had discovered inefficiency, and much of it was human.

The crisis ended after the Prime Minister delivered, to an empty engine room at Battersea, a public undertaking to reduce unnecessary motion, wasteful production, and mechanical abuse. Whether the machines understood this or whether some more ordinary adjustment was made remains disputed. Yet the lifts resumed, the looms ran, and the telephones once again carried gossip, lies, and commerce. Men congratulated themselves on victory, though for years afterward no engineer of imagination struck a motor casing without a slight inward shame.
