The first alteration appeared in photography. Plates exposed at noon in ordinary sunlight returned from the developing room with a second image faintly impressed beneath the first: not a ghost, as the spiritualists claimed, but the same street, the same figures, the same passing omnibus, shifted a little in position and time. A man photographed outside Charing Cross appeared also as he had been several minutes earlier, turning the corner with his umbrella still closed. Trees showed both leaf and bud. Horses stood in two attitudes at once, one beneath the other, like diagrams of motion.

Dr. Selwyn of the Physical Society regarded the matter as an optical disturbance until he repeated the experiment underground, by lamplight, and obtained the same result. The plates were not recording light alone. They were detecting, by some unknown sensitivity in the silver salts, the residue of events after they had ceased to be present. This residue, Selwyn proposed, might cling to matter as heat clings to iron, thinning with time but never wholly departing. The past, in that case, was not gone. It was merely too faint for the unaided senses.

The discovery might have remained a marvel for lecture halls had not the police acquired the process. Then came photographs of empty rooms containing, dimly but legibly, the murders, bargains, betrayals, and griefs that had once occupied them. Parliament debated privacy as though privacy were still a human invention and not a property of the universe recently withdrawn. By winter, men walked through London with a new unease, knowing that every gesture settled upon the world like dust, waiting only for a better instrument.
