The first iron tree was found beside a railway embankment in Yorkshire. It had bark like oxidised steel, leaves thin as razors, and roots that had passed clean through granite. Botanists denied that it was a tree, metallurgists denied that it was metal, and both parties disliked the miners who pointed out that it had grown fifty feet in a month.

Soon others appeared along telegraph lines and near power stations. They grew best where the ground was rich in iron, copper, and human industry. Their roots drank from rails, pipes, boilers, and buried cables. A neglected factory might be consumed in a week, its walls pierced by metallic saplings and its machinery reduced to a glittering mulch around the trunks.

Professor Sedgewick believed the seeds had arrived in meteoric dust. They were not seeds in the botanical sense, but small mechanisms capable of reorganising local materials into living structure. The forest did not photosynthesise. It fed on tension, magnetism, and the residues of manufacture. It was, in effect, life adapted not to nature but to civilisation.

The first proposal was to burn it, and failed. The second was to cut it down, and failed more dangerously. Every severed branch rooted where it fell. By winter, the Iron Forest had crossed roads, surrounded villages, and drawn railway lines into itself like threads into cloth. Men had long imagined that machines would conquer the wilderness. They had not foreseen a wilderness that understood machines and preferred them as food.
