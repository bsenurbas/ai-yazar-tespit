The new star appeared first above the southern horizon, low and reddish, and so faint that only the habitually watchful saw it. It did not twinkle, nor did it keep the fixed manner of the planets. Night by night it rose higher, and night by night its colour deepened, until even the newspaper readers, who are among the last to notice the heavens except in war or weather, began to speak of it over breakfast.

Sir Nathaniel Broome, who had spent forty years in the observation of small bodies beyond Mars, rejected the popular theory of a comet. It had no tail, no visible atmosphere, no softness of outline. Its light, examined through the spectroscope, showed bands unlike any known mineral or gas. More troubling still, it seemed to alter its path by minute degrees, not as a stone obeys gravity, but as a vessel under correction.

The public took comfort in numbers. Astronomers gave distances in millions of miles, and these sounded large enough to be moral reassurance. Yet Broome knew that the significance of a body is not only in its distance, but in its intention. The thing did not merely fall through space. It selected.

By late autumn it was visible before sunset, a red point above the smoke of London. Horses shied from it; dogs howled at its rising; and men who had never troubled themselves with the sky began to feel watched from above. On the final evening before the first signal came, Broome wrote in his diary that humanity had mistaken isolation for safety. The universe, he added, had always been populated; only our instruments had been provincial.
