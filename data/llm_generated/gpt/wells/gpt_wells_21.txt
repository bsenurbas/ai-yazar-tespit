The first artificial moon was launched by accident, or so the Ministry claimed. It was a shell of aluminium and polished glass, fired from a coastal gun during experiments in high-altitude artillery. Instead of falling back to earth, it remained above Europe, a bright bead moving night after night among the stars.

Its success produced a fever of imitation. Nations fired their own shining instruments into the sky, each equipped with lenses, mirrors, or wireless apparatus. Within three years the heavens, once the province of poets and sailors, had become crowded with public works.

The consequences were not wholly military. Farmers received weather signals. Ships crossed oceans with new confidence. Lovers complained that the night was no longer dark enough for sincerity. Yet a few astronomers watched in silence, knowing that mankind had not conquered space; it had merely littered the threshold.
