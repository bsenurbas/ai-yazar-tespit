The trouble began in the British Museum, where a student copying an Assyrian tablet found that the inscription had altered during lunch. The cuneiform characters had not moved in any visible manner, yet the lines now conveyed, according to three shaken scholars, a complaint about misquotation. Within days, Latin inscriptions in churches corrected dates, Greek papyri expanded missing passages, and Egyptian hieroglyphs added remarks in the margins with a confidence unbecoming to antiquities.

At first the scholars rejoiced. Dead languages had become self-explanatory. Broken texts healed. Obscure verbs clarified themselves. Lost names returned. History seemed to have acquired a tongue. But the joy lasted only until the inscriptions began to disagree with accepted chronicles, royal monuments, national myths, and the footnotes of eminent men.

Professor Maverson proposed that language, once fixed in durable matter and repeated by generations of minds, had developed a faint persistence resembling life. The new electrical lighting in museums, together with photographic reproduction, had stimulated these dormant linguistic structures. Words were not symbols only. They were organisms of memory, and some had grown tired of being misread.

The political consequences were severe. Empires discovered that treaties meant other than they had claimed. Families lost heroic ancestors to revised epitaphs. A celebrated conqueror’s monument confessed, in excellent stone, that the battle beneath it had been a retreat. The public demanded that the inscriptions be silenced, but no one knew how to kill a language already dead. In the end, the museums closed certain rooms, and history resumed its older method: selective hearing.
