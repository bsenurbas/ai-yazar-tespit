The first man to hear the subterranean bells was a deaf pensioner in Kent. He felt them through the soles of his boots while cutting cabbages. The sound, if sound it could be called, rose at exact intervals from somewhere beneath the chalk, too low for ordinary ears and yet strong enough to set window glass faintly shivering.

Geologists brought instruments, then soldiers, then officials who disliked both instruments and publicity. The pulses came from a depth at which no cavern should exist. They were regular, deliberate, and increasing. Some said they resembled machinery. Others, more cautious, called them stresses in the earth.

On the seventeenth day, the ground opened in a pasture and exhaled warm air smelling of iron and salt. Nothing emerged. There was only the shaft, smooth-sided and descending beyond the reach of lamps. The bells continued from below, not as a warning, but as the steady work of some intelligence that had begun its ascent long before mankind learned to listen.
