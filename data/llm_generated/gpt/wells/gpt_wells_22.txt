The first transparent man was a clerk in the Patent Office. He came to work as usual, removed his hat, and sat down at his desk before anyone noticed that the gas flame behind him could be seen, blurred but unmistakable, through his chest. His organs showed only faintly, not with the vulgar horror of anatomical plates, but as shadows in tinted glass. He was sent home in a cab after considerable argument about whether such a condition fell under illness, indecency, or fraud.

Within a week, three similar cases appeared in London, two in Paris, and one in Boston. All were men of sedentary habits. All had worked for years beneath the new violet lamps then fashionable in public offices. Dr. Frobisher proposed that prolonged exposure to their rays had altered the refractive character of living tissue, reducing the body, in optical terms, to something near air and water.

The practical consequences were absurd before they became dreadful. Newspapers made jokes; tailors advertised lined clothing for “the discreet invisible gentleman”; the police watched the affected persons with suspicion, though most of them were too frightened to leave their homes. The military, less amused, inquired whether the condition might be induced in scouts.

But the change did not stop at the skin. The men reported first a thinning of appetite, then of memory, then of emotional force. Their bodies had not become invisible; they had become less involved in matter. One by one they faded from touch as well as sight, until chairs failed to bear them and wives could no longer feel their hands. The last record of the Patent Office clerk was a voice heard at the edge of his bedroom, asking, very politely, whether the window was open.
