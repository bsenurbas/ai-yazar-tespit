At first it was taken for a mirage over the Egyptian desert, a wavering outline of towers where no town existed. The caravan men saw it at sunrise and again at sunset: walls, domes, terraces, and long inclined roads climbing through the air. It vanished when approached, yet left shadows upon the sand, which mirages do not commonly do. A British surveyor, irritated by the reports, made drawings from three stations and proved that the object possessed position, scale, and a regrettable independence of opinion.

An expedition reached the site in February. There was no masonry, no ruin, no buried foundation. But at dawn the city returned, first as a tremor in the air, then as a translucent architecture rising from the desert with extraordinary speed. Men could see the sun through its walls, but not the stars. Sound changed near it; voices became thin and metallic, and a rifle shot took nearly half a minute to die.

Professor Amberley suggested that the city did not exist in our present interval. It was an overlap from some remote epoch, projected through a fault in time as a lantern image is thrown upon a sheet. Whether it belonged to the past or future he would not say. The streets were empty, yet dust moved in them. Doors opened upon unseen inhabitants, and once, from a balcony higher than St. Paul’s, something like a banner stirred in a wind that did not touch the expedition.

On the sixth morning, Captain Vane entered the nearest gate. His figure dimmed at once, but did not disappear. He walked up a broad road between transparent houses and turned once, raising his hand with a gesture half warning and half farewell. At sunset the city faded, taking him with it. The following dawn it returned. On the balcony above the gate stood Vane, older by many years, looking down upon his companions with the grave embarrassment of a man who has learned that rescue is a word tied too closely to time.
