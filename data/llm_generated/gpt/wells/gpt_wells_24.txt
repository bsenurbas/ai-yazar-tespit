The first disappearance occurred during a lecture on optics. Professor Marr raised his hand to sketch a diagram, and the hand vanished from the wrist. There was no blood, pain, or wound. He flexed invisible fingers and, to the astonishment of the room, picked up a piece of chalk.

Soon the condition appeared elsewhere. Portions of bodies, furniture, and streetlamps lost visibility while retaining weight and force. A cart horse in Leeds became visible only from the shoulders forward, producing a public panic of unreasonable size. The newspapers spoke of rays.

Marr suggested that visibility was not an inherent quality of matter, but a relation between matter and the surrounding field of light. That relation had been disturbed. More troubling still, the invisible portions did not cast shadows, yet they occupied space; and so, for the first time, men found themselves bruised by what their eyes had no right to deny.
