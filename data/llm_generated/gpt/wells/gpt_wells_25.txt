The plague began without fever. Its victims remained at work, ate well, and slept normally. Only their decisions altered. A grocer in Putney gave away his entire stock to strangers. A judge interrupted a trial to apologise to the prisoner. A banker, previously considered sound, began publishing the details of his firm’s concealed losses in the morning papers. Physicians called it hysteria until the same conduct appeared in clerks, soldiers, factory girls, and members of Parliament.

Dr. Mellor found the cause in the blood: a minute organism not unlike a bacterium, though far smaller and marked by a crystalline core. It did not attack the organs. It lodged in the brain and seemed to weaken those habits of caution, ambition, and self-protection upon which modern society rests. The infected person did not become irrational. He became, by ordinary standards, ruinously humane.

Governments reacted more quickly than hospitals. Troops surrounded districts where the organism spread, but the soldiers, once exposed, put down their rifles and shared their rations with the crowd. A Cabinet committee recommended silence, then published its own suppressed minutes under the influence of the infection. The newspapers, infected in their turn, ceased exaggerating and became almost unreadable.

Mellor believed the organism to be terrestrial, perhaps long dormant in polar ice and released by the warming of the northern seas. Others suspected a more deliberate origin. The germ had the curious property of sparing children and the very old, as though it selected those most responsible for the world’s machinery. For three months civilisation came near to moral collapse. Then the organism faded, leaving behind bankruptcies, reconciliations, resignations, and a general uneasiness in those who had recovered. They had been made better men for a season, and they could not forgive the experience.
