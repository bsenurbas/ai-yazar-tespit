The first complaint came from the Royal Geographical Society, where a wall map of Africa was found to have altered overnight. A river had moved forty miles east. A mountain range, not previously recorded, now crossed a blank region with cartographic confidence. The curator blamed damp until explorers’ reports confirmed that the new mountains existed and, more alarmingly, bore signs of great age.

Other maps followed. Coastlines shifted on Admiralty charts before the tides obeyed them. Railway maps added stations whose buildings appeared weeks later in empty fields. A school atlas gave an island in the North Sea, and by morning fishermen saw its cliffs from Hull. It became difficult to say whether the maps were recording change or causing it.

Professor Delane argued that cartography had become an active force. For centuries men had drawn the earth, named it, measured it, and repeated those forms in millions of copies. Perhaps matter, subject at large scales to habit and expectation, had begun to conform to its descriptions. A line printed often enough might become a valley; a border believed by enough schoolchildren might harden into mountains.

This theory made governments attentive. Ministries guarded maps as they once guarded arsenals. Smugglers commissioned private coastlines. Speculators printed railways through land they had purchased cheaply. The world entered a period of geographical instability, not through earthquake or flood, but through ink. Men learned too late that to describe the earth is not always harmless. The planet, it seemed, was suggestible.
