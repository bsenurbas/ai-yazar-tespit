The explorers found the engine beneath six hundred feet of Greenland ice, although no human craft could have reached that place in any known age. It was embedded not in a cave, but in ice older than history, and around it the layers curved as though they had flowed about a warm object and then closed over it. The metal was white, uncorroded, and gave off a steady warmth that no thermometer could properly measure.

Lieutenant Harben believed it to be a meteorite until the first panel opened. Inside were wheels, tubes, and dark prisms of crystal, all moving without steam, combustion, or visible power. The parts did not grind or vibrate. They turned with that smooth indifference which is more disturbing than violence, as though they had been doing so for ten thousand years and found the arrival of men beneath their notice.

The scientific party debated whether it had been made on earth by some lost race, or had descended from elsewhere before mankind possessed language. Professor Lynd maintained that both possibilities were provincial. The machine might not have been made for travel at all. It might have been placed there to perform a task so slow that civilisations could rise and vanish during a single cycle of its operation.

When they attempted to remove a small crystal from its housing, the aurora appeared overhead at noon. Its green curtains streamed southward across Europe and were seen as far as Rome. At the same hour, compasses failed, whales fled the northern seas, and every telegraph line from Edinburgh to Moscow carried a low continuous tone. The engine had not been sleeping under the ice. It had been maintaining some delicate balance, and mankind, with its usual confidence, had put a hand upon the lever.
