The first sign was a pattern of lights beneath the skin of a sleeping child in Leeds. They moved across her arm in lines and clusters, too regular for fever and too bright for ordinary inflammation. The physician called it phosphorescence. Her mother called it possession. Neither was correct.

Within days, similar lights appeared in hundreds of persons, most visibly at night. They travelled under the skin without pain, gathered near the spine, then dispersed toward the hands and face. Blood tests showed nothing unusual except a faint increase in electrical conductivity. The afflicted remained healthy, though many reported hearing, just before waking, a sound like distant conversation through water.

Dr. Semple discovered, by magnifying a drop of spinal fluid, innumerable motes moving with purposeful speed. They were smaller than cells and brighter than bacteria, each surrounded by a minute field of light. He did not call them organisms. He called them visitors, for their behaviour suggested exploration rather than infection. They mapped nerves, tested muscles, paused at scars, and showed particular interest in the optic nerve.

The panic became unmanageable when the first shared perception occurred. A woman in Leeds described, in exact detail, a room in Bristol where another affected person was reading beside a lamp. The visitors had not come to kill us. They had come to connect us, using the nervous system of mankind as a temporary instrument. For one winter, thoughts crossed counties without speech, and secrets became porous. Then the lights faded. We returned to privacy with relief, though some mourned the brief, terrible intimacy of the human species.
