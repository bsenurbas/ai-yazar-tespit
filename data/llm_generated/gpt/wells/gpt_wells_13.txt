London fell asleep at twenty-three minutes past two on a Thursday afternoon. Cab drivers slept on their boxes, bankers over ledgers, judges beneath wigs, thieves in the act of escape, and one unfortunate violinist in the middle of a solo at Queen’s Hall. The city did not collapse because the horses slept too, and most of the machines, lacking supervision, merely continued until fuel or momentum failed.

Outside the circle, which extended roughly from Hampstead to Greenwich, life continued in alarm. Troops entered with masks, stimulants, prayers, and drums. Nothing woke the sleepers. Their breathing was regular, their pulses calm, and their expressions, in nearly all cases, mildly attentive. It was not unconsciousness. It was as though the city listened inwardly to a lecture of great importance.

Dr. Carfax located the centre of the effect in a basement near Holborn, where an inventor named Pettle had been experimenting with directed etheric vibration. Pettle himself was found beside his apparatus, asleep but smiling. His notes suggested that he had sought a cure for insomnia by broadcasting a rhythm common to the healthy brain. He had not considered that ten million brains might accept the invitation at once.

The sleep lasted nine hours. When London woke, there was confusion, then relief, then a more private disturbance. Many people remembered the same dream: a wide plain under a green sky, where London stood rebuilt in glass and silence after the extinction of its inhabitants. Pettle maintained, upon recovery, that he had not put the dream into their minds. He had merely tuned the city to a frequency on which the future had already been speaking.
