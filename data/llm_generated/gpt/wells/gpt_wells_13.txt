The village of Harrowden lost its shadows on a Tuesday. The sun shone, the chimneys smoked, the geese crossed the green, but nothing in the place cast darkness upon the ground. At first the villagers spoke of mist, though the air was clear enough to see the teeth of the distant hills.

A photographer from Northampton proved the matter beyond gossip. His plates showed houses, trees, cattle, and men with a hard, unnatural brightness, as though the world there had been painted without depth. Yet at sunset, when all England darkened, Harrowden remained visible under a pale and sourceless light.

The explanation came too late to comfort anyone. Beneath the village lay a vein of mineral newly exposed by quarrying, a substance that reflected not light but the absence of light. It had not removed the shadows. It had stored them. And in the third week, when the vein cracked after frost, the stolen darkness rose at noon and covered Harrowden like a second night.
