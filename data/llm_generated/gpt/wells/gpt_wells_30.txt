The last experiment of Dr. Sarn concerned memory in metals. He believed that repeated use left a pattern in tools, weapons, coins, and keys. A knife remembered cutting; a bell remembered ringing; a sovereign remembered hands. His colleagues found the theory picturesque and therefore unsafe.

Sarn built an apparatus to excite these residues and translate them into sound. At first it produced only scratches, sighs, and dull impacts. Then a Roman nail spoke with hammer blows from a vanished fort. A wedding ring yielded a woman’s sob. A prison key gave, again and again, the same brief prayer.

The discovery did not make the past clearer. It made it crowded. Every object became a witness, every room an archive, every coin in a pocket a confusion of touch and loss. Men had thought history lay in books and monuments; Sarn showed that it slept in matter itself, waiting for a sufficient disturbance.
