Mr. Ashdown was struck by lightning while testing a wireless mast outside Guildford. He did not die. Indeed, after three days of unconsciousness, he rose, ate breakfast, and complained of the slowness of speech in those around him. His doctors found no paralysis, no burns beyond a branching mark across the chest, and no injury except one impossible peculiarity: his reactions preceded their stimulus.

If a pistol was fired behind him, he flinched before the hammer fell. If a question was asked, he answered before the final word. At first the interval was only a fraction of a second. Then it lengthened. He became able to catch a glass before it was dropped, step aside before a horse bolted, and finish sentences that the speaker had not decided to utter.

The newspapers treated him as a marvel, and the military as an asset. Ashdown regarded his condition with increasing disgust. He did not see the future as a vision. He received it as an irritation, a pressure in the nerves. The world came toward him late, like a badly kept appointment. Music lost melody, conversation lost surprise, and affection became painful because every gesture of kindness arrived already spoiled by anticipation.

In the end he withdrew to a cottage on the coast, where the sea, being repetitive, troubled him least. His final letter to Dr. Penrose stated that the human mind is mercifully timed to the world and that to be even a little ahead of events is not power but exile. He vanished during a thunderstorm. Some say he drowned. Penrose, who knew better than to trust ordinary sequence, believed Ashdown had simply passed beyond the visible edge of consequence.
