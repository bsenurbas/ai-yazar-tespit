The discovery was made not by astronomers, but by gardeners. In the summer of 1907, certain vines in Surrey began to grow against the sun. Their tendrils did not climb toward warmth or light, but stretched steadily westward at the rate of nearly an inch an hour, crossing lawns, walls, and even roads with a persistence that seemed almost intentional. Cuttings placed in darkness continued the same movement. Specimens sent to Kew behaved likewise, and within a fortnight the phenomenon had been observed in France, Spain, and the coast of Africa.

Professor Wrentham, whose reputation had suffered from his earlier theories on vegetable sensation, declared that the plants were responding to a signal. This suggestion was received with amusement until the direction of growth was plotted upon a map. Every line, extended far enough, converged upon a point in the Atlantic where the ocean was then reported to be unusually warm and strangely still. Ships passing through that region described a faint humming in their compasses and a greenish glow beneath the water at night. One captain wrote that the sea there looked “as if it were thinking.”

When at last the expedition reached the site, it found no island, no wreck, no volcanic vent. It found a circular forest growing upward from the floor of the ocean, its trunks pale as bone and its leaves spread flat beneath the surface like great listening instruments. The thing was not plant life in any familiar sense. It was an apparatus, rooted in the earth and addressed to the stars. And all over the continents, vine by vine and field by field, the vegetation of our planet had begun to answer it.
