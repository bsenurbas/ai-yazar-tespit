The first sign was not a flash, nor a report, nor any theatrical disturbance of the air, but a faint alteration in the conduct of ordinary things. The compass in Professor Halden’s laboratory no longer pointed north. Dust gathered in little rings upon the floor. A spoon, dropped from the breakfast table, fell with a perceptible hesitation, as though the earth had momentarily reconsidered its ancient habit of attraction. These facts, trivial in themselves, were entered in the Professor’s notebook with the dry precision of a man who knows that catastrophe most often begins as an inconvenience.

By noon, the phenomenon had spread across London. Clocks ran unevenly; gas flames leaned toward the east; pigeons, robbed of some instinctive geometry, circled chimney pots in widening spirals until they came down exhausted in the streets. The newspapers spoke first of magnetism, then of electricity, then of foreign experiments. But Halden, with a slide rule, three cracked lenses, and the remains of a meteorite purchased years before from a dealer in Holborn, had already formed a colder hypothesis. Some external body, unseen and perhaps artificial, was passing near the earth, disturbing not matter itself but the relations by which matter kept its place.

That evening, as a copper-green dusk settled over the city, he climbed to the observatory roof and saw it: not a star, not a comet, but a black circular absence moving against the heavens. It was as though a small hole had been cut in the universe, and through it there watched some intelligence that had mastered distance as men had mastered steam. Halden felt, with no melodrama and no prayer, the sudden smallness of mankind. Our empires, our parliaments, our machines, and our confident maps of the world were no more than chalk marks upon a schoolroom slate, while beyond them moved powers for whom the earth might be only an experiment.
