The rain that fell in Manchester in March did not wet the ground. It struck the streets, bounced once, and remained there as shining beads like quicksilver. Children touched it and found it cold; dogs would not approach it; iron nails placed near it turned slowly toward the east.

Analysis showed no known element. The drops joined when close, separated when warmed, and moved at night by fractions of an inch toward the same compass point. A physicist proposed that they were not liquid at all, but fragments of some larger substance dispersed through the atmosphere.

By summer the beads had crossed gutters, gardens, and railway cuttings, gathering into threads that led always seaward. There, along the mudflats, they formed a single trembling band. Something vast had been broken above the earth, and its pieces, obedient to a memory deeper than chemistry, were trying to become whole again.
