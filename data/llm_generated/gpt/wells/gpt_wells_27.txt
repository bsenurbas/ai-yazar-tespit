The cloud was first seen off the coast of Cornwall, a low violet mass moving against the wind. Fishermen said it pulsed. The meteorological office denied this in its first statement, then admitted that the object had no measurable vapour content and was travelling at a fixed height of six hundred feet. It cast a shadow, though sunlight passed through it in places.

From the cliffs, one could see internal motion: slow contractions, bright knots forming and dissolving, filaments extending downward and withdrawing again. Birds that flew into it did not fall. They emerged altered in direction, silent and exhausted, as though they had crossed not air but thought. A flock of gulls entered from the west and came out in a perfect line, wingbeat for wingbeat, flying like machines.

Professor Orme proposed that the cloud was a colonial organism, not unlike certain marine creatures, but sustained in the atmosphere by electrical fields. It fed, he believed, upon nervous discharge. This unpleasant theory gained support when crowds watching it from the shore experienced simultaneous memories: childhood rooms, dead relatives, forgotten fears. The cloud had no mouth, yet it grazed upon consciousness.

When it passed over Plymouth, every sleeping person in the town woke at the same instant and spoke a single sentence in an unknown language. The sentence was written down by hundreds and found to be identical. No translation was made. By dawn the cloud had moved out over the Atlantic, larger than before. It left no corpses, no burned fields, no visible wound upon the country. It left only the feeling, common and incommunicable, that some immense creature had lowered itself briefly into the human mind and found the feeding tolerable.
