The silence began in the telegraph offices. At first it was thought that a storm had damaged the lines, though the sky over London was clear and hard as polished slate. Messages sent from Whitehall to Manchester returned upon the wire as meaningless pulses, then as long, even beats, like the ticking of a clock too large for human rooms. By evening, the same rhythm was heard in Paris, Berlin, and New York, passing through every cable and instrument that mankind had stretched across the earth.

Mr. Lorrimer, an electrical engineer of uncommon patience, was the first to see that the beats were not random. They varied with the position of the moon and increased whenever the earth’s shadow crossed a certain region of space. He proposed, with a caution that saved him from ridicule for nearly a day, that our electrical networks had become the receiving apparatus for a signal not directed at any one nation, but at the planet itself. We had built, unknowingly, a nervous system; something beyond the atmosphere had touched it.

The governments suppressed what they could, which was little. The wires continued to speak at midnight, in banks, railway stations, post offices, and private homes where the new telephones trembled on their hooks. No translation was possible, yet no man who heard the sound mistook it for nature. It had intention in it. It waited between its repetitions, as a caller waits for an answer, and mankind, having filled the world with instruments, discovered that it had no idea how to reply.
