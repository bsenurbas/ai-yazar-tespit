The machine that counted thoughts was built to settle a medical dispute. Sir Alton Greaves wished only to prove that memory had a measurable electrical residue. He fixed small silver contacts to the skull of a sleeping dog and watched the needle leap whenever the animal dreamed.

The experiment became alarming when the apparatus was applied to men. It did not merely record intensity; it classified. Hunger made one mark, fear another, calculation another. Love, to the embarrassment of several observers, made a pattern almost indistinguishable from possession.

Greaves refused all military requests for the instrument. He said that mankind had survived because the mind remained, in part, unregistered territory. But the newspapers had already printed enough. Within months, banks, courts, and marriage offices were asking whether sincerity might soon be certified by wire and needle.
