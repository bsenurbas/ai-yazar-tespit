The island was not on any chart, though ships had passed within five miles of its later position for a century. It appeared after an undersea earthquake in the South Pacific, low, black, and nearly circular, with beaches of polished stone. There were no trees. The interior consisted of shallow basins filled with clear water, and in each basin lay thousands of floating disks, pale, veined, and responsive to shadow.

Captain Drew thought them jellyfish until one of them looked at him. The disk contracted, darkened at the centre, and followed his movement with unmistaken attention. Soon every pool on the island had turned its gaze upon the landing party. There was no sound except the surf and the small wet movement of eyes adjusting themselves to men.

A naturalist travelling with the expedition argued that the island was a sensory organ, perhaps the exposed portion of a larger creature rooted in the seabed. The eye-disks were not animals but apertures. Through them, something beneath the crust of the earth, or within the oceanic depths, had at last acquired sight above water.

They left before sunset, but not before Drew placed a mirror beside one of the pools. The effect was immediate. The eyes recoiled, then gathered around the reflection in a slow tightening circle. A tremor ran through the island. Far below, something vast seemed to shift in its sleep. When the ship returned two months later, the island had vanished, but in harbours across the world sailors began reporting that the sea, under certain lights, appeared to be looking upward.
