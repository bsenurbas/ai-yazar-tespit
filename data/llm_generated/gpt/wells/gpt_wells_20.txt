The fog that entered London in November behaved with suspicious intelligence. It avoided open fires, thinned before policemen’s lanterns, and gathered most densely around hospitals, libraries, and telegraph offices. Ordinary fog is democratic; this fog had preferences.

A chemist named Rudd captured a sample in a glass bell and found that its droplets contained minute structures like coiled threads. Under heat they withdrew. Under darkness they extended. When a page of printed text was placed beneath the bell, the threads settled upon it, and by morning the ink had faded.

Within a week, half the city’s records were blank. Births, debts, titles, sentences, and treaties vanished into damp air. Rudd suggested that the fog was not feeding upon paper, but upon information fixed in visible form.
