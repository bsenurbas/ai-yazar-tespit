The last tide was not recognised at first. It came in at Whitstable one morning with the usual glimmer over the mud, lifted boats, wet ropes, and withdrew. Only the harbourmaster noticed that it had withdrawn too far. By evening the sea had retreated another hundred yards. The next day it did not return.

Along every coast, the oceans began to pull away from the land. Beaches widened into plains. Harbours became pits of rotting weed and stranded fish. Ships leaned helplessly in mud. The exposed seabed steamed under the sun, releasing smells older than mankind and revealing wrecks, bones, anchors, and strange ruins drowned before any known history.

Astronomers found the cause beyond the moon: a gravitational disturbance, vast and dark, passing through the outer system. It did not strike the earth. It merely altered the allegiance of water. The oceans, drawn by an invisible master, gathered slowly toward the equator and away from the old coasts, forming belts of depth where maps had promised shallows and deserts where nations had built their ports.

At first men rushed to claim the new land. Surveyors marked it, soldiers crossed it, companies sold shares in future farms upon the floor of the sea. Then the greater truth emerged. The tide had not ended; it had lengthened beyond human patience. The water would return in perhaps eighty years, perhaps a hundred, and when it came back it would come not as a daily motion but as a planetary correction. Cities would stand for a lifetime upon borrowed ground, and children would grow old beneath maps already condemned by the sea.
