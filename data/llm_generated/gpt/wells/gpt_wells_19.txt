The Museum opened without trustees, builders, or announcement. One morning it stood at the end of a narrow street near Holborn where, the day before, there had been a row of condemned houses. Its façade was plain stone, its doors unlocked, and above them appeared the inscription: “Things That Did Not Occur.” London, being curious after breakfast, entered.

Inside were galleries of objects both familiar and impossible. There was a crown for a republic that had never failed, a steam carriage perfected fifty years too early, a treaty that might have prevented three wars, and a child’s toy from a civilisation that had not survived its first plague. Labels gave dates, inventors, causes of non-existence, and the precise accidents by which each thing had been lost to history.

Scholars at first treated the Museum as an elaborate fraud. Then one mathematician found, in a drawer, the complete proof of a theorem he had abandoned in youth. A widow found the portrait of a son she had miscarried. An engineer discovered a safe flying machine conceived by a man who had died of influenza before drawing it. The Museum was not storing fantasy. It was cataloguing the roads by which reality had declined to travel.

No one found the curator. The building closed at dusk, and those who hid inside were found next morning outside the doors, older by a day and unwilling to speak. The final gallery, which few reached, contained a model of mankind as it might have become: calmer, taller, less predatory, and already at work among the planets. This exhibit produced more anger than wonder. Men can forgive the universe for being cruel; they find it harder to forgive evidence that it was once offered a better bargain.
