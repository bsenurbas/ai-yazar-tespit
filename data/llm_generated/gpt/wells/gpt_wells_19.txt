The growth of the children was at first praised as a triumph of modern nutrition. In Chelsea, three infants reached the size of five-year-olds within eighteen months; in Vienna, a school admitted pupils who had been in cradles the previous summer. Their intelligence kept pace with their bodies, and for a season the matter was celebrated.

Dr. Hess found the common factor in a new milk powder prepared from algae cultivated under electric lamps. The food did not feed the body in the usual sense. It stimulated some ancestral command buried in the cells, instructing flesh to hurry through its appointed stages.

The first generation of accelerated children reached maturity before the inquiry concluded. They were tall, grave, and curiously detached from their parents, whom they regarded with an affection touched by impatience. The tragedy was discovered only later: what had been gained in childhood had been borrowed from the far end of life.
