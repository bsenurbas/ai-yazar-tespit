I waited for dawn, though dawn, in that house, was a rumor told by the faithless. The child’s cry did not cease; it thinned, it deepened, it traveled through the beams and buried pipes until it was no longer a cry at all, but a presence—small, cold, and circling. The guests fled before sunrise, leaving doors open, trunks half-packed, and one candle burning upon the floor where the boy had vanished.

For many years thereafter, no living soul crossed the threshold; yet I was not alone. The house spoke in seasons: in winter, with the crack of frozen wood; in spring, with the wet bloom of mold behind the tapestries; in autumn, with leaves scratching the windows like fingers asking admittance. But always, beneath these lesser voices, came the cradle’s motion, patient and exact, and with it the knowledge that the house was not finished. It never finished.

At last, a woman came with men bearing hammers, ropes, and lanterns, and declared the place unfit for habitation. I watched from my portrait as they laughed at the walls, measured the beams, and spoke of demolition. Then the first hammer struck. The house shuddered—not in pain, but in pleasure. From every locked room came a rustle of awakening, and as the walls began to bleed their dust into the air, I saw, in the woman’s widening eyes, the terrible understanding that some houses do not fall. They are opened.
