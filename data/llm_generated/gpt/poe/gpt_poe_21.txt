The word had scarcely dried before the room altered, not by movement, but by recognition; the desk became the table of my childhood, the candle became the pale lamp beside my mother’s chair, and the walls receded into a chamber I had buried more completely than any corpse. There was the window where rain had once gathered in trembling beads; there the rug stained by wine and anger; there the door behind which my brother had waited for an apology I never gave.

Home! What monstrous tenderness resides in that word, when the soul has made of it both sanctuary and sentence. I stood amid the furniture of a life that had not forgiven me, and from every object came a soft indictment: the chair I had overturned, the letter I had hidden, the hearth before which I had spoken gently while thinking cruelly. The house, the key, the grave, the portrait—all had been only elaborate ornaments around this simpler ruin.

Then the childhood door opened, and my brother entered as a boy, not the burial-pale judge of the halls below, but living, small, and hopeful, with mud upon his shoes and a folded paper in his hand. He held it out to me. I knew what was written there before I took it; I knew, and still my hand shook. Upon the page, in that round and innocent script, were only three words: Tell me why.
