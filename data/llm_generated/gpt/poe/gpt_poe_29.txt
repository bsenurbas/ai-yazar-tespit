Where I faded, no monument rose; the meadow kept no memory but the bending of grass beneath rain. And yet, in seasons when the air grew heavy and the evening leaned close to the earth, travelers crossing that field would sometimes pause, not from fear, but from the sudden impression that they had forgotten something merciful. They would look toward the western trees, where mist gathered in the shape of a roofline, and then pass on, carrying a silence they could not name.

Beneath the soil, the old house did not sleep; it had become something less terrible and more enduring. Not a prison, not a curse, but a caution laid into the ground like a buried bell. When lies were spoken nearby, when pride dressed itself as duty, when a child’s question was met with a door closing, the grass would darken, the air would cool, and somewhere far below, a key would turn once—not to open, but to warn.

And on certain mornings, after long rain, a single black flower appeared where the nursery had stood. No hand planted it; no root held it. It bloomed only until sunrise, its petals wet and shining like ink, and within its center lay a drop of light so small that it might have been mistaken for dew. Then the sun would touch it, the flower would vanish, and the field would remain: open, ordinary, and free of doors.
