I crossed the threshold, though no threshold had stood there for many years, and the world beyond received me with a silence so ordinary that it seemed, at first, a new species of terror. The garden lay before me in ruin: statues noseless and blind, fountains choked with leaves, cypresses bent like mourners who had forgotten the corpse. Yet above them, impossibly, the morning widened; and the air, freed from the house’s long breath, moved across my face with the shy indifference of life continuing.

Behind me, the old walls faded into their simplest truth. No more eyes watched from the plaster; no hidden stair descended beneath the nursery; no cradle marked the measure of inherited sin. Only a house remained—broken, damp, abandoned—and in that plainness there was a horror deeper than enchantment, for I saw at last how little supernatural aid is needed for a family to become a tomb. Men had built the rooms, men had locked the doors, men had taught the silence to speak.

At the gate I turned once more, compelled by grief rather than fear, and saw in an upper window a pale hand lift in farewell. Whether it belonged to my brother, the buried mother, the child, or some remnant of myself, I cannot say. The house did not call me back. It did not forgive me. It merely stood beneath the growing day, emptied of its legend, while from the earth beneath its foundations came one last faint sound—not a knock, not a cry, but the soft turning of a key in a door that no longer required opening.
