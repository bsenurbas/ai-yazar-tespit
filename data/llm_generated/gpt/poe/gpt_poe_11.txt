I turned the key in the first lock, and the sound it made was not metallic, but human—a thin gasp drawn from the throat of the house itself. The door opened upon a dining room set for a feast no mouth had tasted; the plates were filled with ash, the goblets with rainwater, and at the head of the table sat my father, long dead, carving the empty air with a silver knife. He did not look up. The dead, I learned, are sometimes too ashamed to haunt properly.

Room by room, I walked the estate of our ruin. In one chamber, a bride stood forever before a mirror that showed only her grave; in another, a soldier polished medals that had been awarded for cowardice; in another still, a little girl counted teeth in a porcelain bowl and asked each one whose mouth it had betrayed. With every door I opened, the knocking ceased behind it, and a new sound began elsewhere, deeper, fainter, more urgent. Remembrance was not release. It was appetite.

At last I came to the final door, smaller than all the rest, hidden behind the torn paper of the nursery wall. No name was written upon it, yet I knew it as I knew the pulse beneath my skin. The key resisted; the cradle rocked; my brother, standing behind me, began to weep without sound. When the lock gave way, I saw not a room, but my own face as it had been before envy, before silence, before the first secret found shelter in my heart—and from that innocent darkness, a hand reached out to take me home.
