The nursery had not changed, though no hand had tended it for generations; the little bed remained beneath its veil of yellow lace, the rocking horse bowed its wooden head, and the cradle—ah, the cradle!—waited in the center of the room with the awful dignity of an altar. I approached it as one approaches a wound: unwilling, yet compelled by the knowledge that refusal is itself a form of touch.

Within lay no child, no corpse, no relic wrapped in ancestral linen, but a key. It was small, black, and warm as living bone, and when I lifted it, the walls exhaled. At once the portraits above began to whisper, not in plea or warning, but in recitation, naming door after door, sin after sin, life after life, until I saw the true architecture of the house: not timber, not stone, but confession layered upon confession, each room built from what someone had tried to bury.

Then my brother entered, though I had left him in death, in memory, in every place where the dead are meant to remain. He looked upon the key in my hand and smiled with that old, unbearable sadness. “Keeper,” he said, and the title passed through me like a nail. Behind him, the nursery door opened upon a thousand darkened halls, and from each, softly, patiently, came the sound of someone knocking to be remembered.
