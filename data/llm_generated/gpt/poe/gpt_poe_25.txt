The paper’s ash rose once, curled like a question, and vanished into the chimney’s black throat. I watched until nothing remained but a pale smudge upon the grate, and I felt, not peace, but the humbler absence of pursuit. The room held no hidden door, no patient key, no cradle measuring the pulse of buried years. There was only morning, and the plain furniture of survival.

Yet silence, when no longer forced, has its own voice. In it I heard not my brother, nor the house, nor the long procession of accusing dead, but the small and mortal sounds of life proceeding without witness: water poured into a basin, boots upon the stair, a woman laughing in the street below. These things, once too slight for my grief to notice, now seemed immense. I had mistaken terror for depth; I had mistaken punishment for truth.

So I opened the window. The air entered cold, carrying the smoke of bakeries, the dampness of stone, and the distant bell that marked no doom, only the hour. I placed my hands upon the sill, and beneath my fingers felt not the pulse of the house, but the worn grain of wood, innocent and mute. Then, from somewhere beyond the roofs, a child called another child by name—and no door answered.
