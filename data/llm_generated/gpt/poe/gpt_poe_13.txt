Each night thereafter I spoke, though no mortal ear could bear the language in which the house preferred to be named. It began with a whisper in the nursery, passed through the walls as a fever passes through blood, and rose at last to the rafters, where the old beams answered with the groan of ships sinking in black water. At my tongue’s command, the doors awakened; at my breath, the portraits opened their eyes; and at the first syllable of remembrance, the cradle resumed its measured motion.

Yet power, if such bondage may be called power, brought no mastery. I was less keeper than instrument, less man than hinge; and the house, playing upon me, opened griefs I had never known, drawing from my throat the histories of strangers, servants, mothers, liars, lovers, and children whose names had been eaten by dust. Their sorrows did not pass through me cleanly. They lodged within me, one by one, until my own guilt became almost dear to me, for it was at least familiar.

But one evening—if evening can be said to exist where time crawls on its knees—the house fell silent before my recitation was complete. The cradle stopped. The portraits looked not at me, but beyond me. I turned, and there in the nursery doorway stood a figure neither dead nor living, neither guest nor memory: a woman robed in rain, holding in her arms a sleeping child. “I have come,” she said, and the walls drew back in fear, “for the one sin this house did not commit, but merely hid.”
