The moon had risen, pale and diseased, above the shattered gables of the old house, and its light—if light it could be called—fell in narrow, trembling bands upon the corridor where I stood, listening to that most dreadful of all sounds: the sound of something almost silent. From the walls came a faint moisture, cold as the breath of a tomb; from the floorboards, a slow and secret creaking, as though the house itself, burdened by memory, shifted in its sleep and dreamed of ruin.

I had come there in defiance of reason, summoned by a letter written in a hand I knew too well, though its author had been dead these seven years. It spoke only of return, of forgiveness, and of a chamber beneath the eastern wing where “the heart of the matter” still remained. At first I laughed—yes, laughed!—but the laughter died before it reached my lips, for the seal upon the letter bore the impression of a ring buried with my brother in the black winter of our family’s disgrace.

Downward, then, I went, through dust, through darkness, through a passage so narrow that the stones pressed against my shoulders like accusing hands; and at last I found the door. It was small, iron-bound, and wet with a red dew that no season could explain. When I touched it, something behind the door answered—not with a knock, nor with a voice, but with a pulse; slow, deliberate, and hideously patient, as though some buried thing had waited all these years not to live again, but merely to be heard.
