For many mornings thereafter, I practiced the discipline of remaining among the living. I bought bread while it was warm, answered letters before they yellowed, spoke plainly where once I would have folded meaning into silence and called it restraint. Yet I did not mistake these small acts for absolution. They were not keys; they opened no grand door. They were merely steps upon a road, and for a man long imprisoned by his own echo, a road is no small mercy.

At times, passing a darkened window, I still saw behind my face the faint outline of another hall, receding, patient, almost tender in its malice. But it no longer commanded me. I would stop, look steadily into that reflected gloom, and name what stood there: fear, pride, hunger for innocence once squandered. The image would tremble, thin, and fade, as mist fades when the sun has no drama in it, only persistence.

And so, when my own final evening came—not with thunder, nor the creak of hinges, nor the black ceremony I had once imagined, but with rain touching the glass and a lamp burning low—I did not call for the dead. I opened the window, listened to the city breathing beyond me, and felt the old terror rise one last time, courteous and cold, to take its place beside the bed. “Yes,” I whispered to it, as one greets a familiar guest. “I know you.” Then I turned my face toward the rain, and slept without dreaming.
