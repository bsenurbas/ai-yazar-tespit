Years later, a road was cut across the meadow, and men came with carts of stone, speaking loudly of progress, drainage, and the stubbornness of old ground. Their boots pressed the grass flat; their iron stakes pierced the soil; their laughter rose and fell in the morning air with that careless confidence by which the living declare all buried things irrelevant. Yet when the first shovel bit deep, the earth exhaled—not foulness, not decay, but the dry scent of old paper kept too long in a locked drawer.

They found no bones. This disappointed them. Men prefer guilt when it has a shape they can measure, lift, and carry elsewhere. Instead, they uncovered a small wooden threshold, blackened but unburnt, lying alone beneath the field as though a house had once begun there and then thought better of itself. Upon it were carved many names, some noble, some poor, some worn to scratches; and at the center, in letters no rain had softened, a single sentence remained: Tell the child why.

By dusk, no man would touch it. The road bent around the place, as roads do when pride meets something older than command, and the meadow kept its quiet circle. From that day forward, travelers passing at twilight saw, for an instant, a doorway standing in the field with no wall about it and no darkness beyond it—only a pale interior light, and the sound, faint but unmistakable, of someone within finally answering.
