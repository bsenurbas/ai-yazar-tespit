The name did not echo; it descended. It passed through the nursery floor, through the vault, through those black corridors suspended beneath the house, until even the earliest stones seemed to hear and tremble. Then, from below, there came a sound more dreadful than any cry I had yet endured: a lullaby, faint and broken, sung by a voice that had been denied even madness as a refuge.

The woman in rain lowered the child, and where its feet touched the floor, the boards bloomed with dark water. In that water appeared the first room, the first locked door, the first hand turning the key from the outside. I saw her there—the mother whose name I had spoken—seated upright in a chamber without windows, her hair unbound, her eyes clear, her crime no more than memory and speech. Men stood beyond the door and called her condition necessary. The house, young then, listened; and learned.

At last the walls split from roof to root, not with the violence of collapse, but with the slow shame of confession. The portraits fell face-down. The cradle cracked. My brother reached for me, but his hand passed through the dust where my body had stood, for the house, losing its first lie, had lost the language that held me there. As dawn entered through the broken ceiling, pale and uncertain, I heard the woman in rain whisper the buried mother’s name once more—and every door, above and below, opened without a sound.
