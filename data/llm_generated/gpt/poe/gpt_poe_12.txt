The hand was small, unstained, and terrible, for innocence, when returned too late, is more dreadful than any corpse. It did not seize me; it invited me. Behind me, my brother’s silent grief became the rustle of leaves over a grave, while before me the child I had been stood in a room without walls, holding a candle that burned with no flame, only memory. His eyes were mine, yet not mine; they had not yet learned concealment.

“Come,” he seemed to say, though his lips remained closed, and the house repeated the summons through pipe, plaster, hinge, and bone. I stepped forward, and with that step the years fell from me—not gently, but in strips, like wet paper from a ruined wall. I saw every choice unmade, every mercy possible, every word that might have saved us; and there, in that merciless clarity, I found the punishment greater than death: not to suffer what had been done, but to behold what might have been.

When I touched the child’s hand, the house gave one vast shudder, and every door opened at once. The portraits sighed, the cradle stilled, my brother bowed his head, and from the depths came a rising chorus—not of forgiveness, no, never that—but of recognition. Then the child placed the black key upon my tongue, and I understood the final office of a keeper: to speak the house into being each night, so that no buried sin, however small, may sleep unremembered.
