Dawn did not free the house; it exposed it. The light entered timidly at first, touching the broken beams, the fallen portraits, the nursery floor slick with that impossible black water; then it widened, and with each pale inch it claimed, some hidden corridor vanished, some old whisper lost its mouth, some grief—long enthroned in darkness—became only grief again.

I found myself standing where the cradle had been, though I no longer knew whether I stood as man, memory, or the last shadow of a curse already spent. My brother was gone. The woman in rain was gone. Only the child remained, sleeping upon the floorboards as calmly as if the house had never learned cruelty, as if generations had not fed it names, bones, and silence. I knelt beside it, and for the first time since my descent, I did not hear knocking.

Then the walls gave one final sigh, vast and weary, and the house began to settle into its own ruin. Not collapse—no; nothing so mercifully quick. It softened. It loosened. It became timber, stone, dust, and morning. As I turned toward the open threshold, the child stirred and smiled in sleep; and I understood, with a sorrow too quiet for terror, that remembrance had not destroyed the house. It had made escape unnecessary.
