I awoke beside the ash tree, with dawn spread thinly over the fields and the road continuing before me as if no door had ever interrupted its course. The black key was gone. In my hand lay instead the folded paper, dry, ordinary, and light as any scrap a child might carry home from school. Yet I could not open it; for I knew that some writings are not meant to be read, but kept close, where their unanswered questions may trouble the heart into honesty.

For many days I walked, and wherever I lodged, I listened at night for the cradle, the knocking, the breath behind the wall—but heard only rain, shutters, horses in their stalls, and the soft unrest of men dreaming beside their own unspoken griefs. The world had not become kind. It had merely become legible. Every inn, every churchyard, every family table seemed built upon some buried tenderness or convenient silence, and I understood then that the house had been monstrous only because it had been truthful without mercy.

Years have passed, if years may be trusted, and I now live in a narrow room overlooking a street where children call to one another at dusk. Sometimes, when the light declines and the window darkens, I see behind my reflection the faint outline of a door. It never opens. It need not. I keep the folded paper beneath my pillow, and each night, before sleep can claim me, I place my hand upon it and answer, softly, again and again, until the dead are satisfied with the effort: “Because I was afraid—and because I remember.”
