Beyond the gate, the road stretched pale and wet beneath the morning, and I followed it with the slow uncertainty of one newly released from a dream that has bitten too deeply into the flesh. Birds moved in the hedges; a cart creaked somewhere beyond the fields; the world, indifferent and immense, resumed its small negotiations with time. Yet with every step I took, I felt in my coat pocket the weight of something I had not carried through the threshold: the black key.

I drew it forth beneath an ash tree, and there, in the honest light of day, it seemed no longer bone, nor iron, nor any substance born of earth, but a fragment of night that had forgotten to disappear. Upon its narrow stem, letters slowly surfaced, wet and red as if written from within: not the name of the house, not my brother’s name, not the buried mother’s, but mine. I laughed then—quietly, horribly—for I understood that a door need not remain standing to remain locked.

When I looked back, the house was gone. In its place lay only an open field, silvered with dew, harmless beneath the sun. But at my feet, where the road should have continued, a small dark door had appeared in the earth, no wider than a grave and no deeper than a breath. From behind it came a gentle knocking; not pleading, not accusing, but patient. And in my hand, the key grew warm.
