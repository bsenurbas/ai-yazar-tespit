Yet the answer did not end the visitation; it changed only its posture. No longer did the past come as a judge robed in shadow, nor as a house with doors beneath its floors, but as a chair left slightly turned from the table, a second cup cooling beside my own, a footfall stopping just beyond the reach of lamplight. I learned, in those years, that remembrance is not a storm through which one passes, but a climate in which one must learn to breathe.

One winter evening, as snow began to gather upon the sill, there came a knock at my door—three soft taps, neither hurried nor hesitant. I opened it and found no one in the hall, only a child’s muddy footprint upon the threshold, though the street below lay white and untouched. In my room, the folded paper had opened by itself upon the pillow, and beneath the old question, in a hand no longer childish, another line had appeared: Then live otherwise.

I stood there until the candle sank into its own waxen grave, and for the first time the darkness that followed did not seem alive with accusation. It was only darkness—vast, waiting, and poor in miracles. I closed the paper, placed it beside my heart, and slept. And if, in sleep, I heard from some immeasurable distance the faint music of a key turning, I did not rise to follow it; for some doors, once answered, are best left to open inward.
