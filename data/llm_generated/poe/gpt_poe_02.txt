Beyond the door there lay no chamber, as I had feared, but a stair descending into a depth so absolute that my candle seemed less a flame than a frightened thought, flickering at the edge of extinction. Each step groaned beneath me with the weary protest of age, and from below there rose a vapor—thick, mineral, and faintly sweet—which coiled about my face like the breath of some vast sleeper. I knew then, with that peculiar certainty which visits the mind only when reason has abandoned it, that I was not entering a hidden room of the house, but the secret conscience of my bloodline.

At the stair’s end I found a circular vault, its stones blackened by old smoke and inscribed with names nearly erased by time. In the center stood a cradle, though no child had been born to our family in decades; and above it, suspended by a chain from the ceiling, hung a mirror whose surface did not reflect my face, but the face of my brother as I had last seen him—cold, beautiful, and smiling with the tenderness of a judge. Then the cradle moved. Not greatly, not violently, but with the smallest motion, as though rocked by an invisible hand.

I tried to pray; I tried to scream; I tried, at last, to flee—but the mirror darkened, the stones whispered, and the cradle began its slow, infernal rhythm. From within it came a sigh, ancient and infantile, and then a voice—my own voice, though older, broken, and wet with grave-dust—murmured the words I had written on the night of my brother’s burial: “Let him be forgotten.” At that, the candle died; and in the blind and breathing dark, I understood that memory, once murdered, does not perish—it waits.
