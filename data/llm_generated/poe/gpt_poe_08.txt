The woman did not command the men to stop; command had left her, as blood leaves a wound, silently and all at once. The hammers fell from their hands, one by one, and the lanterns, though still aflame, gave forth no light, but only a dull red stain that crawled across the walls and revealed, beneath the peeling paper, not brick, nor plaster, but handwriting—thousands upon thousands of lines, cramped, desperate, and all repeating a single phrase: I was here.

Then the floorboards rose. Not broke, not split, not yielded to age, but rose, slowly and with dreadful ceremony, as lids rise from eyes long feigning sleep. Beneath them lay no cellar, no vault, no earth, but a vast black depth filled with doors, each suspended in the air, each knocking softly from within. The men fled then, or tried to flee; yet every exit opened only into another hall, another stair, another room where some old guilt sat waiting in the shape of furniture.

And I, from my portrait, watched the woman turn at last toward me, her face whitened by the knowledge that escape was no longer a direction, but a memory. She reached out—not to beg, not to accuse, but to touch the frame in which my torment had been hung; and when her fingers met the wood, the canvas softened like water. I fell through her gaze into the hall, she rose into the portrait, and behind us both the house, satisfied with its exchange, closed every door at once.
