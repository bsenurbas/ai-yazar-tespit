Within that darkness there was, at first, a mercy; for it had neither shape nor sound, and my mind, starved of terror’s objects, almost persuaded itself that annihilation had come gently. But soon I felt beneath my palm the grain of polished wood, above my face the close and breathless ceiling, and around my limbs the narrow courtesy of a coffin built not for the dead, but for the remembering. I beat upon it once—only once—for the answering echo came not from outside, but from the chambers of my own breast.

Then began the counting. A drop fell somewhere near my ear; then another; then another, each interval precise, judicial, intolerable. With every drop there returned a forgotten hour: the letter unanswered, the hand withdrawn, the candle left burning beside a sickbed while I, coward that I was, closed the door and called it necessity. The coffin did not imprison me; it instructed me. Its darkness became a ledger, and my soul, at last, was made to read.

When the lid opened, I did not rise into the vault, nor the corridor, nor any room known to man. I looked down instead from a cracked portrait in the great hall, where strangers, years hence, would pause and remark upon the curious grief in my painted eyes. Below them, beneath the floor, the house gave a slow contented sigh; and somewhere, deep within its ancient timber, a cradle began again to move.
