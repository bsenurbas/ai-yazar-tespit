I stood free—free!—yet the word curdled in the chamber of my mind, for freedom, when granted by a house such as that, is but another corridor whose end has not yet learned to show itself. My hands, once painted, now trembled with warmth; my lungs drew in the dust of the hall; and beneath my feet the boards recognized me, not as master, not as guest, but as something returned from storage. Above the mantel, the woman’s portrait stared down with fresh terror in its varnished eyes.

I might have fled then, had the door remained where doors belong. But the entrance had vanished, replaced by a wall of portraits—my brother, the child, the men with hammers, the pale mothers of our line, and others whose faces I did not know but whose suffering I understood with the intimate shame of inheritance. Each pair of eyes followed me as I moved, and from their painted mouths came no accusation, only breathing. The house had not collected ghosts. It had curated witnesses.

Then, from the nursery below, the cradle gave a single creak. The portraits went still. The dust settled. Even the walls seemed to lean inward, listening. I knew what was asked of me before the question formed, and I descended—not as a prisoner dragged to judgment, but as a servant answering the bell. For in that final and most monstrous revelation, I understood that the house had never needed victims. It needed keepers.
