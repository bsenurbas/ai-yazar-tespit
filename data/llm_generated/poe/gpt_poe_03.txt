I do not know how long I remained there, kneeling upon the cold stones, with my hands pressed over my ears and yet hearing—hearing!—that slow cradle-song rise from the darkness like a confession sung by the dead. The air thickened around me until each breath became a labor, and the names upon the wall, those faint ancestral scars, seemed to loosen from the stone and hover in the vault like pale insects drawn to the last warmth of my terror.

Then, from the extinguished candle, there came a spark—not of fire, but of memory—and by its ghastly glimmer I saw the cradle empty. Empty! Yet the chain above it swung, the mirror trembled, and upon its dim surface appeared not my brother, not myself, but the house as it had been long before my birth: bright-windowed, laughing, innocent, and doomed. One by one the windows darkened, as though unseen hands moved from room to room, closing the eyes of the past.

When I rose, the stair was gone. In its place stretched the corridor from which I had first descended, though now it was lined with doors I had never seen, and from behind each came the murmur of a life denied, a plea unanswered, a mercy withheld. At the far end stood my brother, holding the letter in his burial-white hand; and when he smiled, I understood the final cruelty of that house—that it had not summoned me to punish the dead, but to make me its newest room.
