The strangers came at dusk, as all intruders must, their laughter bright and vulgar against the solemn hush of the hall. They admired the staircase, the ruined velvet, the long descent of ancestral faces; yet when they reached my portrait, their voices faltered. One of them—a child with solemn eyes—lifted a finger toward me and whispered that the painted man had moved.

Ah, would that I had not! For within the prison of that canvas, I had learned the full economy of damnation: to see without turning, to hear without speaking, to remember without dying. Night after night the house opened its hidden doors, not for me, but within me; and from each chamber came some small forgotten wickedness, dressed now in the grandeur of judgment. The cradle below marked the hours, back and forth, back and forth, as no clock made by human hands would dare.

At midnight, the child returned alone, carrying a candle that bent away from my portrait as if from a draft. He gazed at me with pity—pity!—and in that unbearable mercy I felt the paint of my lips soften, crack, and part. “Do not listen,” I tried to say; but the house had already heard me. The floor beneath him sighed open, the candle fell, and as his small cry sank into the darkness, my painted hand rose slowly to cover my painted eyes.
