It was on a night of unwholesome calm, when the fog lay upon the low fields in pale, horizontal folds, and the stars, though not obscured, appeared remote and pitiless, that a stranger came walking into the little town at the end of the marsh road. No inhabitant, even among those whose infirmities kept them wakeful past the hour of decent sleep, could afterward agree from which direction he had first emerged; for some maintained that he had descended from the hill where the abandoned mill stood against the sky, while others, with less confidence but greater terror, declared that he had risen out of the vapors by the bridge, as if the earth itself had exhaled him. He was dressed in black, though not with the elegance of mourning; rather with that severe and obsolete propriety which suggests garments preserved for a solemn occasion long delayed, and as he passed beneath the dim lamps fixed to the fronts of the houses, each flame gave a small, troubled flutter, like a conscience stirred by an unwelcome remembrance.

The town, which had once possessed a modest trade in wool and timber, had by degrees withdrawn from prosperity, then from traffic, and finally from the ordinary sympathies of mankind; its people married seldom beyond their own narrow circle, kept their doors barred before dusk, and spoke of neighboring places with the faint disdain of prisoners who have persuaded themselves that liberty is vulgar. There were stories, of course, for every isolated settlement breeds them as damp wood breeds rot; yet here the stories had no shape fit for telling, no beginning upon which the mind might safely rest, but existed instead as gestures cut short, rooms left unused, letters burned unread, and certain family faces turned carefully from the firelight when winter talk grew thin. Thus the stranger’s arrival did not create fear so much as uncover it. Behind every shutter, hands grew still. In cradles, infants whimpered without waking. In the inn yard, a horse pressed itself against the stable wall and shook until the boards trembled.

When the stranger entered the public room, the company within beheld him with that mute hostility which men reserve for a witness they had thought dead, though none could have said why such a fancy seized them. He bowed slightly to the landlord, removed from his breast a small square of linen marked by a brown and ancient stain, and laid it upon the counter with a delicacy more dreadful than violence. “I have come,” said he, “for the room that faces the water.” At this request the oldest man present uttered a sound not unlike a laugh, yet stripped of all mirth, and the landlord, who had been polishing a glass already clean, let it fall unbroken from his hand. Above, in the vacant chamber whose window had been nailed shut for twenty years, a chair scraped once across the floor; slowly, with intention! The stranger raised his eyes toward the ceiling, and in the silence that followed, every soul in that room perceived that the town had not been forgotten by the world—it had merely been left alone until its appointed auditor should arrive.
