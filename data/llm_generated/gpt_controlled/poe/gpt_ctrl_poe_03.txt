Upon a November night, when the wind moved over the moor with a hollow and funereal respiration, and the village lay withdrawn into itself like a guilty thought concealed behind the brow, there appeared upon the southern causeway a figure whose coming was neither heard nor expected, yet whose presence, even before it had taken form beneath the dim lamps, seemed to trouble the sleep of walls, wells, and thresholds. The place was small, ancient, and almost forgotten; a dozen houses, a chapel with a cracked bell, an inn whose sign creaked without rhythm, and beyond these only the black fenland, the thorn hedges, and the road which travelers, with prudent instinct, had long ceased to favor after dusk. Yet the stranger advanced with neither lantern nor companion, his face half-buried beneath the brim of a weather-stained hat, his garments pale with dust and mist, and his step so measured that it suggested not a journey newly ended, but a sentence long ago pronounced and now, at last, executed.

Those who saw him first were not the bold, for boldness was no common inheritance in that place; they were old women sleepless by their hearths, a lame boy posted at an attic pane, and the keeper of the toll-house, who later declared that the stranger had passed without casting a shadow, though the lamp behind him burned with a steady flame. In other towns such testimony might have been mocked, but here, where every family possessed some sealed grief, some discolored recollection, some whispered prohibition handed down with the bed-linen and the prayer-books, no one laughed; for the inhabitants had long suspected that their isolation was not merely geographical, but moral, and that the hills around them were less a boundary than a wall. By midnight, though no bell had rung and no messenger had gone abroad, the whole village knew that a visitor had arrived, and knew, moreover, with a certainty born of dread rather than evidence, that he had come for one among them.

At the inn, when he entered, conversation did not cease, for there had been none to cease; the few men gathered there sat as they had sat for years, each guarding his own silence, each nursing in his cup a bitterness older than the drink. The stranger removed his hat, and the room, already low and close, seemed to contract about him; his hair was white, yet his countenance bore not age, but that more terrible freshness which belongs to a face preserved by a single consuming purpose. He asked for no supper, declined the fire, and requested only a chamber facing east, though the eastward rooms had been shut since a winter so remote that no one named it without lowering his voice. At this, the innkeeper’s hand shook upon the ledger, and a stain of ink spread beneath the nib like a wound opening in paper. “You know the room, then?” he whispered. The stranger turned his eyes upon him—calm, luminous, intolerably patient—and answered, “I know what was left there.” Then every heart in that miserable assembly understood that the night had not brought a guest, but a reckoning; and in the rafters above, something unseen stirred once, softly, as if it too had been waiting!
