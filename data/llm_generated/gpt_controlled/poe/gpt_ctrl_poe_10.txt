It was during a night of such sepulchral quiet that the very air appeared to have been laid under an injunction of silence, when a stranger descended the long chalk road and entered the hamlet, whose few dwellings clung to the slope like guilty creatures fearing exposure. The houses, though inhabited, displayed no cheerful sign of occupancy; their windows were narrow and dim, their doors warped by damp, their chimneys without smoke, and their walls so deeply stained by age and weather that they seemed less the work of masons than the natural growth of the melancholy earth. The stranger came alone, wrapped in a cloak fastened at the throat by a tarnished pin, and though the mud lay thick upon the road behind him, no soil marked his boots; a circumstance which, had any resident possessed the courage to name it, might have broken at once the miserable composure with which the town had long concealed its inward sickness.

No one greeted him, yet no one failed to know of his arrival. A pressure passed from house to house, intimate and soundless, troubling sleepers in their beds, stiffening hands upon latches, drawing the breath from those who stood concealed behind curtains and dared not stir. For many years the hamlet had endured its isolation with the sullen pride of a penitent who mistakes punishment for virtue; strangers were distrusted, letters were answered late or not at all, and old events, though never spoken of, governed every domestic habit with a tyranny more complete than law. There were rooms that remained unopened, cupboards whose contents had not been touched since before many of the living were born, and paths beyond the orchard upon which no child was permitted to play. Thus the stranger’s slow passage through the street awakened not surprise, but recognition of a kind more dreadful than memory; it was as if each watcher had expected him always, while praying never to witness the hour of his approach.

At the inn, where the public room held only a mean fire, a table scarred by knives, and four men whose silence had the density of accusation, the stranger entered without asking leave. He removed his gloves with scrupulous care, laid them beside a sealed packet of yellow paper, and looked upon the landlord with an expression so composed, so mournfully patient, that rage would have seemed merciful beside it. “I require the chamber at the rear,” he said, “and before dawn, the company of those who remember.” The landlord’s jaw slackened; one of the men crossed the room as if to bar the door, then stopped, for the latch had already fallen of its own accord. From somewhere within the walls there came a faint and measured tapping, neither loud nor near, yet possessed of such deliberate intelligence that every face turned pale. The stranger inclined his head, as though acknowledging an answer. Then the packet upon the table darkened at its edges, the fire sank into a red and watchful glow, and the town, locked in its hollow of hills, seemed at last to understand that remorse, though buried under decades of custom, had risen and taken lodging among them!
