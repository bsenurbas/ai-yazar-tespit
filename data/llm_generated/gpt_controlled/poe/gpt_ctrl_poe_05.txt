Near the deadest quarter of the night, when the last cottage taper had guttered into smoke and the fields lay drowned beneath a pallid exhalation from the marshes, there came into the village of Harrowmere a stranger whose arrival was so noiseless that many afterward insisted he had not approached along the road at all, but had merely become visible where the darkness was thickest. The village stood at the end of a forgotten lane, pressed between a black wood and a sluggish stream, and seemed less a settlement of living men than the residue of some old calamity; its roofs sagged, its windows squinted toward the road with a bleared suspicion, and the very stones of the square appeared worn not by traffic, but by waiting. The stranger wore a coat of antiquated cut, buttoned high about the throat, and carried beneath one arm a narrow parcel wrapped in oilcloth; yet it was not this object, nor his colorless face, nor the unnatural dryness of his boots after so miry a road, that chilled those few who saw him from behind their curtains—it was the grave serenity with which he looked upon each dwelling, as though he had counted them before.

No bell marked the hour, for the bell had cracked in a storm some thirty winters past and had thereafter remained mute, a blackened shape in the little steeple; nevertheless, as the stranger crossed the square, several inhabitants woke at once, seized by the same obscure oppression, the same conviction that some presence had entered not merely the town, but the locked interior of their recollections. In that place, men did not speak freely of former days. A prudent silence had been cultivated there, first as habit, then as inheritance, until children learned, before they learned their letters, that certain questions brought a pallor to the mouth and a tremor to the hand. Thus the stranger’s passage beneath the leaning sign of the inn was received not as an event, but as the visible return of a thing long deferred; and though he had uttered no word, every shuttered room seemed to grow smaller, every sleeper more wakeful, every secret less securely buried in the breast.

The innkeeper, a broad man whose boldness had been much praised by those who had never tested it, was trimming the lamp behind his counter when the latch lifted of itself, or seemed to lift, and the stranger stood within. For a moment neither man moved. The lamp flame lengthened, then shrank; the rafters gave a faint dry sigh; and from the room above, which had been kept locked since the death of the innkeeper’s mother, there came the slow dragging of something heavy across the floor. At this sound the innkeeper’s face lost all its earthly habit of command, and he whispered, with the cracked humility of a child discovered in some shameful act, “What do you want of us?” The stranger placed the oilcloth parcel upon the counter and untied it with hands of almost ceremonial patience. Within lay a small ledger, warped by damp, its clasp eaten green with age. “Only the truth,” he replied; and at that mild sentence the wind rose suddenly around the house, not loud, but close—close as breath at the ear!
