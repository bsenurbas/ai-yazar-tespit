It was past the hour when even the most stubborn lamp should have yielded to darkness, and the small town, lodged between a drowned meadow and a wall of firs, lay under a sky so low and livid that it seemed not heaven above, but some vast, bruised ceiling pressing slowly downward upon the roofs. Along the northern road, where no traveler had been seen after sunset for many years, there moved a solitary figure, wrapped in a coat whose long skirts were stiff with rain, though no rain had fallen since noon. His progress was soundless, yet not furtive; he came with the awful composure of one expected by the dead and dreaded by the living, and as he passed the first cottage, a woman who watched from behind a torn curtain felt, without knowing why, that every secret room in her memory had opened at once.

The town had once been ordinary, or so the oldest inhabitants said when compelled by strangers to speak of its history; but ordinary things, neglected long enough, acquire a malignity of their own, and this place had ripened into something close to human guilt. Its lanes were narrow, its gardens choked with bitter herbs, its chapel door swollen fast in its frame, and its people possessed that pale, inward look common to those who have chosen silence so often that silence has become not a habit, but a master. No child laughed there after dark. No one sang at work. Even the dogs, lean and watchful under carts and porches, seemed to understand that certain sounds were unwelcome after dusk. Thus, when the stranger entered the square, the disturbance spread without cry or signal: shutters drew tighter, breath shortened, candles sank low in their saucers, and in more than one locked chest some forgotten object appeared, by mere memory, to grow heavier.

He stopped before the inn, a crooked house of timber and stone, where three men and the landlord sat beneath a ceiling blackened by generations of smoke, each pretending not to listen for the tread now mounting the outer step. The latch lifted; the door opened inward; and the stranger stood there, pale, erect, and strangely dry-eyed, with a small leather case held against his breast. For several moments he regarded them with a courtesy so grave that insult would have been less terrible. Then he placed the case upon the counter and drew from it a strip of faded ribbon, a child’s glove, and a broken silver clasp, laying each article down as though it were evidence before a court no mortal law had convened. The landlord’s mouth moved, but speech failed him. From the shuttered room above came a faint sound, not loud enough to be named, yet deliberate enough to be obeyed. The stranger raised one finger toward the ceiling and whispered, “It has waited long enough.” At those words, the fire bent low, the men rose without willing it, and the town, outside in its pit of darkness, seemed to listen!
