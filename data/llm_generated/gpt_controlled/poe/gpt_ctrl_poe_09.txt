It was near midnight, and upon a road so narrow that the brambles from either side clasped above it like the fingers of a buried hand, there came a stranger into the town of Alderwick, though none had heard the gate at the outer stile give way, nor the gravel confess beneath his tread. The moon, swollen and indistinct behind a veil of vapor, cast upon the roofs a wan illumination, under which every chimney, gable, and warped shutter appeared less built than exhumed from some elder century of sorrow; and the town itself, lying at the foot of a treeless hill, seemed to shrink from the figure who now entered its single street with a composure so grave that haste, fear, and fatigue appeared vulgar passions beneath him. He carried no bundle, no staff, no lantern; only a folded handkerchief pressed within one gloved hand, and upon that handkerchief, though the night was damp, there spread a stain so dark and settled that the eye recoiled from naming it.

The inhabitants of Alderwick were not asleep, though they had long pretended to be. In that town sleep was a negotiation, never a surrender; men lay rigid beside their wives, listening to old boards speak under no visible weight, while women stared into the black corners of their rooms and rehearsed, in silence, the omissions by which a life may be made outwardly clean. For years, perhaps for generations, the place had been avoided by merchants and pilgrims alike, not because any road was wholly impassable, but because some indefinable discouragement seemed to gather at its boundary and turn the heart elsewhere. Yet the stranger crossed that boundary as one crosses a threshold already familiar. At each house he paused, not long enough to knock, but long enough to make those within feel themselves observed with a precision more intimate than sight; and before he reached the inn, shutters had trembled, prayers had failed midway, and one old woman, rising barefoot from her bed, had whispered into the darkness a date no one had spoken aloud in forty years.

The inn door opened before him after a single touch, though the landlord had bolted it with his own hands; and the company within, five men seated about a dying fire, turned their faces toward him with the slow horror of persons who recognize a visitor before memory permits the recognition. The stranger removed his hat. His hair was black, his brow unlined, yet his eyes possessed that remote and ruinous weariness which belongs not to age, but to knowledge endured too long. “I ask little,” he said, and his voice, though soft, seemed to settle upon the room like dust upon a sealed chamber; “a bed until dawn, and the return of what was taken.” No one moved. Then from beneath his coat he drew a small brass button, green with age, and placed it upon the table. The landlord stared at it; his hands opened helplessly at his sides; and from the cellar below there rose a slow exhalation of cold air, bearing with it the faint odor of river mud. At that moment every man understood that the stranger had not come seeking shelter from the night; the night had followed him there, obedient, patient, and hungry!
