At an hour when the night had grown so dense that the very lamps along the single street seemed less to illuminate the darkness than to reveal its substance, a stranger came down from the western ridge and entered the little town of Bellweather Hollow. No coach attended him, no servant followed, and no mark of haste betrayed either fear behind or welcome before; he descended with that composed and dreadful deliberation which belongs to men who have long ago surrendered all lesser impulses to one fixed design. The rain had ceased, yet the roofs continued to drip with a slow persistence, as if the clouds had left behind them some malignant residue; and from the fields beyond the last house there rose a white vapor, creeping low along the earth, folding itself about the stranger’s boots, and seeming, to those who watched from the narrow panes above, not to impede his progress but to accompany it.

The town, removed from all ordinary intercourse by miles of sunken road and a belt of pines whose tops moaned ceaselessly even when no wind could be felt below, had acquired through many years an air of inward corruption, though its outward forms remained almost piously correct. Each door was barred at dusk; each shutter was fastened with care; each family took supper beneath the same cracked portraits and spoke in the same guarded tones, as if language itself were a substance to be measured sparingly, lest some forbidden particle escape. Thus the appearance of an unknown man at such an hour produced no open tumult, for these people were not given to displays, but a colder and more intimate disturbance: candles were pinched out in upper rooms; hands withdrew from curtains; sleepers started awake without knowing why, and listened, breathless, to the soft, exact tread that passed over the stones. It was afterward remarked—though never in daylight—that the stranger turned his head at every house in which a watcher stood concealed, and inclined it faintly, with the mournful courtesy of one acknowledging an appointment already made.

He stopped at last before the house of public lodging, a narrow, timbered structure that had outlived its builder, his sons, and all honest memory of its first misfortune. Within, the landlord sat alone beside a fire that gave more smoke than warmth, counting coins he had counted before and would count again, not from need but from a miserly terror of stillness. When the door opened, he did not look up at once; yet the flame bent sharply away, and the coins, slipping from his fingers, rang upon the table with a sound so clear and accusatory that he covered them with his palm. The stranger entered, removed his gloves, and laid upon the boards a small key, black with age, though no lock in the town was known to fit it. “I require the northern room,” he said, in a voice whose gentleness was more appalling than anger. The landlord rose, then sank back, his cheeks loose and bloodless. Above them, from a chamber long unoccupied, there came three slow knocks—measured, patient, and final!
