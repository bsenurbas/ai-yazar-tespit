It was upon a night of such oppressive stillness that even the rain, descending from a sky without star or moon, seemed less to fall than to accumulate upon the roofs, the fields, and the bent backs of the ancient houses, that the stranger first crossed the little stone bridge and entered the town. No wheel had marked the road before him, no lantern swung in advance of his approach, and no dog, though many were kept in those narrow yards, lifted its voice against him; indeed, a hush preceded him, as though the earth itself, warned by agencies too subtle for mortal apprehension, had withdrawn its customary sounds lest they should profane the solemnity of his arrival. His coat, soaked through and clinging to his meagre frame, gave no evidence of station or occupation, yet there was in the grave inclination of his head, and in the rigid economy of his steps, something that bespoke neither poverty nor fatigue, but rather a terrible inward resolution.

The town, if town it could be called, lay in a hollow between black woods and a motionless mere, and had for many years maintained only the faintest commerce with the outer world; its people were few, intermarried, suspicious, and possessed of that sallow cast which comes not merely from damp air and insufficient sun, but from the constant habitation of thoughts unshared. Behind the seams of their curtains they watched the stranger pass, and although no face was long visible, he appeared to know that he was observed, for once, before the shuttered schoolhouse, he paused and turned his countenance upward with a smile so slight, so bloodlessly composed, that several witnesses afterward denied having seen it, while admitting, with curious reluctance, that from that instant their hearts had beaten with an audible and shameful violence. The clock in the church-tower had long been mute, its mechanism ruined by rust, yet some swore that night they heard, from within its sealed and leaning height, a single iron note.

He came at last to the inn, where a handful of men sat in silence over their cups, speaking only when necessity forced speech from them, and even then with the brevity of prisoners uncertain which word may condemn them. The door opened before he touched it—opened inward with a long complaint of wood and hinge—and the air that entered with him was so cold that the fire sank low, blue at its edges, as though it had recognized an enemy. “I seek a room,” he said; and then, after a pause during which his dark eyes moved from face to face with a dreadful civility, “and perhaps, before morning, a confession.” No man replied. Yet every man knew, in that awful vacancy after his words, that he had brought with him no accusation written on paper, no witness, no blade, no visible instrument of redress; he had brought only memory, and memory, once admitted, is the most merciless visitor of all!
