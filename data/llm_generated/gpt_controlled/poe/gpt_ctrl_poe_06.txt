In the last and most malignant depth of night, when the clouds hung so low above the heath that the roofs of the little town seemed crouched beneath them in bodily apprehension, a stranger appeared upon the eastern road and moved toward the clustered dwellings with a pace neither hurried nor slow, but measured by some inward necessity more exacting than fatigue. No carriage track followed him in the mire; no village cur, though lean shapes stirred behind many a gate, broke the silence with protest; and the road, white in places with frost and black in others with standing water, received his steps without splash or echo, as if it had been prepared, through many lifeless years, for that single visitation. He bore no lantern, yet his face, long and bloodless beneath the shadow of his hat, possessed a pallor by which it appeared faintly visible of itself, and those who glimpsed it from their curtained windows felt at once that they had not looked upon a traveler, but upon the human form of a question they had spent their lives refusing to ask.

The town lay in a basin of hills, cut off from neighboring parishes by flooded meadows to the south and a dense plantation of pines to the north; in summer it was avoided for its feverish vapors, in winter for its roads, and in all seasons for a reputation so old that no one living could say whether it had first arisen from fact, malice, or the more durable substance of fear. Its inhabitants were not numerous, and they had the furtive manners of people who listen more than they speak, who weigh every footfall beyond the door, who leave spaces in conversation around matters no one has forbidden and yet no one names. On this night, as the stranger crossed the empty square, shutters trembled without wind, a candle went out in the baker’s loft, and an old man, waking from a dream he could never afterwards recount, began to weep with his hands pressed hard against his mouth. The stranger paused before the dry fountain at the center of the square, touched the rim with two gloved fingers, and smiled—not with triumph, nor kindness, nor cruelty, but with the weary recognition of one who has reached, at last, the appointed place.

He entered the inn by the side door, though it had been bolted from within; and the few men gathered there, who had been pretending to speak of weather, harvest, and road conditions, turned upon him with faces from which every common expression had been stripped. The fire gave a sudden inward collapse, scattering no sparks, only a low red glare that drew long shadows up the walls; and in that distorted light the stranger seemed at once very old and not old at all, as though age had failed to claim him in the ordinary manner and had left upon him only its ceremonial signs. He requested a room for one night, then placed upon the counter a folded paper, brittle, yellowed, and sealed with wax darkened almost to black. The landlord did not touch it. His eyes fixed upon the seal; his lips parted; and from somewhere beneath the floorboards there rose a faint, deliberate sound, like knuckles answering from below. “You remember it,” said the stranger, softly. At that, the landlord sank into his chair, and every man in the room understood that the town’s long silence had not protected it; it had only taught the past how to speak more terribly!
