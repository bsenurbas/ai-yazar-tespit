It was a little after nine o’clock when the stranger came down the hill road into Wrexford, though the hour seemed later there than it would have seemed in any place with a railway station, a theatre, or even a respectable coffee-room. The town lay in a hollow between chalk ridges, and its lamps, few and yellow, did not illuminate the street so much as mark its existence. There had been rain during the afternoon, and the mud retained, with a kind of exact memory, the prints of carts, boots, sheep, and the narrow wheels of the carrier’s van. Into this record the stranger’s footprints entered abruptly. They were long, close-set, and unusually deep at the heel, suggesting either fatigue or a burden carried with care. He had no visible luggage except a black valise, which he held in his left hand, not as travellers commonly hold such things, but as a chemist might hold a vessel containing some volatile preparation. This detail was noticed by three persons: the ostler at the Crown, Mrs. Peake at the post-office window, and a boy who should have been at home but was instead studying the world through a gap in a stable door.

The man’s appearance was not, in the ordinary sense, remarkable. He was of middle height, thin, and perhaps forty years of age. His coat was dark and wet at the shoulders. His hat, being pulled low, cast his face into shadow, but when he passed under the lamp by the pump one saw a pale cheek and a mouth set with an expression of effort rather than resolution. He asked for lodging in a voice so flat and civil that it checked curiosity more effectively than rudeness would have done. “A room?” said the landlord. “For the night,” said the stranger. “Supper?” “Bread, cheese, and hot water.” “No meat?” “No meat.” This small exchange became, within twenty minutes, a public fact. In an isolated town information travels not by system but by appetite. The poorer sort repeated it as proof that he was mean. The shopkeepers made of it a sign of illness. The vicar’s sister, with that moral confidence often mistaken for intelligence, said that men who refused meat after walking in rain were seldom of settled habits.

Yet the most curious part of the matter was not the stranger himself, but the effect of his arrival upon Wrexford. The town had long believed itself complete. It had its church, its inn, its petty quarrels, its distinctions of pew and payment, its small industry of gossip, and its larger industry of endurance. A new person entered it as a foreign body enters a living organism. At once the social tissues contracted. Windows showed faces and then darkness. Doors opened a little and closed with emphasis. Even the dogs, those conservative philosophers of the village street, barked first at him and then at one another, as though disputing the proper classification of the event. I do not say there was fear. Fear is too definite a word. There was rather that primitive irritation which any closed community feels when confronted by an unknown quantity. A town, like a laboratory culture, reveals its nature most clearly when something not bred within it is introduced from outside. By ten o’clock the stranger’s candle was seen burning in the upper room of the Crown, steady and white behind the blind. By eleven the whole place slept uneasily beneath it, as if a question had been set in the night and left there unanswered.
