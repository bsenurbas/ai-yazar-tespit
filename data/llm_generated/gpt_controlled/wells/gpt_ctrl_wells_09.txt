The stranger arrived at Barley Cross a little before midnight, coming down the long road from the northern heath with the steady pace of a man who had already measured his fatigue and decided it was unimportant. Barley Cross was a small town of brick, chalk, and habit. It lay in a shallow cup of land, with the church at the centre, the inn opposite it, and the cottages spread lower down toward the stream, where the mist collected and the rent was cheaper. The place was commonly described by its inhabitants as quiet. This was an incomplete statement. It was not quiet as a wood may be quiet, full of hidden life, but quiet as a stopped clock is quiet, by reason of limited motion. The stranger wore a dark overcoat, muddy at the hem, and carried a leather portfolio under one arm. He had no umbrella. His face, when he passed beneath the lamp by the horse trough, showed a narrow jaw, tired eyes, and the pale, composed expression of a man used to forming conclusions from insufficient comfort.

He paused outside the closed post office and looked along the street. There was no mystery in this. He was observing. He noted the broken paving near the pump, the refuse in the gutter, the light still burning in the house of Mr. Clegg, who owned the brickfield, and the dark cottages opposite, where his men slept five to a room. He looked also at the schoolhouse roof, patched with tin, and at the churchyard wall, newly pointed and well kept. Such contrasts are not accidental. They are the visible algebra of a community. Barley Cross had chosen, by many small acts of obedience and economy, to keep stone neat for the dead and leave damp plaster for the living. No one in the town would have put it in that way. They would have spoken of custom, rates, weather, and what could not be helped. But a phrase like “what can be helped” is often only the name a society gives to the discomforts it has agreed not to examine.

At the Crown and Ledger he knocked twice. The landlord opened from within, holding a lamp in one hand and his braces in the other. “We are done for the night,” he said. “I need lodging,” said the stranger. “No coach comes this way now.” “I did not come by coach.” “What are you carrying?” “Papers.” “Business papers?” “Records.” The landlord did not like the answer. It was too bare, and it gave him nothing to hang a story upon. Still, the stranger paid in advance and was taken to the room above the kitchen, where the ceiling sloped and the window looked toward the stream. He asked for hot water, a chair with a firm back, and a lamp that smoked less. He would take no beer, no meat, and no company. By the time the house was locked again, Barley Cross had begun to explain him to itself. Mrs. Clegg said he was from the county. The ostler thought him a ruined clerk. The postmistress, who had seen the portfolio, believed he had come about accounts. None of these views had foundation, but they spread because each was useful to someone. Above the kitchen, the stranger’s lamp burned on, small and steady. Below it the town slept with that uneasy feeling which comes when a familiar room is entered by someone who does not admire the furniture.
