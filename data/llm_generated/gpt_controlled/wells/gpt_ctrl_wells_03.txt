The stranger reached Low Mardle at about eleven on a wet September night, when the last of the village lights had withdrawn into the upper rooms and the main street had the deserted appearance of a place already abandoned by history. Low Mardle was not poor in the dramatic sense. It had no ruins worth naming, no wild legend that any educated person would credit, and no visible misery beyond the usual English arrangements of bad drainage, low wages, and respectability. Its houses stood shoulder to shoulder along the road, each with a strip of garden in front and a complicated moral universe behind the curtains. The stranger came from the direction of the downs. His boots were muddy to the ankle, and his coat had dried stiff in some places and shone darkly in others. He carried a square tin box, not large, but held close to his side with a care disproportionate to its size. This was the first point that impressed itself upon the village mind, though at that hour the village mind consisted chiefly of a sleepless widow, a publican with indigestion, and a policeman who had no great talent for observation but a useful suspicion of movement after ten.

The man halted at the pump in the middle of the street and looked about him. He did this slowly and without any theatrical gesture. He observed the line of the roofs, the position of the inn, the telegraph wire crossing the road, and the ditch into which the rainwater ran with a faint chemical smell from the tannery beyond the mill. It is probable, from what was afterwards known of him, that he was forming some first estimate of the place. A town may be read, if one has patience, almost as plainly as a specimen under a lens. Its trades, its fears, its habits of obedience, and even its scale of imagination leave marks. Low Mardle had the marks of a community that had been trained by distance to think small thoughts intensely. The railway had passed it by. The motor coach came only twice a week. Men worked, women economised, children were corrected, and every novelty was treated first as an inconvenience and then as an offence. The stranger, standing there in the rain, was not yet a person to them. He was a disturbance in the experiment.

At the White Hart, he knocked three times before the landlord opened. “Too late for travellers,” said the landlord. “I am here,” said the stranger. “That does not answer me.” “It is the only answer I have.” The landlord stared at him, then at the tin box. “What’s in that?” “Instruments.” “Musical?” “No.” The answer was plain and not discourteous, but plain answers are often the most alarming to people accustomed to evasions. He was given the small room over the yard, the one with the cracked basin and the view of the slaughterhouse roof. He asked for hot water, a candle, and a table that did not wobble. He refused beer. He refused company. Within half an hour Low Mardle had given him three histories and two occupations, none of them compatible, all of them confidently held. The widow thought him a medical man. The publican’s wife thought him ruined. The policeman thought him foreign, by which he meant unclassified. In truth, he had done very little. He had arrived, looked carefully, and spoken with economy. Yet in a closed place those actions are enough. By midnight his candle burned steadily above the yard, and the town below, which had long mistaken stillness for order, began for the first time in many years to feel the pressure of an unknown fact.
