The stranger entered Coldmere at eleven o’clock, by the road that came over the common and dipped suddenly between two hedges into the town. Coldmere was a place of some age and little movement. It had a market square too large for its trade, a church too large for its congregation, and an inn whose painted sign swung with a noise that seemed, at that hour, almost an announcement. The railway had missed the town by six miles, and the canal by three, and these omissions had left upon the inhabitants a settled expression of being wronged by progress while remaining morally superior to it. The stranger came on foot. His coat was wet across the shoulders, and the lower part of his trousers was stained with the pale mud of the common. He carried a black dispatch box, and though it was not large, he shifted it from one hand to the other with the care of a man handling something more important than clothing or food. He was first seen by the watchman of the malt house, who later said the man had “a measuring look.” This was not a bad phrase for it. He did not glance about as a timid traveller might. He inspected.

At the edge of the square he stopped beneath the lamp and turned slowly from one side to the other. He noted the empty stalls, the cracked basin of the pump, the dark windows above the baker’s shop, the better houses facing the church, and the poorer cottages in the lane beyond the tannery, where the smell lay heavy even after rain. It is a mistake to suppose that such details are only picturesque. They are a kind of public confession. A town declares its values in brick, drainage, light, and distance. In Coldmere, the tradesmen lived in sight of the church clock, the labourers lived downwind of the vats, and the old almshouses stood where visitors might admire them without being troubled by the people inside. The stranger appeared to take in these facts with no visible emotion. Perhaps he had seen too many such places to be surprised. England, in those years, was full of small communities that praised thrift while wasting human strength, feared disorder while tolerating decay, and called their isolation character because no better word had been offered to them.

He knocked at the Three Kings, and after some delay a bolt was drawn. The landlord opened the door only wide enough to show his face and the yellow light behind him. “Too late,” he said. “For supper, perhaps,” said the stranger. “Not for a bed.” “Where from?” “Across the common.” “No one crosses it at night.” “That statement is now inaccurate.” The landlord frowned, not because the answer was rude, but because it was precise. Precision has an unfriendly sound to people who live by approximation. He looked at the dispatch box. “Commercial line?” “No.” “Legal?” “No.” “Then what are you?” “A student of causes.” This reply, once repeated, did him considerable harm in Coldmere. It suggested education without usefulness, inquiry without permission, and judgment without local standing. Still, he paid in advance, and the landlord gave him the small room above the taproom, with a sloping floor and a window that rattled in the wind. The stranger asked for hot water, ink, and a lamp. He declined beer. He declined meat. By midnight, the square was dark again, but not at rest. The watchman told the maltster. The maltster told his wife. His wife, who had never lacked material for interpretation, said the man had come to count something that ought not to be counted. Above the inn door his lamp remained alight, steady and unblinking, while Coldmere slept beneath it with the first faint discomfort of a place about to be explained.
