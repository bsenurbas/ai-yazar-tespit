The stranger came into Fallowby at ten minutes past eleven, by the road that descended from the old chalk pits and ended, with an air of apology, beside the blacksmith’s shed. Fallowby was a town only by courtesy. It had a church, an inn, a shop that sold bacon, candles, bootlaces, and moral judgments, and a row of cottages whose windows looked upon the road with the steady suspicion of animals in burrows. Beyond it lay marshland, flat and shining in the dark, and beyond that the railway, which had once been proposed and then placed elsewhere by men in London who had never heard of Fallowby again. The stranger walked without haste. His coat was dark, his collar turned up, and his hands were gloved, though the night was not especially cold. In one hand he carried a cylindrical parcel wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string at intervals so precise that young Pritchard, who saw him first from the loft above the stable, afterwards said it looked less like luggage than a specimen.

He stopped at the milestone and studied the street. The action was simple, but it had a quality that made it memorable. Most travellers entering a strange town look for warmth, food, or a human face. This man looked first at gradients, drains, chimneys, rooflines, and the set of the doors against the wind. He noticed, no doubt, the ditch water standing green beneath the bridge, the cracked glass in the schoolroom window, the ash heaps behind the cottages, and the fact that the only bright lamp burned not at the inn but in the counting-house of Mr. Voss, the mill owner. These details would have seemed trivial to Fallowby. They were not trivial. A settlement is a diagram of power if one knows how to read it. Here was a place where money sat awake while labour slept early, where damp rose through walls and was called Providence, where children coughed in winter and the doctor blamed their mothers’ housekeeping. The stranger, pausing under the weak lamp, appeared to take all this in with the detached interest of a man collecting conditions before an experiment.

At the Bell and Plough, he knocked once. The landlord opened after a long delay, wearing boots but no coat, which suggested that he had not been asleep. “You’re late,” he said. “The road was longer than reported,” said the stranger. “Reported by whom?” “By those who had not walked it.” This answer was repeated in Fallowby for three days, each time with a different tone and a darker meaning. The landlord looked at the oilcloth parcel. “What have you there?” “Apparatus.” “For trade?” “For inquiry.” The word was not liked. It was too large for the hour and too cold for the firelit passage. Still, a room was given to him because money, when placed on a table, has an influence superior to local dislike. He asked for hot water, a lamp, and vinegar. He refused supper, except for bread. By midnight, the news had moved through the town by methods no less efficient for being unofficial. Mrs. Voss called him a government man. The blacksmith said he was ill. The chapel steward thought him proud. In truth, he had done little more than arrive and observe. But observation is never innocent in a place that depends upon not being examined. His lamp burned late in the upper window, and Fallowby slept beneath it with the uneasy sensation of a creature whose covering stone has been lifted.
