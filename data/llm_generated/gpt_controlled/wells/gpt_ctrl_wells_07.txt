The stranger entered Nether Hobb at half past eleven, by the east road, which was little more than a cart track after the rain. The town lay in a hollow of pasture and clay, with one inn, two chapels, a mill, and a row of small houses pressed together as though warmth were a public resource. It had no railway. The nearest station was nine miles away, and this inconvenience had been converted, over many years, into a local article of pride. The people of Nether Hobb said they were spared the hurry of modern life, when in fact they were spared its opportunities. The stranger came walking steadily, with his hat low and his coat fastened tight across the chest. He carried a canvas satchel that was not large, but he kept one hand upon it as he walked, as though reassured by its presence. His boots made little sound in the mud. Only the wheelwright’s daughter saw him first, from an upper window, and she later stated that he looked less like a man seeking shelter than a man who had found the precise place he meant to reach.

He stopped at the bridge beside the mill pond and looked down at the black water. Then he turned and studied the town. This observation lasted perhaps a minute. It was not idle curiosity. He noted the low cottages near the stream, the better houses on higher ground, the broken pump, the narrow schoolroom, the mill with its lit office window, and the silent bulk of the owner’s house beyond the yew hedge. These things, to the inhabitants, were merely Nether Hobb. To a more analytic mind they formed a plain statement. The poor lived where the damp rose. The children learned in a room smaller than the stable of the mill owner. Public works decayed where private profit stood in good repair. No sermon could have described the town’s morals more exactly than its drainage. The stranger seemed to read this evidence without surprise. Perhaps he had seen the same experiment repeated elsewhere, with only the names altered and the mud of a slightly different composition.

At the Swan, he found the door locked but a lamp still burning inside. He knocked with the handle of his stick. After some delay the landlord appeared, holding a candle and wearing the injured expression of a man disturbed in his rights. “We are shut,” he said. “I need a bed,” said the stranger. “Where from?” “From Lanton.” “That’s a long walk.” “Longer in this weather.” “Business?” The stranger looked past him into the passage, where the air smelled of beer, coal smoke, and damp wool. “Inquiry,” he said. The landlord disliked the word, though he could not have explained why. It did not belong to trade, illness, family duty, or any other accepted reason for movement. Still, the stranger paid in advance and was shown to the room above the yard. He asked for hot water, a clean glass, and a table. He refused supper except for bread. By midnight, Nether Hobb had begun its work upon him. The mill clerk said he was from the county office. The chapel keeper said he had the look of a man with dangerous opinions. The landlord’s wife said he was too pale to be harmless. None of this was knowledge, but in Nether Hobb, as in many places, opinion was a cheaper substitute and more widely used. Above the yard his lamp burned with a steady light, and the town slept below it with the discomfort of something measured before it understood the instrument.
