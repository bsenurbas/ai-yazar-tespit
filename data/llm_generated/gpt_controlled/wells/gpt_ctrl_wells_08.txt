The stranger came to Ashwell Fen at twenty minutes before midnight, along the raised road that crossed the marsh and entered the town beside the coal shed. It was a poor road and a poorer town, though the inhabitants used neither word if they could avoid it. Ashwell Fen had one inn, a chapel with a cracked bell, a row of brick cottages, and a drainage office that had once promised improvement and now served chiefly to store old forms. The nearest railway station was eight miles away, and this fact had produced in the people a curious mixture of resentment and pride. They disliked being forgotten, but they also distrusted every sign that they had been remembered. The stranger walked with an even step, though his clothes showed the road had treated him badly. His trousers were wet to the knee. His coat was buttoned high. Under one arm he carried a long black case with iron corners, and in his other hand a notebook, which he had not troubled to put away despite the rain.

He stopped where the road widened by the cattle trough and looked at the town. This act was afterwards described as suspicious, but it was only careful. He observed the lamps, the level of the ditch water, the direction of the wind, the smell from the tallow works, and the way the cottages leaned slightly away from the marsh as though the ground itself were tired of supporting them. Such details are more informative than local histories. A place explains itself in its repairs, its drains, its windows, and the distribution of dry rooms among wet families. In Ashwell Fen the manager of the tallow works lived on the only rise of ground, in a square house with a gravel path and two lamps at the gate. The labourers lived where the fog collected first. This was called custom. In a more exact language it would have been called an experiment conducted for several generations upon the lungs of the poor. The stranger, standing under the weak public lamp, appeared to read these facts without surprise and without pity. Pity is often only a visitor’s form of self-congratulation. Observation, though colder, may in the end be more useful.

At the Fenman’s Rest he knocked on the door and waited. The landlord opened after a minute, carrying a lamp with a smoked chimney. “We are closed,” he said. “I require a room,” said the stranger. “You have come late.” “The marsh road is slow at night.” “What’s in the case?” “Instruments.” “For measuring land?” “For measuring conditions.” The landlord stared at him, for this answer seemed to include more of Ashwell Fen than a stranger had any right to include. Still, money was produced, and money has a plainness that often succeeds where manners fail. The stranger was given a room above the back parlour, with a narrow bed, a loose window catch, and a view over the yard where empty barrels stood shining in the rain. He asked for hot water, paper, and a candle that would last until morning. He refused beer. He refused cooked food. Within half an hour, his arrival had passed from the inn to the cottages, losing accuracy and gaining force at every door. He was said to be an inspector, a surveyor, a creditor, a doctor, and a man from London with power to close the works. None of these statements rested on evidence, but evidence is a slow traveller where fear has already found the road. By one o’clock his candle still burned in the upper window, steady and pale, and Ashwell Fen slept beneath it with the uneasy feeling that its ordinary life had become, without warning, material for examination.
