The stranger entered Brackenmere at a quarter to twelve, when the rain had passed eastward and left the roofs shining under a thin and watery moon. Brackenmere was a small town of one street, one inn, one chapel, and many opinions. It stood in a fold of the moors, twelve miles from the nearest station, and had acquired from that accident of geography a confidence quite out of proportion to its importance. The inhabitants had the habit, common to remote communities, of mistaking repetition for truth. What had happened often was considered natural; what had not happened was considered improper. The stranger came by the quarry road, walking with a measured step, as though he had calculated the remaining distance and found no reason to hurry. He wore a long grey coat, stained at the hem with clay, and carried under his arm a flat wooden case bound with brass. It was not heavy, for he did not stoop, but he held it carefully, with the attentive pressure one gives to a thing whose value is not visible to others.

At the market cross he stopped and looked about him. This pause, later much discussed, lasted no more than half a minute. He examined the inn sign, the locked door of the grocer’s shop, the broken gutter by the apothecary’s window, and the line of smoke still rising from the bakehouse chimney. To a trained eye these were not separate details. They were data. The hour at which bread was baked, the state of public repair, the number of lamps still burning, the absence of wheeled traffic, all gave a rough but useful measure of the town’s habits and resources. Brackenmere, if viewed in this way, was not a quaint old place but a small social mechanism running with much friction and little power. Its people were poor without admitting poverty, proud without possessing authority, and observant without being accurate. They watched everything, but understood only what confirmed their prior view of the world. The stranger, unfortunately for his peace, confirmed nothing.

He knocked at the Red Lion, and after some delay a window opened above. “We are closed,” said a woman’s voice. “I require a room,” he replied. “At this hour?” “Especially at this hour.” There was a silence, then the sound of bolts and a chain. The landlord appeared with a candle in one hand and a poker in the other, which showed both caution and a limited imagination. “Where from?” he asked. “North.” “Where bound?” “That depends.” “On what?” “On what I find here.” The landlord did not like this answer, though there was nothing in it that a reasonable person could call threatening. A bed was given to him at the back of the house, near the loft stairs, where the plaster was damp and the window looked upon a yard of barrels. He asked for hot water, paper, and a lamp with a clean chimney. He took no ale. He asked no local questions. This last fact did more to disturb Brackenmere than any number of inquiries would have done. By morning it was said he had come to buy land, expose fraud, spread disease, inspect mines, or claim an inheritance. Each theory told more about the speaker than about the man. At midnight, while his lamp burned steadily behind the blind, the town lay around him like a closed hand, not yet knowing whether it had grasped a coin, a seed, or a spark.
