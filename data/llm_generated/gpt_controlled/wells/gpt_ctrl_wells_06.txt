The stranger reached Mallow End shortly before midnight, on a night of low clouds and wet hedgerows, when the town had drawn itself in like a snail and left only the public house and the baker’s oven to prove that human activity had not ceased altogether. Mallow End stood in a shallow valley, four miles from the main road and seven from the nearest railway station. Its people spoke of this distance as if it were a virtue. They said it kept out trouble, by which they meant competition, new manners, and questions that had not already been answered by their fathers. The stranger came on foot from the western lane. He was bareheaded despite the rain, and his hair lay flat against his brow. His clothes were ordinary but badly treated by weather, and he carried a narrow metal case suspended from a strap across his shoulder. The case was the first thing noticed. It struck softly against his side as he walked, not with the loose clatter of tools, but with a dense and regular sound, as if its contents had been packed with care.

He stopped beneath the lamp outside the corn merchant’s office and examined the street. There was nothing romantic in the act. He did not peer about him like a criminal, nor did he show the vague helplessness of a lost traveller. He looked, measured, compared. He observed the open gutter, the slope of the road toward the mill pond, the high wall of the rectory garden, the poor glazing of the cottages, and the curious fact that the poorest houses stood lowest, where the damp settled most thickly. It is often said by comfortable people that such things happen naturally. They do not. A town, like a bone or a shell, records the pressures under which it has grown. Mallow End had been shaped by rent, habit, and a fear of offending those who owned the larger fields. Its geography was not innocent. The stranger appeared to understand this before he had spoken to a soul.

At the Blue Hart he found the door bolted but not barred. A line of light showed beneath it. He knocked twice, then waited. The landlord opened with the expression of a man who believes hospitality should end at ten. “What do you want?” he asked. “A room.” “At this hour?” “At this hour.” “Where have you come from?” “From beyond Harrow Fen.” “On foot?” “As you see.” The landlord looked past him into the rain, then at the metal case. “Commercial?” “Scientific.” This answer, though exact, was a mistake in Mallow End. It had the chill of education and the smell of institutions. A commercial man could be understood, cheated, or avoided. A scientific man suggested reports, measurements, and some possible interference with established comforts. He was given the attic room, because it was empty and because the money he put down was real. He asked for hot water, a basin, and a table near the window. He refused ale. He declined conversation. By midnight, three separate accounts of him had already begun their journey through the town. He was said to be an inspector, a foreign manufacturer, and a doctor of unusual diseases. None of these guesses had much evidence, but evidence is less valued in small places than agreement. Above them all his lamp burned steadily, and below it Mallow End slept with the troubled dignity of a community that had been looked at too closely.
