The stranger arrived at Elmbury by the northern road at half past ten, when the last cart had gone and the last shopkeeper had put out his lamp. Elmbury was not marked on many maps, and on those that did include it the print was small, as though the surveyor had hesitated to admit its full existence. It stood on a wet plain, five miles from the nearest railway, with a low church tower, a brewery chimney, and one principal street that appeared to have been made less for traffic than for the preservation of local opinion. The stranger came on foot. His coat was buttoned to the throat, his boots were white with chalk at the edges, and in his right hand he carried a small leather case. There was no drama in his arrival. He did not stagger. He did not look behind him. He simply entered the town, paused under the lamp by the weighing office, and looked at the houses with the close attention of a man observing not shelter but evidence.

The first person to speak to him was old Tapp, who kept the turnpike cottage and supplemented his income by knowing everyone’s business. “You’re late on the road,” he said. “I am,” said the stranger. “For the inn?” “For a bed.” “You’ll find one at the King’s Arms, if Wilmer is awake.” “He will wake.” This was said without impatience, and Tapp afterwards repeated it as if it had contained a threat. In fact it contained only a prediction. The stranger had observed the red crack of firelight under the inn door and the faint movement of a shadow across the lower blind. His conclusion was sound. But in Elmbury, where most judgments rested on habit, a sound conclusion reached by observation had the appearance of insolence. By the time he had crossed the street, two women had seen him from behind curtains, a groom had followed him with his eyes from the stable yard, and the butcher’s apprentice, lying awake above the shop, had decided that no honest man carried so small a case on so long a road.

There is a common error that small towns are simple. They are often more complex than cities, because their forces act at shorter range. In a city a man may be unknown and therefore free. In Elmbury, to be unknown was to be an interruption of the whole social order. The stranger had not merely asked for lodging; he had disturbed a delicate system of rank, suspicion, debt, deference, and inherited dislike. His wet footprints on the inn floor were examined next morning with almost clerical seriousness. His refusal of beer was taken as evidence of illness, foreignness, poverty, pride, and secret purpose, according to the temperament of the speaker. Yet there was nothing in him, so far as any witness could state, except pallor, restraint, and an unusual habit of looking carefully at ordinary things. He looked at the cracked plaster, the smoky lamp, the bread set before him, and the landlord’s swollen hands. It may be that he saw, in those small facts, more of Elmbury than Elmbury had ever seen of itself. By midnight his candle burned in the back room above the archway, and below it the town resumed its sleep, though not its peace.
