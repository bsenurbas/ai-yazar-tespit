I reached Pellingford-on-Mire a little before ten, after a journey made irksome by rain, a lame horse, and the settled ill humour of a driver who had twice lost the road and once his temper. My errand was connected with a disputed annuity, and I had brought with me a packet of memoranda, two letters of instruction, and a strong desire for supper. The town, when at last we entered it, proved to be no more than a tight knot of houses set about a triangular green, with the church on the rise, the inn below it, and a row of shops whose signs creaked in the wet wind. I observed that, though the hour was not advanced, every blind had been drawn, and the street lay empty from end to end. This interested me. In a country town, bad weather keeps people indoors; but it does not make them extinguish curiosity. Here curiosity had been suppressed. That was a more serious matter.

As I stood beneath the lamp of the White Swan, shaking the rain from my coat, a stranger came into the green by the lane beside the churchyard wall. He walked slowly, but not from fatigue. His step was careful, measured, and silent where the stones allowed it. He wore a dark travelling coat, a grey scarf, and gloves which had once been fine but were now split across the knuckles. In his left hand he carried a leather document case, and in his right a short cane with a silver top. “Inn?” asked the landlord, appearing at my shoulder. “For a few minutes,” said the stranger. “Room?” “No.” “Food?” “No.” “Then what?” “A message delivered before witnesses.” The landlord stiffened. I saw it plainly, for the lamp was between us. The stranger’s boots gave me my next fact: the mud upon them was yellow clay, though the road from the west, by which he had seemed to come, was black and peaty. Therefore he had crossed the fields from the old quarry road and entered by the church lane to avoid the main street. A man does not choose wet fields at night for comfort. He chooses them for concealment, speed, or surprise.

“Your name, sir?” said the landlord. “Fane.” “I have heard no such name.” “You have heard the one I used before.” “Not in this house.” “In this house least of all.” At that, a murmur passed from the taproom behind us, where three men sat close to the fire and pretended to study their mugs. One of them, a heavy fellow in a brown coat, half rose, then sat again. The stranger noticed him, but did not turn his head. This restraint was revealing. An angry man faces his enemy; a prepared man saves movement until it has value. I put the facts together in their proper order. He had come by a hidden route, carried papers of weight, declined all ordinary comforts, and spoke to the innkeeper as to a participant rather than a servant. The affair was old, local, and documentary. Presently the church clock struck ten. The stranger opened his case, removed a sealed envelope, and placed it on the table beside the landlord’s candle. “Take it to Mr. Calder,” he said. “He will not receive it.” “He will when he sees the hand.” “And if he refuses?” “Then read it aloud.” The landlord looked toward the brown-coated man by the fire. The man looked away. That was enough. I had come to Pellingford-on-Mire to examine annuities; but before my supper was ordered, I knew that another account, long hidden and more perilous than mine, had been presented for settlement.
