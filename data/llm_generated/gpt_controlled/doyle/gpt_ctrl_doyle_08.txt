I came upon Grimshaw Holt at nine o’clock on a raw March evening, after a journey which had shaken both my bones and my temper over twelve miles of broken turnpike. My commission was simple: I had been retained to compare a set of shipping receipts with the books of a deceased maltster, and I expected no disturbance beyond cold mutton and provincial obstinacy. The town lay in a shallow basin, with one road in and, so far as I could learn, the same road out. A few gas lamps burned along the street, their light falling upon wet cobbles, shuttered shopfronts, a pump, and the square bulk of the Crown Posting House. Before I left the chaise, I noticed that the inn door stood open though no servant came out. This was odd. In small towns, an arrival is an event; a traveller is met before he knocks. Therefore either the household was neglectful, or its attention had been drawn elsewhere.

The answer came at once. A man stood beneath the lamp beside the pump, as still as the post itself. He was not of the town. His coat was too well cut, his collar too clean, and his hat bore the slight gloss of London ware, though the brim was bent and his right cuff had been torn. In one hand he held a small tin dispatch box. In the other, a folded paper, damp at the edges. “You seek the inn?” asked the ostler from the arch. “No.” “The malt house?” “No.” “Then what?” “A door that was closed to me.” The ostler took one backward step. I observed this with care, for fear is often more informative in the listener than anger in the speaker. The stranger then looked from the inn to the counting-house opposite, then to the upper window above it. His order of inspection was exact. Shelter first dismissed, business premises considered, private quarters confirmed. I reasoned that he had not come for lodging, nor trade, but for a personal interview tied to commercial papers.

The landlord appeared then, wiping his hands upon his apron, though they were already clean. “Name?” he said. “Renn.” “I know no Renn.” “You knew another name.” “Not here.” “Here most of all.” The exchange was short, but it opened the matter like a key in a lock. The man had returned under a name which was either changed or incomplete; the landlord knew enough to deny too quickly; and the dispatch box, held close even in conversation, contained the proof upon which the visit depended. I set these facts in their order and reached a plain conclusion: Grimshaw Holt had kept some compact, and this wet, weary stranger had come at the hour when secrecy is weakest. A lamp was lit in the upper window across the street. The stranger saw it, folded the damp paper once, and placed it within his breast. “Now,” he said. No one answered. He crossed the cobbles alone, and every shutter in the town seemed, in that instant, to have an eye behind it.
