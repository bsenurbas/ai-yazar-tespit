I had occasion, in the autumn of ’87, to visit the small market town of Wrexford, which lies some twenty miles beyond the nearest railway junction and is approached, after dusk, by a road so narrow and ill-kept that my hired trap more than once lurched perilously toward the ditch. I had been summoned there upon a matter of inheritance, my profession at that time being connected with the examination of family papers, and I carried in my valise several letters whose contents had already given me reason to think that the inhabitants of Wrexford were not so simple as their quiet chimneys and shuttered shops might suggest. The night was clear, though without moon, and the lamps set at intervals along the high street showed me a place of sober aspect: a church tower, a posting inn, a row of brick-fronted houses, and beyond them the black outline of fields rising toward the downs. I had just stepped down before the Red Hart when a second conveyance came rattling in from the north road at a pace quite unsuitable to the stones, and from it descended a tall stranger in a dark travelling coat, who paused upon the threshold not as a man relieved to find shelter, but as one who measures an enemy’s ground before crossing it.

“Room?” said the landlord, coming out with a candle in his hand. “If it is quiet,” answered the stranger. “We have little else, sir.” “That remains to be seen.” The words were spoken mildly, yet I observed that the man kept his gloved hand upon the leather case at his side and that his eyes moved, not vaguely, but in a fixed succession: signboard, stable-door, upper windows, inn passage, landlord’s face. This interested me. A common traveller looks first for comfort; a commercial gentleman for food; a fugitive for concealment. This man looked for exits. I formed the conclusion, therefore, that he either expected to be followed or had come to Wrexford upon business in which surprise would be an advantage. His boots were splashed with pale clay, not the red mire of our southern road, and his collar bore a faint dusting such as one receives in a railway carriage. Thus he had not driven the whole distance, but had left the train at some nearer point and hired privately beyond it. “Your name, sir?” asked the landlord, opening the register. “Grant.” “Christian name?” “Put Edward.” “You are expected?” “Not by the man who matters.” The landlord looked up sharply, but the stranger had already turned away.

I confess that, being of an orderly habit of mind, I found the incident more engaging than my supper. There was nothing theatrical in the man’s behaviour, and that, to my thinking, made it the more significant. A person who wishes to alarm a village speaks loudly, stamps about, and orders brandy; a person engaged upon a serious errand saves both breath and motion. When, a quarter of an hour later, I saw him standing alone in the inn yard with his face turned toward the old solicitor’s house opposite the church, several scattered facts placed themselves in proper relation. The private arrival, the assumed or incomplete name, the care with which he surveyed the inn, and the remark about being expected by one man rather than another all pointed to a deliberate interview, late in hour and private in character. “You had best come in, sir,” said the ostler from the stable. “Cold out.” “Not too cold for waiting.” “For whom?” “For a light in that upper window.” I followed his glance. At that instant, behind the blind of the solicitor’s room, a lamp sprang up, steady and yellow. The stranger took one step forward, no more, and I saw upon his face not fear, but confirmation. Whatever had brought him to that remote town at such an hour, the first move had been made, and I, who had come only to read old papers, understood that before morning Wrexford would be required to answer for something long kept out of sight.
