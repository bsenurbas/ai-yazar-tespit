I first saw Latchford Magna in the closing days of February, when a raw east wind swept across the flat pastures and sent the rain in thin, slanting lines against the windows of our coach. I had been engaged to examine the accounts of a wool merchant recently deceased, and my expectations were limited to bad ink, swollen ledgers, and the dull company of provincial executors. The town lay in a hollow, reached by a single road that dipped between two banks of leafless thorn and then opened suddenly upon a small square, where the inn, the church, the saddler’s shop, and a row of narrow houses stood as close together as books upon a shelf. I observed, before I had even descended, that several curtains stirred and fell back again. This was not curiosity alone. Curiosity lingers. These faces appeared and vanished. The distinction is useful, for it separates common interest from anxiety. I had just formed this opinion when a man stepped out from the shadow of the market cross and raised his hand to stop the coach.

“Too late for passengers,” cried the driver. “I am not a passenger,” said the man. “Then stand clear.” “I need the inn.” “It is before you.” The stranger turned his head, not toward the sign, but toward the upper windows, and I saw by the lamp over the archway that he was a gentleman of perhaps five-and-thirty, though travel and weather had dealt roughly with him. His coat was dark, his linen clean, his gloves ruined by mud, and his boots bore the white dust of the turnpike over a lower layer of black marsh soil. These details were not without value. He had not come directly by the road on which he now stood. He had walked part of the way across wet ground, then taken the turnpike near the town. Why should a man do that at night? To avoid the toll house, the main gate, or some person stationed upon the common road. He carried a square japanned box beneath his arm, and though his hat dripped rain upon his collar, he made no effort to brush it away. A man indifferent to discomfort is often intent upon purpose. “Name?” asked the landlord, who had come out with a lantern. “Harcourt.” “We have no room kept.” “I did not ask you to keep one.” “Then what do you ask?” “Ten minutes and a locked door.”

The landlord’s face changed at once, and that small movement told me more than any speech. He knew either the man or the matter. Behind him, in the glow of the passage, a maid dropped a tray with a clatter that seemed enormous in the tight little square. The stranger did not start. He looked only at the clock above the taproom mantel, visible through the open door. “Fast by four minutes,” he said. “How would you know that?” asked the landlord. “Because I set my watch by the mail at Colchester.” “You came from Colchester?” “No.” “Where from, then?” “From the place he thought I would never leave.” There was a pause after this, and in that pause I heard one shutter close across the square, then another. It seemed to me plain that Latchford Magna had been waiting for this man, though not with welcome. I reasoned thus: the hidden watchers, the landlord’s alarm, the stranger’s careful route, and the box guarded under his arm all pointed to a compact of silence, old enough to have become local habit, yet fragile enough to be broken by one determined visitor. When he passed within, I followed at a distance proper to a guest and close enough for observation. I had come to examine a dead merchant’s accounts; but before my coat was dry, I understood that the true balance due in Latchford Magna was not written in any ledger.
