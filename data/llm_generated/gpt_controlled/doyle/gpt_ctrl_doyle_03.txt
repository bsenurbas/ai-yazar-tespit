My journey to Briarwell was undertaken in the last week of November, when the hedgerows were bare, the roads deep with ruts, and the air had that sharp metallic quality which makes a man draw his collar higher about his ears. I had been engaged to inspect certain title deeds belonging to an elderly widow of the parish, and, having missed the afternoon coach at Amesbury, was compelled to hire a small cart from a carrier who knew the road and disliked it. “Bad going?” said I, as the horse stumbled for the third time. “Bad company,” he answered. “On the road?” “At the end of it.” This reply, delivered without drama, put me upon my guard; for countrymen are not given to ornamental speech when cold, tired, and unpaid. Briarwell itself appeared at length as a cluster of dim lamps about a square, with the church at one end, the inn at the other, and a dozen shuttered houses pressed close together as though the open country beyond had no welcome in it.

I had scarcely set foot before the Crown and Thistle when a man came walking in from the western lane, not hurriedly, but with a measured stride that suggested endurance rather than ease. He was tall, spare, and wrapped in a long coat of dark wool, much stained at the hem. His hat was drawn low, yet I saw enough of his face beneath the lamp to note a firm mouth, sunken cheeks, and eyes which travelled quickly while the rest of him remained composed. “You are late,” said the landlord from the doorway. “I am in time,” said the stranger. “For bed?” “For business.” “Name?” “March.” “Mr. March?” “That will serve.” The landlord hesitated, then stood aside. Now, the exchange was brief, but quite instructive. The stranger gave too little information for an honest guest, yet too calmly for a rogue in flight. He carried neither portmanteau nor parcel, only a flat leather case beneath his arm, held close against his ribs. A man may forget luggage; he does not forget what he values. I therefore set down three points: he had come for a particular interview, he wished to leave no more trace than needful, and the papers in that case were of greater moment than his comfort.

Over supper I watched him without appearing to do so, which is a useful habit in inns, where mirrors, tankards, and windowpanes provide more intelligence than direct staring. He refused meat, took only bread and ale, and sat where he could see both the staircase and the front door. Presently an old woman entered from the kitchen passage and whispered to the landlord. He shook his head. She whispered again. At that the stranger looked up. “Has he sent word?” he asked. “No,” said the landlord. “Then he is afraid.” “Mind your tongue.” “I have minded it for twelve years.” There was no bluster in the answer; indeed, it was spoken almost wearily. But the room altered at once. The carrier, who had brought me there, set down his mug. The old woman crossed herself in a quick, furtive fashion, then seemed ashamed of the action. I reasoned that Mr. March was not unknown in Briarwell, though his arrival had not been desired. When the church clock struck ten, he rose, placed two coins upon the table, and turned to me for the first time. “You are not of this town.” “No.” “Then keep to your papers.” He went out before I could reply. I saw him cross the square toward a narrow house beside the churchyard wall, where one lamp still burned in an upper room. It was then clear to me that the isolation of Briarwell was not merely a matter of roads and distance, but of some old compact among its inhabitants; and the stranger, arriving alone at night with his leather case and his twelve years of silence, had come to break it.
