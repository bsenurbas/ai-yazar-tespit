I reached Edgestone Parva near eleven o’clock, after a journey that had begun with civility at the railway hotel and ended in a hired cart with one lamp, a bad spring, and a driver who answered every question as though it were an accusation. My errand concerned the examination of a packet of lease papers belonging to a retired clergyman, and I had looked for nothing more dramatic than damp rooms and crabbed handwriting. Yet the town, when it appeared below us, had an aspect that invited attention. It lay in a fold of the hills, so closely shut in by dark pasture and stone walls that one might have passed within a quarter mile of it and never guessed at its existence. There was a single street, a low inn with a swinging sign, a chapel, a smithy, and a row of cottages whose windows showed narrow bars of lamplight behind thick curtains. I observed that no dog barked at our arrival. This was curious. In such places dogs give notice before men do. Either the animals had been taken in, or the town had been warned against disturbance.

I had just stepped down before the Bell and Spur when a stranger entered the street from a footpath between two barns. He was a tall man, though he carried himself with a slight stoop, and his clothes were those of a gentleman reduced by hard travel rather than poverty. His collar was clean, his coat brushed, but his boots were scored with chalk and his left glove hung split across the palm. In one hand he held a narrow leather folio. In the other, nothing; yet he kept that empty hand close to his side, as though habit had taught him caution. “You want the inn?” said the landlord, who had come out with a lamp. “For shelter,” said the stranger. “For the night?” “For an hour.” “Name?” “Lorrimer.” “We know no Lorrimer here.” “No. You knew me before that.” The landlord lowered the lamp by an inch. It was a slight movement, but a valuable one. Fear often shows itself not by retreat, but by the wish to see more plainly. The stranger’s manner gave me further material. He did not look about as a traveller looks; he counted the houses with his eyes, paused at the chapel door, and then fixed upon a shuttered building opposite the inn. From this I reasoned that he had studied the place long before he entered it.

“Who sent you?” asked the landlord. “A dead man.” “Speak softer.” “I have spoken softly for fifteen years.” A woman within the passage made a faint sound and put her hand to her mouth. The stranger heard it, but gave no sign. He placed the leather folio upon the table just inside the door and tapped it once with two fingers. “Where is Mr. Vey?” “Gone to bed.” “Then wake him.” “He will not come.” “He will when you say what I carry.” There was no threat in the words, and therefore they had the greater force. I put the facts together in sequence: the hidden approach by the field path, the guarded folio, the landlord’s alarm, the old name left unspoken, and the reference to a dead man. All pointed to an affair buried by agreement rather than forgotten by chance. At that moment a lamp appeared in the upper room of the shuttered house across the way. The stranger turned toward it with the calm air of one whose calculation has met its proof. I had come to Edgestone Parva for leases and signatures; but before my portmanteau had been carried upstairs, I perceived that the town itself was a document, sealed for years, and that this late visitor had brought the knife to open it.
