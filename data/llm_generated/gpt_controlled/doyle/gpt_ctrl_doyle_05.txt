I came to Ashmere Stile on a wet October night, in the year when the new telegraph line had just been carried as far as Banbury, and when many a country town still lived by the rhythm of the coach, the church bell, and the market cart. My errand was professional, though not romantic: a retired naval officer had asked me to inspect a packet of bonds said to be locked in the strongbox of the local bank. The last part of my journey was made in a hired gig over a road so narrow that the hedges brushed both wheels, and when at last the lamps of the town showed ahead, they seemed less like a welcome than a warning. Ashmere Stile consisted of one crooked street, an inn, a church, a smithy, and a row of stone houses whose curtains were drawn tight against the weather. I noticed at once that the street was empty, though it was not yet late enough for honest people to be abed. That fact had meaning. A town does not withdraw indoors without reason. Either news had passed through it, or fear had.

I had just entered the porch of the King’s Arms when a second figure appeared at the far end of the street, walking in from the north road with a small valise in one hand and a cane in the other. He was a man of middle height, dressed in a dark frock coat much too fine for the lane by which he had come, and wearing a silk hat now battered by rain. His face, when the inn lamp caught it, was composed but colourless. “Room?” said the landlord. “Later,” said the stranger. “Supper?” “No.” “Then what do you want?” “A witness.” The word passed through the little hall like a draught under a door. I observed that he did not look at the landlord while speaking, but past him, toward the stair and then toward the public room. This was my first point. He sought not comfort, but persons. My second lay in his boots, for the mud upon them was grey and gritty, not the brown clay of the north road. My third was the valise: it was light, since he swung it without effort, yet he guarded it with the caution of a man carrying papers rather than clothes. From these facts I reasoned that he had not come by the road he appeared to have taken; that his name, when given, would be of little value; and that his business touched some written matter of consequence.

“Name?” asked the landlord, recovering himself. “Merrick.” “Mr. Merrick of where?” “Of no place that concerns you.” “You speak sharp.” “I have travelled far to speak plainly.” At this, an elderly man seated near the fire rose so quickly that his chair struck the boards behind him. “You should not have come back,” he said. “Then you know me.” “I know enough.” “Not yet.” The stranger opened his valise and drew from it a sealed packet bound with faded ribbon. He did not break the seal. He merely held it where the firelight fell upon the writing, and I saw three faces in the room turn suddenly rigid. The matter was now clear in its outline, though not in its particulars. A man absent for years had returned under another aspect; certain inhabitants knew him; and the packet was the instrument by which he meant to disturb a settlement long accepted. Outside, the rain beat steadily upon the inn sign, and beyond the windows the shuttered town of Ashmere Stile lay as quiet as a ledger before audit. Yet I knew, with a firmness grounded not in fancy but in observation, that before the next coach left for Banbury, those closed houses would have yielded up more truth than their owners cared to spare.
