I reached Nether Combe upon a Thursday evening, after a journey so tedious and uncomfortable that I had begun to think the place less a town than a rumour maintained by coachmen. The last eight miles lay over a white chalk road between bare downs, and the wind came across them with a force that set the lamps of our chaise trembling in their sockets. My errand was connected with a valuation of some agricultural machinery, a matter dull enough in itself, though profitable to a young man not yet burdened with reputation. As we descended toward the town, I observed that the houses were gathered close about a single street, with no outlying cottages, no cheerful scatter of farms, and no sign of life save a glimmer at the inn and another at what appeared to be the apothecary’s shop. Such closeness in a country place may spring from thrift, from weather, or from fear of loneliness. In Nether Combe, as I soon perceived, it sprang from habit strengthened by long suspicion.

The coach had not yet drawn up before the Blue Lion when another traveller appeared on foot at the lower end of the street. He came without lantern, though the road behind him was rough, and his pace was so even that I judged him to be neither drunk nor exhausted. A town man, plainly; his coat was well cut, his hat of good beaver, and his gloves too fine for that muddy approach. Yet the left sleeve of his coat was torn near the cuff, and one boot bore a fresh scrape across the toe. “Lost your way?” called the landlord, who had come out to receive me. “No.” “Looking for the inn?” “For a man.” “There are few here.” “One will be enough.” I turned at that, for the answer was quietly spoken, but carried across the street with uncommon distinctness. The stranger stepped into the light, and I saw a face rather pale than weak, with a narrow jaw and eyes that rested upon each object only long enough to dismiss it. He looked first at the inn sign, then at the church clock, then at the closed shutters opposite. His sequence was not idle. The sign told him his place, the clock his hour, and the shutters whether he had been observed.

“Your name, sir?” asked the landlord. “Bale.” “From London?” “From the train.” “There is no station.” “Not within walking distance for most men.” That reply completed the picture. He had left the railway before the nearest ordinary stop, had come across country, had met with some mishap upon the road, and had still arrived by a fixed hour. Such conduct is not random. It belongs to a man who either wishes to avoid notice or wishes to give another person no warning of his approach. While I considered this, a boy came running from the apothecary’s shop and stopped dead at sight of him. “Well?” said the stranger. The boy swallowed. “He said you would not come.” “Then he has lost his wager.” “He said you were dead.” “He was misinformed.” The landlord made a small sound in his throat, and at once the door behind him opened wider, showing three faces within the taproom, all turned toward the visitor. The stranger took off his gloves with care, folded them once, and placed them inside his coat. “Which house?” he asked. No one answered. After a moment, the boy pointed toward the far end of the street, where a narrow brick dwelling stood beside the locked gate of a yard. The stranger nodded, as though this confirmed all he had reasoned out before his arrival, and walked toward it alone. I had come to Nether Combe to price engines and threshing parts; but before I had crossed the inn threshold, I knew that the more valuable machinery in that town was human, old, hidden, and already in motion.
