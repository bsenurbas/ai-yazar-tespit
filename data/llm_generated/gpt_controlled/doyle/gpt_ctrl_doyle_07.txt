I arrived at Millbeck Thorpe upon the evening of Michaelmas Monday, having been sent down by my employer to inspect a disputed inventory of silver plate belonging to a family whose fortunes had declined with admirable steadiness for two generations. The town stood at the end of a branch road which appeared to have been made for the convenience of no one in particular, since it wound for six miles through wet meadows, crossed a stone bridge barely wide enough for our wheels, and ended in a market square so small that the coach seemed an intrusion. There was an inn, a church, a watchmaker’s shop, and perhaps twenty houses, all built low against the wind. I remember that the lamps burned with an unsteady yellow light, and that the only sound, when the coachman set me down, was the creak of the sign above the door of the Barley Sheaf. Such silence in a town is seldom empty. It has weight. It means either that nothing has happened there for years, or that something has happened very recently and no man wishes to be first to speak of it.

I was still paying the coachman when a stranger came out of the darkness beyond the bridge and walked straight into the square. He wore a travelling cloak buttoned to the throat, though the evening was not bitter, and he carried a narrow wooden case in his right hand. His hat was of London make, his boots were country-worn, and there was a clean tear in the cloth at his shoulder, as though it had caught upon a nail or thorn not half an hour before. “Inn open?” he asked. “It is,” said the landlord. “Private room?” “For supper?” “For speech.” “With whom?” “With the man who has the key.” The landlord lowered his candle a little, and the flame showed his face gone flat and cautious. That was my first observation. My second was the stranger’s hand, which never shifted upon the case. My third was the mud upon his boots, for it was dark river silt, not the pale road mud through which I had just driven. From these points I reasoned that he had not used the coach road, that he had crossed by the lower ford, and that he had come by design to avoid some pair of eyes stationed near the bridge.

“Your name?” said the landlord. “Vale.” “Full name?” “Enough for tonight.” “You have business here?” “Old business.” At that phrase a woman in the passage drew in her breath so sharply that all in the room heard it. The stranger turned toward her. “You remember me?” “No.” “That is well said.” He stepped inside, and I followed, for my papers and valise had already been carried to a chamber above. In the taproom were three farmers, a post-boy, and a grey-haired man in black broadcloth who sat with his back to the fire and his hands folded over a silver-headed stick. The stranger saw him at once. No glance passed between them which could be called greeting, yet each had the air of one who finds a calculation confirmed. I put the matter in order thus: the town had been warned of a possible arrival; the landlord feared to name the expected party; the man in black was the holder, literal or otherwise, of the key; and the wooden case contained the instrument by which Mr. Vale intended to compel an interview. “Well?” said the old man. “Now,” said the stranger. “Here?” “Behind a locked door.” A murmur ran through the room, and then died. I had come to Millbeck Thorpe for spoons, salvers, and family accounts; but as I watched those two men pass into the small parlour beyond the stair, I understood that the town itself had an account to settle, and that the balance had at last been brought to the door.
