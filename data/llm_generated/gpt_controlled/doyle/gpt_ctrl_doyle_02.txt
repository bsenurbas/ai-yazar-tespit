It was in the winter of 1891 that I first came to Marston Hallow, a little town folded among the fens, where the roads ran straight as ruled lines and the lamps burned low behind panes blurred by mist. My business was of the plainest sort: I had been retained to examine a disputed account between two branches of a farming family, and I had expected no greater adventure than bad coffee, damp sheets, and several hours among ledgers. Yet from the moment my post-chaise rolled into the square, I became aware of a tension in the place. No child lingered by the pump. No idler stood at the alehouse door. Even the ostler, who took my horse by the bridle, kept his head half-turned toward the eastern road. I am not fanciful by temperament. When a town is silent, there is a cause. When several men pretend not to look in the same direction, the matter is recent. I had just drawn this modest conclusion when the sound of wheels came out of the fog.

“Who comes so late?” I asked. “No one from here,” said the ostler. “Then from where?” “Ask him.” The vehicle that emerged was a shabby gig, driven hard and splashed to the axle. Its passenger climbed down without waiting for assistance, a lean gentleman of perhaps forty years, clean-shaven, pale, and dressed with a care that made his mud-stained boots more curious than untidy. He carried no trunk, only a small black bag and a rolled rug secured by a strap. This struck me at once. A traveller who brings no trunk does not mean to stay; a man who brings a rug expects delay; a man who wears town clothes upon fen roads has either misjudged his route or chosen haste over comfort. His first act confirmed my line of thought. He did not ask for supper, fire, or lodging. He looked at the clock above the taproom door. “Seven minutes slow,” he said. The landlord frowned. “You know our clock?” “I know the train.” “There is no train here.” “No. That is why I came by road.”

He signed the book with a hand so steady that I stepped nearer under pretence of warming myself. The name he wrote was “James Alder,” but he formed the J as a man accustomed to beginning another name. I mention this not as a flourish, but because small habits are less easily governed than large statements. He then turned to the landlord. “Has Mr. Fenton left word?” “No.” “Has he left town?” “No.” “Good.” These three short exchanges told me much. The stranger was expected by circumstance, not by invitation; he was uncertain of his reception; and Mr. Fenton, whoever he might be, was the hinge upon which the whole affair turned. Presently the church bell gave a single cracked note, though the hour was not struck. At that sound, every face in the room changed. The stranger alone smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile, but the expression of a man who has tested a calculation and found it sound. He took up his bag. “Which house?” “Across the square,” whispered the ostler. “The one with the green door.” “I know.” Then, without another word, he passed out into the mist, and I followed far enough to see that the town of Marston Hallow, which had seemed asleep when I entered it, had in truth been holding its breath.
