The inheritance came to me through a chain of deaths so regular in their spacing—each heir surviving the estate by almost exactly three years before being replaced by the next—that even the solicitor who delivered the news seemed aware of the pattern, though he chose, with professional discretion, not to name it. I was the seventh heir in forty years. I did not ask what had become of the first six. I had already looked it up.

The estate itself was unremarkable in its outward aspect: a house of moderate size, grounds that had been maintained with the minimum of effort consistent with not being noticed, and a reputation in the surrounding district that expressed itself not in specific stories but in the general reluctance of local people to be employed there after dark. I had expected this. What I had not expected was the garden.

The garden was beautiful. This was the wrong word for it—beauty implies pleasure, and what the garden produced was something more fundamental than pleasure and considerably less comfortable. It had been planted with an intention that was visible in every arrangement of stone and plant and path, an intention that had nothing to do with aesthetics and everything to do with purpose. It was a garden designed to be walked in a specific sequence, and on the first morning, before I understood this, I walked it in the wrong order and felt, at a certain junction of two paths beneath an arch of yew, a resistance—physical, palpable, the resistance of air that does not wish to be parted—that stopped me as completely as a locked door and sent me, without entirely understanding why, back to the house to find the journal that the solicitor had mentioned and that I had not yet read.
