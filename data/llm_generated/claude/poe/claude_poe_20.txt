It began with the clocks. Not all at once—that would have been dramatic, and what happened was the opposite of dramatic; it was quiet and incremental and easily dismissed at each individual stage. The grandfather clock in the hallway began to lose time. Two minutes a day, then five, then an inconsistent amount that defeated the efforts of two separate clockmakers to explain or correct. The clock on the mantelpiece in the drawing room followed. Then the small carriage clock in my bedroom, which had kept perfect time for twenty years and which I had wound myself every Sunday evening with the regularity of a man who finds comfort in small rituals.

By the time all seven clocks in the house were losing time at different and uncorrelated rates, I had developed the habit of checking my pocket watch, which I kept wound and which kept perfect time, because I had carried it outside the house when I walked and could therefore trust it in a way I could no longer trust anything that had remained within the walls. The pocket watch was reliable until the Tuesday in February when I checked it against the clock at the railway station and found it forty minutes slow.

The station clock, the town hall clock, and the clock in the window of the jeweler's on the high street all agreed with each other. They did not agree with my pocket watch. I had carried the watch outside the house, but it had been inside the house before that, on the nightstand while I slept, and apparently whatever had settled into the walls and infected the clocks had by that point infected the watch as well—had followed it out, had made itself at home in the mechanism, and was now, with the quiet thoroughness of a thing that has decided to be thorough, working on time itself: slowing it, pulling it, drawing it backward toward something that had happened in that house and that the house, for reasons I was only beginning to understand, had not finished with yet.
