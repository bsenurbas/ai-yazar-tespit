The house had been silent for eleven years before I inherited it, and silent it remained in the manner of things that have chosen silence rather than had it imposed upon them. My uncle had been its last occupant, and the circumstances of his departure—if departure is the correct word for what the inquest had returned an open verdict upon—were known to me only through the compressed and careful language of solicitors, who have a professional gift for describing the extraordinary in terms that make it sound entirely administrative.

I arrived in October, which seemed appropriate. The grounds had gone to a productive wildness, the kind that looks intentional from a distance and reveals itself as abandonment only upon closer examination. The house itself had the quality of a face in repose: not sleeping, not watching, but occupying some intermediate state that resists easy categorization. I had been told the east wing was not structurally sound. I had been told this by three separate people, each of whom had added, without being asked, that they had never personally entered it.

On my third night I understood why. The east wing was structurally sound. What it was not was empty. I do not mean this in the crude sense of rattling chains and cold drafts, the furniture of lesser hauntings. I mean that the east wing contained a presence that had the quality of accumulated intention: something that had been waiting, with the patience of the genuinely purposeful, for someone with the correct combination of blood and foolishness to arrive and open the door at the end of the corridor that my uncle had, I now noticed, not merely locked but plastered shut, and above which someone had written, in a hand I recognized from his letters as his own, the single word: finally.
