My physician assures me that the heart is a mechanical organ, subject to the same laws of deterioration as any other instrument of flesh and labor. I do not dispute this. What I dispute—and what no physician has yet been able to address with any satisfaction—is the question of whose heartbeat I have been hearing in the walls of my study for the past eleven nights. It is not my own; I have verified this by the simple expedient of holding my breath. The sound continues without interruption, steady and deliberate, at a rate somewhat slower than the resting human pulse, as though whatever produces it is in no hurry, has never been in any hurry, and intends to make this patience known.

I have had the walls opened. Behind the plaster there is nothing but the expected timber and horsehair and the accumulated silence of a house built in an age when silence was considered a virtue rather than a symptom. The workmen found nothing. The sound, during their investigations, ceased entirely—with what I can only describe as discretion—and resumed the moment they departed.

I have written this account at my desk, with the sound at my back, because I have found that facing it directly is no longer possible. Not from fear, precisely; I am beyond the common varieties of fear, having exhausted them in the first three nights. It is something more fundamental: the sense that the sound is aware of being observed, and that observation changes it in ways I cannot afford to let it be changed. As long as I do not look, it remains merely a sound. I am terrified of what it might become if I were to give it the dignity of my full attention.
