I am not a superstitious man. I record this not as a boast but as relevant context for what follows, because what follows will sound like the account of a superstitious man, and I wish it to be understood that I have examined each element of my experience at Vorthane with the same skeptical attention I have brought to every other question in a life spent in the practice of rational inquiry, and that the skeptical attention has not, in this case, produced the reassurance I had expected it to produce.

The sounds began on the second night: a rhythmic percussion from somewhere below the floor of the library, at intervals too regular for pipes and too deliberate for settling timber. I descended to the cellar with a lamp and found nothing. The sounds continued above me while I stood below, and stopped the moment I returned to the library. This happened three nights in sequence. On the fourth night I remained in the library and made a careful note of the pattern: seven beats, a pause, three beats, a longer pause, seven beats again. I consulted nothing supernatural in my search for an explanation. I consulted instead a reference work on telegraphy. The pattern was not Morse code. It was older than Morse code. It was a pattern I eventually traced, through considerable research, to a signaling system used by a particular religious community in this region in the seventeenth century—a community that had been dissolved, forcibly and finally, in 1683, and whose last recorded act had been the interment of something beneath the floor of the building that had stood on this site before the present house was constructed. The reference gave no details about what had been interred. It noted only that the community had considered the interment permanent, and had taken considerable pains to ensure that it remained so. The sounds suggested they had not entirely succeeded.
