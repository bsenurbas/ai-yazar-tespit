The painting came to me through a chain of ownership so attenuated and so marked at each link by misfortune that I should, perhaps, have declined to extend the chain further. But I am a collector, and collectors are by nature optimists about acquisition and pessimists about everything else, and so I paid the price asked and had the painting delivered and hung it in the study where the light was good and where I spent my evenings and where I have spent every evening since in the company of something that grows, incrementally and without haste, more present with each passing week.

The subject is a woman standing at a window, her back to the viewer, looking out at a landscape that is indistinct in a way that seems intentional rather than merely unfinished—as though the painter knew what was outside the window and had made a considered decision that the viewer should not. She is dressed in clothing of no particular period, which I initially attributed to the painter's stylistic choice and have since come to suspect was the painter's honest observation. Her posture is the posture of someone who has been standing in one place for a long time and has no intention of moving.

What has changed since the painting's arrival is not the painting itself but the room around it. The study feels smaller. Not measurably—I have checked—but in the way that spaces feel smaller when they are occupied by something that claims them. The woman at the window has not moved. She has not needed to. She has simply been present, with the accumulative weight of a thing that has learned patience as a primary virtue, until her presence has become the room's dominant fact, and everything else in it—the books, the desk, the lamp, and I myself, when I sit beneath her—has become secondary to the question of what she is looking at, and whether, on the night I finally summon the courage or the foolishness to look for myself, it will still be outside the window, or whether it will have come inside.
