Sleep had abandoned me—not gradually, in the way that sleep abandons the anxious or the grieving, but completely and at once, as though a decision had been made by some authority I had not been consulted. Seven days without sleep, and still no fatigue beyond the ordinary fatigue of living; seven days in which the nights passed with the slow, clear, merciless clarity of perfect wakefulness, and the days were indistinguishable from the nights except by the presence or absence of light.

My physician found nothing. This was the expected result and not a reassurance. He found nothing because his instruments were designed to detect the things that are normally wrong with people who cannot sleep, and what was wrong with me was not, I had begun to understand, the kind of thing his instruments were designed to detect. I was not ill. I was altered, in some way that had not yet revealed its full nature to me, and the alteration had begun on the night I had spent in the east room of the Hartwell house, the room that was kept locked, the room I had opened with a key borrowed from a drawer I had been specifically instructed not to open.

On the eighth night, something changed. I was sitting at my desk at three in the morning, reading, when I became aware that the room had acquired an additional occupant. I did not see anyone. I did not hear anyone. I was simply, suddenly, no longer alone, in the way that one is no longer alone when a person enters a room, except that no person had entered. Something had. It settled in the corner by the window with the air of something that had been traveling for a long time and was pleased to have arrived, and I understood, with the clarity that seven days of sleeplessness had given me, that it had come from the east room of the Hartwell house, that it had followed me here, and that the sleep it had taken from me had been, in some sense I did not yet fully understand, the price of its passage.
