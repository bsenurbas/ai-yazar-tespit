There are those who will say that what I experienced in the vault beneath the Usherwood estate was the product of exhaustion and prolonged isolation—that the mind, deprived of ordinary commerce with the world, constructs its own terrible commerce from the materials of fear. I do not argue with such persons. I have ceased to argue with anyone. Argument requires a belief in the persuadability of others, and that belief was the first casualty of what I found beneath the flagstone marked with the symbol I had been instructed, in my employer's final letter, never to examine.

The vault was dry. That was the first surprise. In a structure of such evident antiquity, set so deep beneath the water table of that marshy and desolate terrain, one expected the universal dampness of buried things. But the air within was not merely dry; it was preserved, as the air within a sealed coffin is preserved—not fresh, but unchanged, as though it had been waiting at a particular moment in time for someone to open the door and step inside.

The second surprise was the sound. It came from everywhere and nowhere, as sounds do in chambers that have been silent too long: a low, continuous vibration, not quite a tone, not quite a voice, but possessed of an urgency that the nerves recognized before the mind could name it. I held my lantern high and saw, arranged along the walls with the care of a devoted and patient hand, every object I had ever lost. Every object. Beginning with a toy from my third year of life, and ending—God help me—with my own reflection, framed and mounted, showing a face some twenty years older than the one I wore.
