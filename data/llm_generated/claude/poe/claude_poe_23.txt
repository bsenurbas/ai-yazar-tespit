The twin had been dead for twenty years when his brother began to dream his dreams. Not dreams about him—dreams that were his: the specific, textured, internally consistent dreams of another person's sleeping mind, complete with the furniture of another life, the faces of people never met, the residue of memories belonging to someone who no longer existed to claim them. My patient—for I am a physician, and this account is a medical record, whatever else it may be—described the transition as gradual: first a vague familiarity in certain dreams, then the recognition of specific details that could only have come from his brother's experience, then, finally, the full immersive certainty of inhabiting another consciousness entirely.

I do not offer a clinical explanation. I have spent considerable time looking for one and have not found it, and intellectual honesty requires me to acknowledge the limits of my understanding rather than substituting a plausible framework for an adequate one. What I can report is the phenomenology, which my patient described with a precision and a consistency across multiple sessions that I found both compelling and troubling.

He said the dreams were not unpleasant. He said his brother had been, apparently, a contented man—more contented than he himself had ever been—and that the experience of inhabiting that contentment was, in the dream state, genuinely restful. What disturbed him was not the dreams themselves but their directionality: he could inhabit his brother's consciousness, but his brother, he was increasingly certain, could inhabit his. And his brother, he said, with the careful phrasing of a man who has thought at length about how to say something that sounds impossible, had opinions about how his life was being managed in his absence, and had been, for some time, finding ways to express them.
