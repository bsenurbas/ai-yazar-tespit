The funeral was attended by people who had not known the deceased, which was itself unremarkable—funerals attract the curious as well as the bereaved—but who seemed, on closer observation, to know each other with the familiarity of a community gathered for a regular occasion rather than an exceptional one. They did not speak to the family. They stood at the back of the church and at the edges of the churchyard with the attentive stillness of witnesses rather than mourners, and they left before the coffin was lowered, in a group, without apparent coordination, as though a signal had been given that only they could perceive.

I noticed them because I was the family's solicitor and therefore positioned at the edge of the proceedings, which gave me a view of the whole that those more centrally placed could not have. I also noticed that the family did not notice them, or appeared not to, with the particular quality of not-noticing that is distinct from genuine inattention—the quality of people who have learned, through long practice, not to look at something that has always been present at these occasions.

I made inquiries afterward, as discreetly as I could. The family had been burying its dead in this churchyard for six generations. The same group, or a group indistinguishable from this one, had been present at every funeral in that time, according to the oldest records I could find. They were not recorded in any parish document. They were not local. No one I spoke to could identify them, though several people, when I described them, nodded with the recognition of people who know exactly what is being described and have decided, for reasons they did not share with me, that knowing is not the same as acknowledging.
