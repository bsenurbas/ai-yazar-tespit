The well had been dry for forty years. This was the first thing the surveyor told me when I purchased the property, and he said it with the emphasis of a man imparting information he considers more important than his professional brief requires. The well is dry, he said. Has been dry since the winter of 1943. Nothing comes up. I thanked him for the information and thought no more about it until the night of the first rain, when I lay in bed listening to the storm and heard, beneath the sound of water on the roof and water on the ground and water in the gutters, the sound of something being drawn up.

I went to the window. The well cover was in place, as it had been when I arrived, as it had been in every photograph of the property I had examined before purchase. The sound continued. It had the rhythm of effort: the rhythm of something heavy rising through a long, dark, resistant column of air that had been undisturbed for four decades and did not wish to be disturbed now.

In the morning the well cover was slightly displaced. Not dramatically—not thrown aside, not shattered—but moved, with the carefulness of something that did not wish to draw attention to what it had been doing but had not been quite careful enough. I replaced it and secured it with the chain that had been provided for the purpose. The following night the sound came again, and in the morning the chain was neatly coiled beside the well, and on the cover, in the condensation left by the night's rain, something had written a single word in a script that I did not recognize, in characters that dried and vanished before I could copy them, though their meaning, somehow, remained.
