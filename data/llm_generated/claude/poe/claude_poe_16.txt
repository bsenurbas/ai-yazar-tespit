There are degrees of darkness, as there are degrees of everything that matters. The darkness of a moonless night in the country is not the darkness of a sealed room, and the darkness of a sealed room is not the darkness that I encountered in the lower passages of the Wrentham estate, which was a darkness with substance to it—a darkness that did not merely occupy space but displaced it, that pressed against the skin with the gentle, persistent weight of something that has been waiting in one place for a very long time and has grown accustomed to that place and does not welcome the intrusion of light or of those who carry it.

I had been sent to catalogue the contents of the estate following the death of its last occupant, a commission I had accepted without inquiring too closely into why three previous cataloguers had declined to complete it. The upper floors presented no difficulties. The furniture was unremarkable, the paintings competent, the library extensive but not unusual. It was only when I descended to the lower level, following the map provided by the solicitor, that I encountered the passage that the map labeled simply as storage and that was, as I have indicated, something other than that.

What was stored there I cannot tell you precisely, because precision requires that the thing being described have stable properties, and what I found in that passage did not have stable properties. It was different each time I looked at it directly and different again when I looked at it from the corner of my eye. What remained constant—the one property I can report with confidence—was its awareness. Whatever was stored in that passage knew I was there. It had known before I arrived. And it had been making arrangements, with a patience and a thoroughness that I found, upon reflection, more disturbing than anything else about the encounter, for my eventual return.
