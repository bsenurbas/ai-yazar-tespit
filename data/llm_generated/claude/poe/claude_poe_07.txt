The mirror in the upper hallway was covered when I arrived, as mirrors are covered in houses of mourning, though the mourning in this case had concluded some thirty years prior and no one had thought, or perhaps dared, to remove the black cloth in the interim. I asked the caretaker about it on my first evening. He looked at me with the expression of a man who has been asked a question he has spent considerable effort arranging never to be asked, and said only that the mirror had been covered at the request of the last occupant and that it was not his place to undo what the dead had done.

I respected this for eleven days. On the twelfth, curiosity—that most ruinous of the intellectual virtues—overcame what I had mistaken for prudence. I removed the cloth at noon, in full daylight, with every lamp in the hallway burning. The mirror showed the hallway as it was: the faded runner, the row of empty hooks, the door at the far end standing open. It showed all of this faithfully and in proper proportion, with one exception. In the mirror, the door at the far end of the hallway was not open. It was closed. And the handle, which in reality was still, was, in the mirror's version of the world, slowly, almost imperceptibly, turning.

I replaced the cloth. I have not removed it again. I have, however, taken to sleeping with my door locked and my chair wedged beneath the handle—not from any rational assessment of danger, but from the simpler and more fundamental conviction that there are some questions to which the honest answer is that one does not, after all, wish to know.
