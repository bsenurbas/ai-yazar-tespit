The cataloguing of the library took three weeks, and it was in the third week, on a Tuesday of unremarkable weather, that I found the book that was not a book. It occupied a space on the lowest shelf between two volumes of natural philosophy, and it resembled a book in every superficial respect: it had a spine, a cover of dark green boards, and the remnant of gilt lettering that had worn to illegibility. But it had no pages. What it had instead I find difficult to describe with any precision, because the interior of the object did not behave as interiors ordinarily behave. It was deep in a way that the physical dimensions of the cover did not permit. It was also, in a manner I can convey only by analogy, attentive.

I closed it immediately and replaced it on the shelf. I then spent the remainder of the afternoon completing my catalogue with a thoroughness I had not previously brought to the task, checking and rechecking each entry with an attention that had nothing to do with professional diligence and everything to do with the need to keep my eyes and hands occupied with things that behaved as things are supposed to behave.

That evening I wrote to the estate's solicitor and asked, with what I intended as casual curiosity, whether any of the library's contents had been noted as unusual by previous cataloguers. His reply arrived four days later. It said that the library had been catalogued twice before: once in 1887 and once in 1923. The 1887 cataloguer had resigned without completing the work and had not, to the solicitor's knowledge, taken any subsequent professional commissions. The 1923 cataloguer had completed the work but had requested, as a condition of submitting her final report, that a particular item on the lowest shelf of the west wall be removed and destroyed before she would enter the building again. The solicitor's predecessor had agreed to these terms. He did not know why the item was still there.
