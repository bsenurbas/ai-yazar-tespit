I have been a collector of rare manuscripts for thirty years, and in that time I have learned to distinguish between a document that is merely old and a document that is old in the way that certain things are old—not through the passage of time but through an accumulation of what time has witnessed. The manuscript I acquired in the autumn of last year belonged to the second category. The dealer who sold it to me would not say where he had obtained it. He would say only that he had been glad to see it go, and that he hoped I would be more successful than its previous owners in keeping it closed.

I was not. The temptation of a sealed text is not merely intellectual; it is something older and less dignified than scholarship, something that operates at the level of the hands before the mind has had its say. I opened it on the second evening, in good light, with my customary tools laid out before me, in the manner of a man who believes that professional preparation can serve as a kind of moral inoculation against whatever he is about to do.

The manuscript contained one hundred and forty pages of dense, fine script in a language I did not recognize, interspersed with diagrams of an anatomical character that depicted structures no anatomy I had studied had ever described. What it also contained—and this I did not discover until I had been reading for perhaps two hours and had begun to feel the particular heaviness behind the eyes that signals the onset of sleep—was a method of reading that was not passive. The text read back. Not metaphorically. Not in the sense that great literature engages its reader. The text read back in the sense that by the time I closed it and set it on my desk and went to my bed, it knew considerably more about me than I had learned about it.
