It was upon the eve of the winter solstice that I first perceived the shadow upon the wall—not the shadow of any object within the chamber, for I had long since extinguished every candle and barred every shutter against the moonless night, but a shadow of something that existed, as it were, between the visible and the unseen. I did not speak of it to my companion, for I feared the sound of my own voice might confirm what the silence had not yet made absolute.

The shadow moved with a patience that mocked the hurried pulse of mortal dread. It did not advance; it accumulated, as water accumulates in the hollows of ancient stone, filling first the corners of perception, then the center, until the whole of my understanding was submerged beneath its terrible composure. I pressed myself against the wall and found the wall no longer cold, but warm—dreadfully, intimately warm—as though something on the other side had laid its full length against the plaster and waited.

When at last I cried out, the sound that issued from my lips was not my own. It was older than my name, older than my lineage, and it seemed to satisfy whatever stood beyond the wall, for the warmth receded at once, and in the silence that followed I heard, with a clarity that no darkness could diminish, the slow and deliberate sound of footsteps descending a stair that, to the best of my waking knowledge, had been sealed for forty years.
