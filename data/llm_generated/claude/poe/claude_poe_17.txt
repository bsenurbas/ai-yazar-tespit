My father kept a room locked that I was not permitted to enter, and he kept it locked with a consistency that precluded the possibility of accident or oversight. It was locked on ordinary days and on holidays and on the day of my mother's funeral, when every other room in the house was open to the neighbors who came and went with the casseroles and the condolences that are the currency of collective grief. It was locked when my father was present and locked when he was absent, and on the one occasion when I asked him about it directly, in the forthright manner of a child who has not yet learned that some questions are questions only in grammatical form, he looked at me with an expression I had not seen on his face before and said that some things were better not known and that I would understand this when I was older.

I am older now. I do not understand it in the way he meant. I understand it in a different way, which is that some things are better not known not because the knowledge is harmful but because the knowledge, once acquired, cannot be set aside, and the life one lives with it is irrevocably different from the life one might otherwise have lived. I understand this because I eventually found the key, as children eventually find everything that adults hide from them, and I opened the door, and I saw what was in the room, and it saw me, and I closed the door and replaced the key and went back to my ordinary life, and my ordinary life has never since been entirely ordinary, because the thing in the room is still there, and it is still aware of me, and on certain nights, when the house is quiet, I can hear it, on the other side of the locked door, doing what it has always done: waiting, with perfect patience, for me to come back.
