She had been dead for seven years when she began to leave notes. The first appeared on the kitchen table on a Tuesday morning in March, written in her hand on paper from the notepad she had always kept by the telephone, and it said only: the window in the blue room does not latch properly. This was true. It had never latched properly. I had meant to have it repaired for years and had not, and the fact that she had known this was not remarkable, because she had known everything about the house that I had neglected, and had spent twenty years pointing these things out to me with the patient resignation of a woman who has learned that pointing things out is not the same as having them corrected.

I told myself it was my own handwriting. I told myself this for three notes. By the fourth, which concerned the slow leak beneath the bathroom sink that I had also known about and also not repaired, I had examined all four documents with a magnifying glass and a sample of my own writing, and I could no longer maintain the position that the handwriting was mine. It was hers. Every characteristic that a graphologist would cite as individually distinctive was present and consistent across all four notes.

The fifth note was different in tone. The previous four had been domestic, practical, the continuation of a habit so ingrained that apparently even death had not entirely interrupted it. The fifth note said: I am not trying to frighten you. I am trying to finish the list. There are forty-seven items remaining. I thought you should know, so that you could decide whether you wanted me to continue, or whether you would prefer I stopped. I have left a space at the bottom for your answer. I notice you have not fixed the window.
