The physician who attended my brother in his final weeks was a man of careful speech and careful silences, and it was in the silences that I eventually learned to read the truth of my brother's condition. When I asked direct questions, the physician answered them directly, with the precision of a man who has decided that honesty, properly delivered, is its own form of mercy. When I asked the questions I could not quite bring myself to ask directly, he was silent for exactly as long as it took me to understand that the silence was itself the answer.

It was in one of these silences that I asked what my brother had spoken of in his less lucid moments. The physician looked at me with an expression that combined professional caution with something more personal, something that suggested the question touched on territory he had visited himself and not entirely returned from. He said that my brother had spoken of a room. Not a room in the house where he lay, and not any room that the physician could identify from the description. A room with no windows and no door and no apparent means of entry or exit, which my brother nonetheless described as a room he visited regularly and from which he always returned changed in ways he could not specify.

The physician paused here. Then he said that in forty years of practice he had heard many things from patients in the borderland between life and whatever comes next, and that most of these things he had categorized, with the confident skepticism of his profession, as the productions of a failing brain. He said he was having some difficulty categorizing what my brother had described. He said this not with distress but with the particular discomfort of a man of science who has encountered data that does not fit his existing framework and has not yet decided what to do with it. He asked if I knew the room my brother meant. I said that I did.
