The cataloguer's notes, which I found tucked between the pages of the estate's household accounts for the year 1887, were written in a hand that became progressively less steady as they continued, until the final entries were barely legible, the letters large and irregular in the manner of writing produced by a hand that is shaking or a mind that is no longer fully attending to the mechanics of the task. The early entries were professional, precise, the work of someone accustomed to systematic description. The later entries were something else.

The transition occurred, as far as I could determine from the dates, over a period of approximately three weeks, and it coincided with the cataloguer's work in the portrait gallery. The early entries concerning the gallery were unremarkable: dimensions, condition, attributions, the standard vocabulary of the trade. It was on the fourteenth day of work in the gallery that the entries began to change, first in tone—a note of unease that expressed itself in unnecessary qualifications and a tendency to describe the subjects of portraits as though they were present rather than depicted—and then in content, until by the final entry the cataloguer was no longer describing the portraits at all but was instead recording, in the shaking hand I have described, a conversation that she claimed to be having with a woman in a green dress who she said had been standing behind her for the past seven hours and who wanted, with a patience that the cataloguer found more frightening than urgency would have been, to tell her something about the room at the end of the gallery that no one had entered since 1831.
