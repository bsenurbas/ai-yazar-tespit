The portrait had hung in the eastern gallery since before my birth, and since before my father's birth, and since before the birth of any soul now living who might corroborate or deny what I am about to set down. It depicted a woman of perhaps thirty years, dressed in the fashion of an age I could not precisely identify, her hands folded upon a closed book whose title had been painted with such deliberate obscurity that no examination, however prolonged, could resolve it into legible characters.

I had passed beneath it ten thousand times without incident. But on the night of which I speak—a night of extraordinary stillness, when even the wind seemed to have abandoned its ancient quarrel with the eaves—I paused, as though arrested by a hand upon my shoulder. The woman in the portrait had turned her eyes. Not her head; her head retained its three-quarter profile with all the rigidity of oil and canvas. But the eyes—those flat, painted, impossible eyes—had moved to regard me with an expression I can only describe as recognition.

I did not flee. Flee to where? The house contained no room that did not contain its own darkness, no corridor that did not lead, by one route or another, back to the eastern gallery. Instead I stood and returned her gaze, and in that terrible communion I understood at last why the book in her hands had no legible title. It was not a book. It had never been a book. It was a door, painted shut, waiting for someone foolish enough to read it.
