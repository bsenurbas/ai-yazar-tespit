My grandmother spoke of the room only once, near the end of her life, in the careful manner of someone who has kept a secret for so long that the keeping has become indistinguishable from the secret itself. She said there was a room in the house that was not on any plan and could not be found by anyone who looked for it, and that it could only be entered by someone who had stopped looking, who had given up the search and turned away, at which point the door would be present where no door had been a moment before, open, and inviting, in the way that certain invitations are extended not from hospitality but from necessity.

She had entered it once. She would not say what she had found there, but she said that the room knew things—not about her specifically, though it knew about her too, with the comprehensive intimacy of something that has been observing a family for generations—but about everyone who had ever lived in the house, and about certain things that had happened in the house that no living person had witnessed, and about certain things that were going to happen, which she declined to specify, with the manner of a woman who has decided that some knowledge is not improved by being shared.

She said the room was not malicious. She said this with some emphasis, as though she anticipated the assumption. It was not malicious, she said; it was simply thorough, in the way that time is thorough, in the way that consequence is thorough, in the way that anything that has been accumulating for long enough becomes thorough simply by virtue of having accumulated. She advised me never to stop looking for it. She said the ones who found it by accident were less prepared than the ones who had been looking and had given up, and that preparedness, in this particular matter, made a difference, though she would not say what kind.
