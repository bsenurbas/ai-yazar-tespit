There is a color that has no name in any language I have studied, and I have studied seven. I encountered it first in the lower rooms of the Ashcroft estate, in the particular quality of light that gathered in the corners of the drawing room at dusk, and I have encountered it since in dreams, in the border state between waking and sleep, and once, memorably, in the eyes of a man on a train who looked at me with an expression of recognition so complete that I felt certain, in the moment before he looked away, that he had seen the same color and knew that I had seen it too.

The color is not dark and not light. It does not belong to the visible spectrum in any straightforward sense; it is not a color that objects possess but a color that certain spaces achieve under certain conditions of failing light and accumulated history. I have tried to describe it to people who have not seen it and have consistently failed, because the descriptions that come closest—a particular shade of violet, a certain quality of grey, the color of water over old stone—are all approximations that capture one property while missing the essential one, which is that the color is aware of being seen.

I know how this sounds. I am a painter by profession and a rationalist by inclination, and I am telling you that a color is aware. I tell you this not because I have abandoned rationalism but because rationalism, properly practiced, requires one to report what one has observed rather than what one's framework can accommodate. I have observed a color that is aware. I have observed it in six different locations over eleven years, always in old houses, always at dusk, always in rooms where something irreversible had occurred. I do not know what it is. I know that when I see it, it sees me, and that the seeing is mutual and intentional and, on its side, patient in a way that mine is not.
