It is a curious property of certain old houses that they remember. Not in the manner of documents or monuments, which record what their makers wished preserved, but in the manner of wounds, which retain the shape of what caused them long after the cause itself has passed. The house at the end of the Varlowe road remembered everything—every raised voice, every slammed door, every long silence of the kind that is worse than shouting—and it communicated these memories to those who slept within its walls through the medium of dreams so precise, so documentary, that waking from them was less a relief than a diminishment.

I had been warned. The solicitor who handled the estate had mentioned, with the professional delicacy of a man who does not wish to be held accountable for what he is about to say, that previous tenants had found the house "unsuitable for extended habitation." He did not elaborate. I did not ask him to. I had my own reasons for wanting a house that most people found uninhabitable, and chief among them was the desire to be alone with thoughts that required, for their proper examination, the kind of silence that the living world does not easily provide.

The dreams began on the second night. They were not nightmares—I wish they had been, for nightmares at least have the decency to announce themselves as fiction. These were simply memories, presented with the flat, indifferent clarity of fact: the argument on the landing, the figure at the window, the small hand releasing the banister. They belonged to people I had never met. By the end of the first week I knew their names, their voices, the specific quality of their regrets. By the end of the second, I could no longer be entirely certain which memories were theirs and which were mine.
