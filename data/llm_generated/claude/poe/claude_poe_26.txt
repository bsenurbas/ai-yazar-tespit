The automaton had been constructed, according to the documentation that accompanied it, by a watchmaker in Prague in the year 1743, and it depicted a seated figure of indeterminate age holding an open book. When wound, it would turn the pages of the book at irregular intervals, pause, and then write in a small notebook placed beside it, in a hand that reproduced, with mechanical fidelity, the handwriting of its maker. The current owner had acquired it at auction and had wound it once, for demonstration purposes, at which point it had written in its notebook a sentence in German that translated as: I have been waiting for someone to read what I have written. He had not wound it again and wished to be rid of it.

I acquired it with the detached curiosity of a collector who has learned to find interest in objects that disturb other people, and I wound it that same evening, in good light, with a fresh notebook provided for its use. It wrote, in the antique hand of its long-dead maker, three paragraphs in German, then stopped. The paragraphs described, with a specificity that I verified over the following weeks against historical records, events that had occurred in Prague in the summer of 1743, events that the watchmaker could not have foreseen because they had not yet occurred when the automaton was constructed, events that were nevertheless recorded in the city archives exactly as the automaton described them.

I have wound it seventeen times since. Each time it writes something new. Each entry is dated, in the automaton's notation, with a date that has not yet arrived. I have verified four of these future entries against subsequent events and found them accurate. I am currently waiting for the fifth. The automaton, for its part, continues to write with the patient regularity of something that has a great deal to report and all the time it needs to report it.
