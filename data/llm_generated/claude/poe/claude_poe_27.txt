The letters arrived addressed to a name I did not recognize, at an address that was mine, in a handwriting that was also mine but that I had not produced. I examined them carefully: the ink, the paper, the precise formation of each character. The handwriting was identical to my own in every measurable respect—the particular angle of my ascenders, the way I close the letter o, the slight leftward drift that develops in my script when I am writing quickly. I had not written these letters. I was certain of this, as certain as one can be of a negative, which is to say not entirely certain but certain enough that the uncertainty itself became a source of dread.

The name on the envelopes was Aldric Vane. I knew no one of that name. I searched my memory with the thoroughness of a man who suspects his memory of concealing something from him, and found no Aldric Vane, no connection to that name, no reason why letters addressed to that name should arrive at my address in my handwriting. The letters were sealed. I did not open them for eleven days, on the grounds that opening them would constitute a commitment to the reality of the situation that I was not yet ready to make.

When I opened the first letter, I found it addressed to me—to my name, not to Aldric Vane—and written in a hand that was not mine, describing events from my childhood that no one outside my immediate family could have known, describing them accurately and in sequence, and concluding with a single line: you have been confusing yourself with him for a very long time, and the confusion has consequences that are about to become apparent. The envelope, which I reexamined carefully after reading this, was still addressed to Aldric Vane. The name had not changed. I had.
