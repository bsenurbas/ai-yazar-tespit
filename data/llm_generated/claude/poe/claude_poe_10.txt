There is a particular quality of silence that occurs only in rooms where something has ended—not the active silence of anticipation, which has the quality of a held breath, but the settled silence of aftermath, which has the quality of an exhaled one. I had known this silence in hospitals and in the immediate aftermath of accidents, but I had not expected to find it in a room that, to all appearances, had been empty for years and contained nothing that could have ended.

And yet the silence in the upper room of the Mervale house was precisely that silence—the silence not of emptiness but of completion, as though something had arrived at its conclusion in that room and the room had absorbed the conclusion so thoroughly that it had become part of the atmosphere, breathable, permanent, impossible to dispel by the simple introduction of living persons and the noise they make.

My associate felt it too, though she described it differently. She said the room felt crowded. I did not argue with her description, because it was not wrong—it was simply the other side of my own observation. An aftermath implies an event; an event implies participants; and participants, even after their departure, leave an impress upon space that sensitive persons register as presence rather than absence. The room was both empty and crowded, both silent and full of its concluded sound. We stayed for perhaps four minutes. We did not speak. On the stairs going down, she took my arm, and I was grateful for the contact—not from fear, for I have said I was beyond that, but from the simpler need to verify, by the warmth and weight of another person, that I was still on the correct side of whatever boundary that room represented.
