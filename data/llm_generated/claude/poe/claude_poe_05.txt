The letter arrived on a Thursday, which I mention not because the day holds any particular significance in the calendar of ordinary events, but because it was a Thursday when my brother disappeared, a Thursday when the inquest returned its unsatisfactory verdict, and a Thursday—seven years before either of these—when we had stood together at our mother's grave and made the promise that neither of us, in the years that followed, had found any means of keeping.

The handwriting was his. I say this with the full knowledge of what it implies, and with the additional knowledge that he had been dead for fourteen months when the letter was postmarked. I had identified the body myself. I had chosen the stone. I had read the words carved upon it so many times that they had ceased to be words and become instead a pattern, like wallpaper, like the grain of wood, like the sound one hears in a shell—present, insistent, and finally meaningless.

The letter contained three sentences. The first told me where he had hidden the document I had spent a year searching for without knowing I was searching. The second told me not to open it until the house was sold and I was gone from the county forever. The third sentence I have copied here, though I confess I do not understand it, and understanding it, I believe, is no longer the point: "The door you sealed from the outside was sealed from the inside first, and the one who sealed it is still in the room, and has been waiting, with the patience of the genuinely dead, for you to remember that you put them there."
