The transmission was first detected by the observatory at Jodrell Bank on a Thursday morning in early March, and it was the unremarkable quality of the signal that delayed recognition of its significance by nearly seventy-two hours. It did not arrive in a burst of dramatic interference, did not disrupt electrical systems or cause compass needles to spin. It simply appeared in the data, a regular pulse at a frequency that had no natural source, repeating with a precision that exceeded anything achieved by human engineering, and had been present, upon examination of archived recordings, for at least fourteen months before anyone noticed it.

The astronomer who first identified it as artificial was a postgraduate researcher named Calloway, twenty-six years old, who brought her findings to her supervisor with the careful diffidence of someone who is fairly certain she is right and entirely uncertain how that rightness will be received. Her supervisor called in two colleagues. The colleagues called in three more. By Friday evening there were eleven people in the room, and by Saturday morning the number had grown to include two representatives from the Ministry of Defence who sat at the back of the room and took notes and said very little.

What Calloway had found was not simply a signal. It was a signal with structure: layered frequencies, each carrying information at a different rate, each dependent on the others for its full meaning. It was, in the estimation of the information theorist who was flown in from Cambridge on Saturday afternoon, the work of a mind that had given considerable thought to the problem of communicating with a recipient who might not share any common frame of reference, and had solved that problem in a way that no human researcher had yet managed to solve it even in theory.
