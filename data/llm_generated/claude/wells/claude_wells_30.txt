The last human settlement on the surface of the planet was not abandoned dramatically. It was not evacuated under fire or surrendered after a siege or destroyed by the forces that had made the surface uninhabitable. It was simply, over a period of about three years, emptied by the ordinary processes of people making individual decisions about where they preferred to live, and the last residents left not because they were forced to but because the infrastructure supporting the settlement had reached the point where maintaining it required more effort than the number of remaining residents could justify, and the remaining residents, being practical people, recognized this and made arrangements accordingly.

The settlement had been founded a hundred and forty years earlier by people who believed the surface was still livable, or could be made livable with sufficient effort. They had been partially right. The surface had been livable, with effort, for about ninety years. The last fifty years had required more effort than was reasonable, and the people who had committed themselves to that effort had done so out of a combination of stubbornness, principle, and the particular attachment to a way of life that comes from having built it rather than inherited it. Their children and grandchildren shared the attachment but were more willing to be practical about it, which is the normal trajectory.

The settlement is maintained now as a historical site, which means that it is visited occasionally by researchers and rather more frequently by people who are not researchers but who find it useful, for reasons of their own, to stand in a place where people once lived on the surface of the world and look up at a sky that those people had looked up at and had decided, after a long time, they could no longer afford to live under. The sky has not changed. What has changed is the assessment of what it costs.
