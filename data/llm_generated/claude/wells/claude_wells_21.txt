The news that the comet would pass within a distance that no comet had approached in recorded history reached the general public on a Monday, and by Friday the range of responses it had produced illustrated, more comprehensively than any deliberate survey could have managed, the full spectrum of human reactions to information that is alarming but not immediately actionable. There were those who disbelieved it, on the grounds that official announcements of this character were invariably exaggerated. There were those who believed it completely and drew from it conclusions about the advisability of various immediate actions that kept the banks and the travel agencies and the churches busy for the remainder of the week. And there were those—the majority, as is usually the case—who believed it in a general way while continuing to do the things they had planned to do, because the things they had planned to do were concrete and near and the comet was abstract and distant, and the human mind is not well designed for treating abstract distant threats with the seriousness it extends to concrete immediate ones.

The astronomers who had made the announcement were, by the end of that first week, regretting the precision of their language. They had calculated a probability, stated it accurately, and the public had converted the probability into a certainty, because probabilities are difficult to hold in the mind and certainties are not. What had been announced as a ninety-three percent chance of a passing approach had become, in popular understanding, an imminent collision, and the three percent difference between these characterizations was doing very little work in the public discourse.

Hartley, who had spent twenty years studying how populations respond to scientific risk communication, watched the week unfold with the detached professional interest of a doctor observing a textbook case. He knew what would happen next. He had written papers predicting it. Being right, in his experience, was rarely as satisfying as it was supposed to be.
