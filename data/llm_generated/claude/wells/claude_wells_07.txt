The machine stood in the corner of the workshop under a canvas sheet, and it was the canvas sheet that first told me something was wrong. Penhaligon was not a man who covered his work. In three years of collaboration I had never seen him conceal a project in progress; he had, in fact, a quality of openness about his methods that I had always found both admirable and slightly reckless, given the competitive nature of the field. The canvas suggested either that the work was complete, or that he did not want it seen.

When he removed the sheet, I understood both.

The machine was, in its outward form, not dramatically different from several other devices I had seen in various stages of development in workshops of this kind. It had the characteristic profile of a temporal displacement apparatus—the circular frame, the twin coils, the central chamber barely large enough to contain one person—and the materials and workmanship were consistent with what I knew of Penhaligon's other projects. What was different was the instrumentation. The gauges that should have indicated prospective displacement—how far forward, how far back—were not calibrated in days or years. They were calibrated in probability.

"You've changed the target," I said.

"The target was always too imprecise," he replied. "Any specific date is a point on a line of possible lines. What I've done is change the question. Instead of asking when, I've asked—" he paused, with the slight hesitation of someone choosing between accuracy and comprehensibility, "—which."

I looked at the gauges again and began to understand what three years of apparently conventional research had actually been building toward, and why he had covered it with a canvas sheet.
