The crater was three hundred metres in diameter and perfectly circular, which was the first indication that it had not been formed by any of the processes that ordinarily produce craters. Meteorite impacts are imprecise; volcanic subsidence follows the weaknesses in underlying geology; explosions of human origin leave traces of their chemistry in the soil for decades. This crater left nothing in the soil that had not been there before. The rock at its base was not fractured or fused but simply absent, removed with a cleanliness that suggested not violence but intention.

The government's initial response was to attribute it to a previously unmapped underground cavern. This explanation lasted four days, at which point a second crater appeared in the Gobi Desert, identical in diameter and depth, identical in its geological cleanliness, and precisely eight hundred kilometres from the first. The cavern hypothesis was quietly retired.

By the time a third crater appeared in northern Canada, the pattern had become visible to anyone with access to a map and a ruler, and several people with such access had begun to draw the obvious conclusion, though none of them had yet published it. The craters were not random. They formed a triangle, and the triangle's centre, when calculated, fell in the middle of the North Atlantic at a depth of two thousand metres. What was at that depth, or what was now arriving at that depth, or what had always been at that depth waiting for conditions that had only recently been met, remained a question that the assembled scientists at the emergency conference in Geneva spent three days declining to answer directly, on the reasonable grounds that the answer they kept arriving at was one for which none of them had adequate professional preparation.
