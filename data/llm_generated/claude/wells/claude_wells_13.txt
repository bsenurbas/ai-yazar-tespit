Pemberton had been working on the problem of directed energy transmission for eleven years when he solved it, and the solution, when it came, came not in the laboratory but on a train journey between Birmingham and London, in the manner of solutions that have been prepared by years of systematic work and arrive, when the conditions are finally right, with the suddenness that makes them seem like inspiration rather than the product of accumulated effort.

He wrote the essential equations on the back of a ticket envelope and spent the remainder of the journey checking them with the focused anxiety of a man who suspects he may have made an error and is afraid to find out that he has not. The equations held. He arrived at Euston with the knowledge that he had solved a problem that the scientific community had considered, at best, a generation away from solution, and with no clear idea of what to do with that knowledge.

This uncertainty was not intellectual. Pemberton understood precisely what his solution meant in technical terms. What he was uncertain about was the question that his laboratory training had not prepared him to answer: what his solution meant in terms of what would happen next. Energy transmission without loss across any distance was a problem whose solution would restructure the economics of power generation, the logistics of industrial civilization, and the strategic calculations of every government with the capability to develop the technology. Pemberton was a physicist. He was not a strategist, a diplomat, or a student of the ways in which human institutions respond to the sudden availability of capabilities they have not previously possessed. He was aware, sitting on the train with his ticket envelope, that he was about to find out.
