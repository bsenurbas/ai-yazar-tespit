Visibility came to the insects in the spring of the third year, and it came, as significant biological changes tend to come, not as a single event but as a threshold crossed by an accumulation of small changes that had been occurring for longer than anyone had noticed. The first reports were dismissed as misidentification. The second wave of reports produced a paper that was reviewed with considerable skepticism. By the time the skepticism had been addressed by a sufficient weight of independent confirmation, the phenomenon had progressed well beyond the stage that the original reports described.

What had changed was not the insects' behavior but their coordination. They still performed the functions insects had always performed—pollination, decomposition, the maintenance of the ecological processes that depended on them. They continued to do these things, but they did them now with a precision that went beyond the precision of instinct, and in patterns that could not be explained by pheromone signaling or any other mechanism previously described in the literature. They were, in a word that the entomologists who studied them were reluctant to use and eventually had to use, organized.

The question of what had produced this organization was the subject of the most contentious scientific debate of the decade. The question of what it meant for the ecological systems that depended on insect activity was the subject of a parallel debate that was less contentious but more immediately consequential. And the question of what it meant in a broader sense—for the understanding of intelligence, of coordination, of the relationship between the scale at which a thing operates and the scale at which it can be said to think—was a question that moved from the sciences into the philosophy journals and from there into the general culture, where it produced the usual mixture of wonder, alarm, and determined attempts to find a framework adequate to contain it.
