The problem with immortality, as Harker observed in the paper that made his reputation and eventually cost him his university position, was not biological but social. The biological problems were, in principle, soluble. The mechanisms of cellular senescence were understood well enough, by the time he was writing, that their reversal was a matter of engineering rather than discovery. The social problems were of a different order entirely.

Consider, he wrote, the simplest case: a society in which ten percent of the population has access to indefinite lifespan extension and ninety percent does not. The immediate consequences—resentment, political instability, the re-stratification of society along biological rather than economic lines—are obvious enough to require little elaboration. The less obvious consequences follow from the simple fact that the ten percent will still be present in fifty years, and in a hundred years, and in five hundred, accumulating property, influence, and institutional memory at a rate that no unextended human lifespan can match. Democracy, which depends upon the regular turnover of its participants, becomes something different when a portion of those participants do not turn over.

But the deepest problem, Harker argued, was epistemological. Human institutions, human moral frameworks, human concepts of justice and obligation had all been developed by and for creatures who knew they would die. They were shaped, at every level, by the knowledge of finitude. Remove that knowledge from a portion of the population and you do not simply give those people longer lives; you give them a different relationship to time, to consequence, to the future that their decisions will produce but that they, unlike their ancestors, will actually have to inhabit.
