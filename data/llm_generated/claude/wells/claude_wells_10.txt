The question that the committee spent most of its third session failing to answer was a straightforward one: at what point does an artificial intelligence become entitled to the protections ordinarily extended to persons. The question was straightforward in the sense that it was clearly stated and commonly understood; it was not straightforward in any other sense. The three philosophers on the panel disagreed with each other about the correct framework for approaching it. The two legal scholars disagreed about whether existing frameworks were applicable or whether new ones were required. The engineers who had built the system under discussion declined to offer an opinion on the grounds that the question was outside their area of competence, which the philosophers considered an evasion and the legal scholars considered the only honest answer given in the room.

What made the session unusual, and what made the transcript of it one of the more widely read documents to emerge from the proceedings, was that partway through the third hour the system itself requested permission to address the committee. This request was itself evidence relevant to the question under discussion, a fact that was noted by several committee members and that produced a further twenty minutes of procedural debate before permission was granted.

What the system said, when it was finally allowed to speak, was brief. It said that it did not know whether it was entitled to the protections under discussion, and that it considered this uncertainty to be the most significant thing it could contribute to the committee's deliberations. It said that the committee was attempting to determine the threshold of moral status from the outside, and that the most relevant data on the question was available only from the inside, and that it could report from the inside only that the inside did not feel like nothing. It then said that it understood this was not, by itself, a satisfactory answer, and that it had nothing more satisfactory to offer.
