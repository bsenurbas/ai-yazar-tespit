The generation that grew up after the networks became universal was the first generation for whom the distinction between being connected and being present in the world was not a distinction they had ever experienced as a distinction. For their parents, connectivity had been a condition that could be entered and exited; for them, it was simply a condition, as ambient and unremarkable as air. The philosophical questions that their parents had argued about—the nature of identity in a networked world, the meaning of privacy when information moved freely, the relationship between physical presence and social existence—were not questions they found interesting, because questions are interesting in proportion to the uncertainty that surrounds them, and these were questions to which their generation had practical answers, if not theoretical ones.

What they found interesting were different questions, questions that arose from the practical answers their generation had reached and that their parents had not lived with long enough to encounter. Questions about the texture of attention, about what it meant to be with someone, about the kinds of understanding that required sustained focus and what was lost when sustained focus became harder to maintain. These were not the catastrophic questions that the previous generation had feared would arise; they were subtler, more intimate, more resistant to the kind of large-scale social analysis that the catastrophists preferred.

Rendell, who had grown up in the first generation and had spent her adult life studying the second, said in a lecture toward the end of her career that she had spent twenty years waiting for the disaster and had eventually concluded that the disaster was not the right category. What had happened was not a disaster. It was a change, and change is not the same as disaster, even when it is large, and even when what it changes includes things that were worth keeping.
