The question of whether the Eloi were happy was one that occupied me considerably during my first weeks among them, and I found, the more I examined it, that it was not a question that admitted a simple answer. They were content. That much was clear. They moved through their days with the easy purposelessness of people who have no needs that are not met and no aspirations that are not exceeded by their circumstances. But contentment is not happiness, or not necessarily; it is the absence of dissatisfaction, which is a different thing, and what I could not determine was whether the Eloi experienced anything that deserved the name of positive pleasure, or whether they had arrived, through the long process that had produced them, at a state that was simply the elimination of everything that causes pain.

What they had lost, as I gradually came to understand it, was difficulty. Not merely hardship—the Eloi had no hardship—but difficulty in the broader sense: the resistance that makes effort meaningful, the uncertainty that makes achievement satisfying, the complexity that makes understanding rewarding. They had solved the problem of existence in the way that one solves a mathematical problem: by reducing it to its simplest form and arriving at an answer that is correct but no longer interesting.

I do not say this with contempt. The civilization that had produced the Eloi had presumably made choices, over a very long period, that seemed reasonable at each stage—choices that reduced suffering, that increased security, that eliminated the conditions of want and struggle that had caused so much misery in the earlier ages. Each choice, taken individually, was defensible. The cumulative result was a species that had optimized itself out of the qualities that had made it capable of optimization in the first place.
