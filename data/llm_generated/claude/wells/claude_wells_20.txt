The education of children in the new order was, from the beginning, the subject of the most sustained and least resolved debate of the period. Every other major question of the reconstruction had eventually produced a working consensus, however imperfect: the energy question, the governance question, the question of what to do with the institutional architecture of the old civilization, insofar as any of it remained. The education question did not produce a consensus, because it was not, at bottom, a question about methods or resources or curriculum. It was a question about what kind of people the new order wished to produce, and that question touched on disagreements so fundamental that no amount of deliberation could dissolve them.

The faction that called itself progressive—the word had retained its positive valence despite the best efforts of those who had used it to describe things that later proved not to be—argued that children should be educated for the world that existed, with its particular demands and its particular possibilities, and not for the world that had preceded it. The faction that called itself traditionalist argued that the things worth preserving from the old civilization were precisely those that had survived the discontinuity by being transmitted through education, and that to revise the curriculum entirely was to sever the connection to everything that had been learned at such cost.

Both factions were right about something and wrong about the conclusion they drew from it, which is the normal condition of serious disagreements about important matters. The children, meanwhile, were educated according to whichever faction had more influence in their particular locality, and grew up with the mixed results that follow from serious disagreements being resolved by geography rather than argument.
