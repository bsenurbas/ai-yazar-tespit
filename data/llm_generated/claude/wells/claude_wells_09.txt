The city that Marchetti explored in the second week of his survey had been abandoned for approximately forty years, which was long enough for the vegetation to have made serious inroads on the lower storeys of most structures but not long enough to have compromised the upper ones. He moved through it with the methodical attention of a man who is trying to understand something by examining its remains, which is, he reflected, a description that applies equally well to archaeology and to the kind of history he had been employed to write.

What struck him most was the orderliness of the abandonment. Cities abandoned in crisis—evacuated before floods, or emptied before advancing armies—leave evidence of haste: objects dropped, doors left open, the interrupted arrangements of daily life. This city had been left as one leaves a house at the end of a long tenancy, with deliberation. Furniture had been stacked. Papers had been organized and in some cases weighted with stones, as though against a wind that was expected but never came. In the central administrative building, Marchetti found a room containing several hundred files arranged in alphabetical order, their contents apparently intact, as though whoever had organized them expected someone to return and make use of them.

This was what he had been sent to understand: not why the city had been abandoned—that was already known, or thought to be known—but why it had been abandoned in this particular way. The orderliness implied intention, and intention implied either that the inhabitants had known they were leaving permanently and had made their peace with it, or that they had believed they were leaving temporarily and had arranged for a return that never occurred. The difference between these two explanations was, for the purposes of the history he had been asked to write, the difference between a conclusion and a question.
