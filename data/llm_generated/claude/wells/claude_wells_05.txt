The submarine Perseverance reached the coordinates at 0340 on a Tuesday morning, and Commander Reyes brought her to a stop at sixty metres depth and waited. The briefing she had received in Portsmouth three days earlier had been, by the standards of naval briefings, unusually candid about the limits of available information. They knew the coordinates. They knew the depth at which the anomaly had been detected. They knew that three previous unmanned probes had reached the site and returned data for approximately four minutes before ceasing transmission. They did not know what the probes had found, because the data from those four minutes had not been successfully decoded, despite the efforts of two teams working independently for six weeks.

What they were to do upon arrival was observe, record, and return. Under no circumstances were they to make contact. Under no circumstances were they to remain at the site for more than thirty minutes. These instructions had been delivered with the emphasis that indicated they were not suggestions, and Reyes had received them with the manner of someone who intends to follow them unless circumstances make following them impossible.

At 0347, the external cameras showed light. Not the bioluminescence of deep-sea organisms, which she had seen before, and not the reflected glow of the submarine's own floods. This was a steady, sourceless illumination that seemed to come from the water itself, or from whatever the water was in the immediate vicinity of the coordinates. It did not increase or decrease. It waited, as light that comes from a natural source does not wait, with a quality that Reyes could only describe in her report as attentive. She gave the order to begin recording and checked her watch. They had twenty-three minutes remaining.
