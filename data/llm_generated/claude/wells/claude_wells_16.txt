The experiment that Holroyd and Chen conducted in the spring of that year was, by the standards of their field, modest in ambition and conservative in design. They were not attempting to overturn existing theory; they were attempting to verify a prediction that existing theory made and that had not yet been tested at the scale their equipment allowed. The prediction was confirmed. The confirmation was, in itself, unremarkable. What was remarkable was the secondary result, which they had not predicted and which their equipment had recorded almost as an afterthought, in a data channel that neither of them had been monitoring at the time.

They noticed it three weeks later, during routine review of the complete dataset. Holroyd saw it first and said nothing for two days, reviewing the recording repeatedly with the focused attention of someone trying to find the error that would make the result explicable. She did not find the error. She showed it to Chen. Chen spent a day looking for the error. He did not find it either.

What the secondary channel had recorded was a pattern in the quantum noise of their experimental apparatus—a pattern that had no physical explanation within their theoretical framework, that was too regular to be random and too complex to be instrumental artifact, and that repeated, with minor variations, at intervals of precisely the age of the universe divided by a constant that neither of them could identify from the literature. They submitted their primary result for publication. They did not submit the secondary result. They were not certain, after considerable discussion, whether what they had found was a discovery or a mistake, and the consequences of publishing a mistake of that particular kind seemed, on balance, more manageable than the consequences of publishing a discovery of that particular kind.
