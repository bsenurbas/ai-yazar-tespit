The process by which the new language spread was documented in considerable detail only after it had already become, in the areas where it had taken hold, the primary means of communication. This retrospective quality of the documentation was itself a feature of the process: the language spread faster than the institutions designed to track such things could follow, partly because it was not recognized as a language in the early stages, being mistaken instead for a set of technical terms, a professional jargon, a local dialect, each of which categories applied partially and none of which captured what it actually was.

What it actually was, as Marchetti argued in the paper that established the field, was a language that had evolved to describe experiences that existing languages could not describe precisely, and that had spread because the experiences were common and the imprecision of existing description was causing practical difficulties. The experiences in question were the experiences of living in close interaction with artificial systems that processed information and produced outputs indistinguishable, in many contexts, from the outputs of human thought, and the practical difficulties arose from the fact that existing language was not well suited to making the distinctions that this situation required.

The new vocabulary that emerged was, like all language, imprecise in ways its users did not always notice. It made distinctions that the phenomena did not always support and collapsed distinctions that might have been useful. But it was better than what it replaced, and better is the normal standard by which languages are judged, the perfect being as unavailable in linguistics as it is in everything else.
