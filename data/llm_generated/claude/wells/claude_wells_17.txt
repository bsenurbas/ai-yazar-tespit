The city had been built for a population of three million and now housed eleven, and the infrastructure designed for the former number was being asked to serve the latter with consequences that engineers had predicted and administrators had chosen to address incrementally rather than comprehensively, on the reasonable grounds that comprehensive solutions required resources that incremental solutions did not, and that the political will to commit those resources had not yet materialized and showed no signs of doing so.

Yates had been studying urban systems for twenty years, and what he had learned in that time was that cities do not fail suddenly. They fail in the manner of materials under sustained stress: not at the moment the load is applied, but at the moment the accumulated fatigue of bearing that load exceeds the material's capacity to compensate. The compensation mechanisms are often invisible until they fail, because their function is precisely to absorb strain that would otherwise be visible, and they do their job quietly and without acknowledgment until the moment they cannot do it anymore.

He walked the older districts in the evenings, when the day's heat had begun to subside and the streets were full of people who lived in them rather than passing through them, and he tried to read the city the way he had been trained to read it: not as an aesthetic object or a social problem but as a system under load, with the calm diagnostic attention of someone who is not yet certain where the failure will occur but is reasonably certain that it will occur, and who believes, without much supporting evidence, that knowing in advance is better than not knowing, even when advance knowledge does not include the means to prevent what it predicts.
