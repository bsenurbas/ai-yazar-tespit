The experiment that changed everything was, like many experiments that change everything, not designed to change anything. It was designed to measure a small effect in a narrow domain, and it measured the effect, and the effect was as predicted, and the paper reporting it was accepted without difficulty by a journal of moderate prestige. What the paper did not report, because the researchers had not yet understood what it meant, was the secondary observation that would, in the years that followed, prove to be considerably more significant than the primary result.

The secondary observation was this: the effect they had measured in the laboratory was also detectable, at a much smaller magnitude, in the general environment. Not in a controlled setting, not under experimental conditions, but simply present, everywhere, as a background feature of the physical world that their instruments were sensitive enough to detect and that no previous instrument had been sensitive enough to notice. It had presumably always been there. They were simply the first to look with eyes capable of seeing it.

The implications were not immediately apparent, because implications of that scale rarely are. It was a theorist at a different institution, reading the paper eighteen months after its publication, who first worked out what the secondary observation meant in terms of the structure of the physical processes that underlay it, and who sent the lead author of the original paper a message that said, in its entirety: do you understand what you've found? The lead author had not, until that message arrived, fully understood. She understood it by the end of the week. She spent the following weekend walking on the coast near her home, looking at the sea, thinking about what it meant to have measured something that had always been present and never been seen, and what else might be there, waiting for the right instrument.
