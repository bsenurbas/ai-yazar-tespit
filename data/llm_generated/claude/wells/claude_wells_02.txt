The accelerator, as Gibbons called it in his notes—though the formal designation was considerably more cumbersome—was not intended to do what it did. It was designed to address a problem in agricultural efficiency: the acceleration of cellular division in food crops, reducing the time between planting and harvest. The mathematics were sound, the initial trials encouraging, and the funding body that supported the work had every reason to expect a practical application within three years.

What Gibbons had not adequately accounted for, as he acknowledged with characteristic understatement in the paper he eventually submitted to the journal that eventually agreed to publish it, was the absence of any mechanism for selective application. The accelerator worked on all cellular division within its range, without distinction between plant and animal tissue, between intended targets and unintended ones, between the cells of the crops in the test field and the cells of the two researchers who spent their afternoons working in it.

The effects were not immediately apparent. Gibbons noticed in October that he required less sleep. By November he had observed that minor injuries healed within hours rather than days. It was not until December, when he reviewed the photographs from the project's commencement and compared them with a photograph taken at the department's Christmas gathering, that the broader implications became unavoidable. He looked, in the recent photograph, approximately twelve years younger than he had looked in those taken eight months earlier. His colleague, who had spent marginally less time in the field, had lost perhaps eight years. They discussed it with the measured calm of scientists who have encountered a result that exceeds the scope of their experiment, and agreed that the first requirement was to determine whether the effect was permanent, progressive, or reversible. The second requirement, which they did not discuss until considerably later, was to determine what they intended to do with what they knew.
