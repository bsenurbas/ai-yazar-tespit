The evacuation of the coastal cities had been planned for forty years and had never been seriously expected to be necessary, because the plans had been made during a period when the data suggested the necessity was unlikely and the political will to act on even unlikely necessities was, in any case, insufficient. By the time the necessity became likely, the plans were outdated, the infrastructure they assumed had not been built, and the populations they were designed to move had grown considerably beyond the numbers the planners had considered.

It proceeded, under these conditions, with a combination of official organization and individual improvisation that is probably the best that can be said of any large-scale human response to circumstances that have exceeded the capacity of prior planning to address. There were failures of communication, failures of logistics, and failures of the kind that occur when systems designed for normal conditions are subjected to conditions that are not normal. There were also, less visibly, successes of the kind that tend to receive less documentation than failures: the informal networks that formed to fill the gaps left by official systems, the improvised solutions that worked well enough to be adopted more widely, the individual decisions made under pressure that proved, in retrospect, to have been better than the alternatives.

Yates, who had spent his career studying urban systems under stress, spent the three months of the evacuation making notes. He was not official; he had no role in the response and no authority to direct anything. He simply watched, and recorded, and tried to understand what he was seeing. He published his account seven years later. It was not widely read, because it did not fit neatly into either the category of heroic narrative or the category of cautionary tale, and those were the categories for which there was a market.
