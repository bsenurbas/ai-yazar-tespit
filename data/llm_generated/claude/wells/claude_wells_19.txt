The terraforming of the northern continent had been underway for sixty years when the first of the original inhabitants were discovered, and the discovery created the kind of problem that no one had planned for because no one had seriously believed it would need to be planned for. The surveys conducted prior to the project had been thorough by the standards of their time, and the standard conclusion—that the planet was uninhabited—had been reached by three independent teams working from the same data. The data had been correct. The conclusion had been wrong, in the way that conclusions can be wrong when they are drawn from evidence that is complete within its own frame but does not extend to what lies outside that frame.

The inhabitants were not visible to the survey instruments because they did not occupy the surface of the planet in the way that the survey instruments were designed to detect. They occupied it in a different way, at a different scale, and the sixty years of terraforming activity had disrupted their existence in ways that they had not been able to communicate and that the terraforming teams had not been able to perceive, because the two parties had no common framework for the communication of disruption or, indeed, for communication of any kind.

The committee established to address the situation was composed of scientists, administrators, and ethicists, and its deliberations were complicated by the foundational question of whether the word address was the right word, given that the situation had been ongoing for sixty years and whatever addressing might have been possible at the outset was considerably less possible now. The ethicists on the committee found this question tractable. The administrators found it less so. The scientists found it interesting, which was, under the circumstances, not entirely the response the situation called for.
