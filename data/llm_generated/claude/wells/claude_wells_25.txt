The children born in the decade following the Adjustment were, by any measure available to the researchers who studied them, different from the children born before it. Not dramatically different—there was no single characteristic that distinguished them clearly from their predecessors—but different in the aggregate, in the way that a population differs from a slightly earlier population without any individual member being obviously anomalous. They were, on average, better at certain kinds of pattern recognition. They were, on average, less susceptible to certain categories of social pressure. They processed certain kinds of information more rapidly, and they found certain kinds of ambiguity less distressing.

Whether these differences were caused by the Adjustment—by the specific changes to the electromagnetic environment that the Adjustment had produced—or by the social and behavioral changes that had followed from it, or by some combination of factors that included both, was the subject of a research literature that had grown very large without becoming very conclusive. The question was methodologically difficult, because the Adjustment had been global and simultaneous and had therefore left no unexposed population against which to compare the exposed one.

What was clear, and what the older generation found variously reassuring and troubling depending on their disposition, was that the children did not experience the world that had existed before the Adjustment as a loss. They knew about it, academically, in the way that one knows about historical conditions: as a context for understanding the present rather than as an alternative to it. The world they had inherited was, to them, simply the world. Whether it was better or worse than the one it had replaced was a question they found, on the whole, less interesting than the question of what came next.
