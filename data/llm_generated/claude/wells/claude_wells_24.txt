The bridge had been under construction for eleven years when the engineer responsible for its foundations made the discovery that rendered those eleven years, in his own assessment, both more significant and considerably more complicated than he had previously understood them to be. He had been conducting a routine inspection of the deepest caissons, at a depth that required a pressurized descent, when his instruments detected a cavity in the bedrock at a level several meters below the survey data that had informed the bridge's design.

The cavity was too regular to be geological. This was the conclusion he reached after three days of additional measurement and one day of not wanting to reach it. Geological formations do not have right angles. This cavity had four. It also had what appeared, from the instrument readings, to be a floor of a material different from the surrounding rock, a material that his instruments could not identify from its acoustic properties alone. He submitted a report to the project authority recommending a suspension of construction pending further investigation. The project authority reviewed the report and declined to suspend construction, on the grounds that the schedule and the budget did not accommodate delays of the kind that further investigation would require.

He resigned the following week. His report was filed and not acted upon. The bridge was completed two years later and opened to considerable ceremony. It stood, as far as I am aware, without incident. What is beneath it remains, as far as the public record indicates, unexamined. The engineer, who retired to a small house on the coast and wrote, in the last decade of his life, a memoir that was published in a very small edition and is now quite difficult to find, said in that memoir that he thought about the cavity most days, and that his uncertainty about its nature and origin had become, over the years, a permanent condition of his thinking rather than a problem awaiting resolution.
