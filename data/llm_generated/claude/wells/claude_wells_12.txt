The food shortage that followed the third consecutive harvest failure was managed, in the initial stages, with the competence that modern logistics makes possible. Reserves were redistributed, imports were accelerated, rationing was introduced with the orderly efficiency of a system that had been planned for precisely this contingency. What the planners had not adequately modeled was the psychological effect of a shortage that was expected to be temporary and was not.

In the first month, people adjusted. Human beings are adaptable in ways that consistently surprise those who study them from a distance and consistently fail to surprise those who live among them. Consumption patterns changed, waste was reduced, and the social solidarity that adversity sometimes produces made the early period of the shortage, paradoxically, one of greater communal cohesion than the period of abundance that had preceded it.

It was in the fourth month, when the expected improvement had not arrived and the official forecasts had been revised downward for the second time, that the adjustment began to fray. Not dramatically—history rarely moves dramatically, whatever its chroniclers later suggest—but in the accumulation of small frictions that precede larger failures. The redistribution system, which had operated on the assumption of temporary disruption, was not designed for sustained strain. The social solidarity, which had been genuine, began to shade into the territorial calculation that is its less admirable neighbor. And the confidence in official forecasts, which had already been tested, was not equal to a second revision. People began to make their own arrangements, which is to say they began to act on the reasonable assumption that official arrangements might not be sufficient, which made official arrangements less sufficient than they would otherwise have been.
