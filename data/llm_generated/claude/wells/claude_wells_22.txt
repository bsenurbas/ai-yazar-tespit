The submarine forest, as the newspapers took to calling it, had been growing for three years before it was noticed, because noticing required someone to look at the seafloor of the relevant section of the North Atlantic with instruments sensitive enough to detect structures on the scale of individual meters, and for most of those three years no one had looked with instruments of that sensitivity for reasons that had nothing to do with the forest and everything to do with the allocation of research budgets and the particular interests of the scientists who controlled them.

When it was noticed, it was noticed by accident, by a graduate student running a routine calibration of a seafloor mapping system, who brought the results to her supervisor with the expression of someone who suspects their equipment is malfunctioning. The equipment was not malfunctioning. What it had mapped, at a depth of two thousand meters in water that had last been surveyed in 1987, was a structure of biological origin—confirmed biological origin, not geological artifact—covering an area of approximately forty square kilometers and displaying a degree of organization that biological structures at that depth, according to everything then known about biology at that depth, were not supposed to display.

The paper that resulted from the subsequent investigation was reviewed by six scientists, three of whom requested substantial revisions, two of whom recommended rejection, and one of whom emailed the lead author at eleven o'clock at night to say that the paper was correct, that the implications were as significant as the authors suggested, and that they should prepare themselves for the response that correct papers with significant implications reliably produce, which is to say a response composed in roughly equal parts of intense interest and determined refusal to accept the implications.
