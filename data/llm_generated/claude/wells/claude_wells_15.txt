The probe reached the outer boundary of the system after a transit of twenty-two years, and the images it returned in the first hours after crossing that boundary were, in the assessment of the mission scientists who had spent careers preparing for their receipt, both more and less than expected. More, in the sense that the structures visible in the outer system were clearly artificial and clearly of a scale that no natural process could account for. Less, in the sense that they were not doing anything. They were simply present, as very old and very large things are present, with the settled permanence of objects that have been in place for longer than the civilization now examining them had existed.

The mission director gave a press conference in which she described the images as extraordinary and their implications as requiring careful study before conclusions could be drawn. This was accurate but insufficient, and the press conference produced the predictable result of a public that understood it had been told something significant and had not been told what that significance was.

What she had not said, and what the mission team had agreed not to say until their analysis was complete, was that the structures showed evidence of recent activity. Not recent in human terms—the evidence suggested activity within the last ten thousand years, which is to say within the period of human civilization, which is to say within the period during which the species that built these structures could, in principle, have been watching. The mission director was a scientist of considerable experience. She understood the difference between what the data showed and what it meant. She was less certain, as she drove home from the press conference through the ordinary streets of an ordinary city, that the distinction was going to matter for very much longer.
