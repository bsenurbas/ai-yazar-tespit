The first generation born after the Transition grew up in a world whose fundamental strangeness they could not perceive, because they had no prior world against which to measure it. To them, the presence of the others was simply a condition of existence, as the presence of weather is a condition of existence—something to be managed and accommodated rather than wondered at. It was the second generation, the children of those who remembered the before, who found themselves in the peculiar position of inheriting nostalgia for a world they had never inhabited.

I belonged to that second generation. My parents had been forty and thirty-eight at the time of the Transition, old enough to have formed the habits and expectations of the previous world, young enough to adapt to the new one with reasonable success. They did not speak of the before often, but when they did, it was with the particular tone of people describing a place they have left and do not expect to return to—not with grief exactly, but with the recognition that whatever they felt about it was beside the point, because the world had moved on and feeling about it would not move the world back.

What they could not give me, and what I spent a considerable portion of my adult life looking for in archives and accounts and the memories of people older than my parents, was a sense of what the before had actually felt like from the inside—not the events of it, which were documented well enough, but the texture of ordinary daily life in a world where the only minds were human ones, and where the sky, when you looked up at it, contained nothing that was looking back.
