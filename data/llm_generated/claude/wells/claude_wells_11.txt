The committee appointed to investigate the Broadhurst anomaly produced, after fourteen months of deliberation, a report of some three hundred pages that concluded, with the careful qualification that characterizes official documents produced by people who are not certain their conclusions will be well received, that the anomaly was real, that it was not attributable to any known natural process, and that its implications, while significant, were not yet sufficiently understood to warrant the public alarm that premature disclosure might produce. The report was classified. It remained classified for six years, during which time the anomaly continued to develop along lines that the committee had not predicted, because the committee had assumed, without adequate justification, that whatever was happening at Broadhurst was static.

It was not static. The first indication of this was the vegetation. Within a radius of approximately four hundred metres from the anomaly's center, plant growth had accelerated to a degree that could not be explained by soil composition, water table, or any other variable the agricultural scientists consulted by the committee were able to identify. By the end of the second year, the rate of growth had increased further. By the end of the third, certain species within the affected radius had begun to exhibit structural changes that their taxonomists described, with the careful understatement of people who are aware of how their words will sound in print, as departures from the established morphological baseline.

The military cordon that had been established in the first month was expanded twice. The local population, whose displacement had been managed through a combination of compulsory purchase orders and explanations involving subsidence, had largely accepted these explanations, because the alternative—that the official explanations were insufficient—was a conclusion that most people, faced with the bureaucratic thoroughness of official explanations, find difficult to sustain.
