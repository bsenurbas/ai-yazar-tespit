The dust had been falling for three years before anyone connected it to the decline in crop yields across the northern hemisphere. This was not, in retrospect, a failure of observation; it was a failure of the assumption that connected phenomena must be proximate in time. The dust was fine, barely visible in ordinary light, and it fell so gradually that only the instruments designed to measure atmospheric particulate matter detected it at all. The crop declines were attributed, in succession, to unusual weather patterns, to soil exhaustion, and to a newly identified fungal pathogen that later proved to have nothing to do with the matter.

The connection was made by a meteorologist in Helsinki named Saarinen, who had been tracking the dust for eighteen months and had noticed that its composition was inconsistent with any known terrestrial source. It contained compounds that did not occur naturally in the earth's crust, in any known meteorite sample, or in any industrial process currently in operation. She published her findings in a journal that was not widely read outside her specialty and waited for a response that did not come for seven months.

When it came, it came from three directions simultaneously: an agricultural chemist in Iowa, a soil biologist in New Zealand, and a marine biologist in the Azores who had been studying the decline of certain phytoplankton species and had independently identified the same compounds in ocean sediment samples. None of them had been looking for what they had found. None of them had, at the moment of finding it, a framework adequate to explain it. What they had, collectively, was the beginning of an understanding that the earth had been receiving something for three years—something distributed evenly, globally, and with a consistency that weather systems alone could not account for.
