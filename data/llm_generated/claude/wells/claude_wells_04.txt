Looking back on the early period of the Change—as it came to be called, for want of a more precise term—what strikes me most is how long ordinary life continued alongside it. The first reports appeared in the scientific press in April, were noted briefly in the general newspapers in May, and had been substantially forgotten by the public by June, when several other events of more immediate consequence occupied the attention of the world. This is not, I think, a reflection of unusual obtuseness on the part of the general population. It is simply the normal operation of human attention, which is limited in its range and necessarily selective in its application.

The Change was slow, and slowness is the enemy of attention. It did not announce itself with any of the traditional signs of crisis: there were no explosions, no dramatic failures of infrastructure, no moments of obvious before and after. There was only the gradual, barely measurable shift in the frequency of certain behaviors in certain species, first noted by ecologists, then by zoologists, then by the veterinary researchers who were the first to use the word "systematic" in connection with what they were observing. Animals were solving problems they had not previously been observed to solve. Not the well-documented intelligence of corvids and cetaceans—this was something broader, something that appeared in species whose cognitive capacities had not previously attracted scientific attention.

By the time the pattern was recognized for what it was, it had been developing for at least eight years. This was, in retrospect, either fortunate or unfortunate, depending on one's assessment of what would have occurred had humanity understood earlier what was changing and why.
