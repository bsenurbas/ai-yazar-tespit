The young woman who appeared at Baker Street on a Thursday evening in November was, as Holmes observed afterward, the most carefully unremarkable person he had encountered in several years—which was itself remarkable, since genuine unremarkability does not require effort. She had dressed to avoid notice, moved to avoid notice, and spoken, initially, in the careful neutral tones of someone who had rehearsed the absence of distinguishing characteristics. Holmes found this, as he found most things that required significant effort to conceal, immediately interesting.

"You've come from Edinburgh," he said, before she had finished her second sentence. "Within the last twenty-four hours. You traveled alone, which you are not accustomed to. And you are not, despite the considerable trouble you have taken to appear so, an unremarkable person."

She stopped. The careful neutrality slipped, briefly, and what appeared beneath it was intelligence and something that might have been relief. "My colleague said you would see through it."

"Your colleague is kind. Most people see through performances that require this much maintenance, given sufficient attention. The question is whether they choose to say so." He gestured toward the chair. "Sit down and tell me what has happened in Edinburgh, Miss—"

"Harcastle. Physician. And what has happened is that my patient has confessed to a crime that the evidence makes impossible for him to have committed, and the police are preparing to accept the confession, and I am the only person who believes he is lying about his own guilt." She sat. "I need someone who understands why a person would confess to something they did not do, and what they are protecting by doing so."

Holmes's eyes had sharpened. "Now that," he said, "is an interesting problem."
