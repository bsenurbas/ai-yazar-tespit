"You have been robbed," said Holmes to the young man who sat before us, "but not in the way you believe. The watch was taken to be returned. The letters were copied, not stolen. And the man you suspect had no part in it, which is why every line of evidence points to him with such suspicious convenience." He pressed his fingertips together and regarded our visitor with the particular expression of sympathetic precision that he reserved for clients who had come to him with the wrong question. "The correct question is not who took your property, but why someone wished you to ask that question."

The young man—a Mr. Gerald Forsythe, twenty-three years old and newly come into his late uncle's estate—looked at Holmes with the expression I had seen many times on the faces of people who had expected either confirmation or denial and received instead a rearrangement of the entire landscape. "Then who?" he managed.

"Someone who knows you well enough to predict your suspicions and methodical enough to arrange the evidence to match them. That narrows your acquaintance considerably." Holmes rose and crossed to the window. "I should like to examine the estate accounts for the past eighteen months, and I should particularly like to speak with whoever has been managing the investment portfolio in the period since your uncle's health began to fail. The watch and the letters are a performance, Mr. Forsythe. The real theft has been happening in the columns of a ledger, quietly and legally and with every appearance of diligent service." He turned. "Watson, I think we shall be in the country by Thursday."
