The woman who came to Baker Street on the morning of the fourteenth was, Holmes remarked afterward, the most accomplished liar he had met in fifteen years of professional practice—a distinction he offered not as a condemnation but as a form of respect, for Holmes had always maintained that the capacity for sustained deception required a quality of imagination that he found, in its proper application, genuinely admirable.

She gave her name as Mrs. Penrose and her business as the recovery of certain letters that she said had been taken from her late husband's study. Her composure was perfect: the grief measured, the anxiety controlled, the appeal to Holmes's discretion delivered with the right degree of reluctance, as though she had overcome considerable pride in coming to him at all. It was an excellent performance.

"You have been in Brussels within the past fortnight," said Holmes, when she had finished.

She did not flinch. "I have not left London in six weeks."

"The mud on your left boot is river mud of a particular clay composition not found on any Thames tributary above Wandsworth. Brussels has three such rivers within the city boundaries. You are, moreover, left-handed—you reached for your bag with that hand before catching yourself and using the right—which means the mud was acquired not in passing but by kneeling, or crouching, at the water's edge." He leaned forward slightly. "The letters you want recovered, Mrs. Penrose, were not taken from your husband's study. They were placed there. By you. Six months ago. And the person you wish me to recover them from knows precisely what they contain and has every intention of using them. I think you had better tell me the truth. It will save us both considerable time."
