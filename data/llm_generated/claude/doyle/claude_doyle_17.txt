The woman had been in Baker Street for ten minutes before she said anything that Holmes considered worth responding to. He sat throughout her initial account with his eyes half-closed and his fingertips together in the attitude I had come to recognize as active listening rather than inattention, and it was only when she reached the detail about the second key—mentioned in passing, as though it were incidental—that he opened his eyes fully and leaned forward.

"The second key," he said. "Tell me about it again."

She looked surprised, as clients often did when Holmes extracted significance from what they had considered the least important part of their account. "It was just a key. On the hall table, when I arrived. I assumed it belonged to my husband."

"Did you ask him?"

"He said he had never seen it before."

"And you believed him?"

A pause. "I—yes. He seemed genuinely puzzled."

"A key that neither occupant of a house can account for is not incidental," Holmes said. "It is the most interesting object in your narrative. What did you do with it?"

"I put it in the drawer of the hall table."

"Is it still there?"

Another pause, longer this time. "I don't know. I haven't checked."

Holmes rose. "Then we must check. Watson, your coat. The key will either still be in the drawer, in which case we have a starting point, or it will have disappeared, in which case we have a confirmation that someone with access to your house is paying close attention to what you do and do not notice." He looked at the woman. "Either way, the key is the case. Everything else you have told me is context."
