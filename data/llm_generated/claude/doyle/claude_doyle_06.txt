It was Holmes's habit, when a case had reached what he called its "clarifying moment," to become very still. Not the stillness of inaction, but the stillness of a mechanism wound to its tightest point and waiting for the release. I had seen it often enough to recognize it: the slight narrowing of the eyes, the pipe held without being drawn upon, the quality of attention that made ordinary silence feel charged, as though the air itself were listening.

We were standing in the garden of the Alderton house at half past nine in the evening, the moon sufficient for our purposes and the windows of the house dark save for the single light in the upstairs library that Holmes had been watching for twenty minutes.

"He will come out within the quarter hour," Holmes said quietly. "He has been reading the letter we left on the desk, and he now understands that we know what it contains. His options are to remain and be questioned, or to run and confirm everything. He will run. Men of his temperament always run; they confuse movement with solution."

"And if he is armed?"

"Then your services as a medical man will not be required, and your services as a companion will be very much valued." There was, as there occasionally was in Holmes, a note beneath the detachment that might, in another man, have been called affection. "He is not, I think, a violent man. He is merely a frightened one, which is, in its way, more dangerous. Stay to the left of the gate. And Watson—whatever he says when we take him, do not believe the first version of it. He has had twenty minutes to compose his story, and he is, as we have established, a man of some ingenuity."

The library light went out.
