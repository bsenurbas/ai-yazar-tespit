The case of the missing musicologist began, as Holmes later remarked, with the one piece of evidence that Inspector Lestrade had decided was irrelevant, and ended, as a consequence, considerably later than it needed to. The evidence in question was a metronome, found ticking on the desk of the absent Professor Vane, set to a tempo that his housekeeper confirmed he never used. Lestrade had noted it and moved on. Holmes, upon hearing the description, had stopped entirely.

"A metronome set to the wrong tempo in the study of a man who is precise about tempo," he said. "That is either a message or a struggle. If a message, it was set deliberately before he left—or was taken. If a struggle, it was knocked and reset accidentally by someone who did not know or did not care about the significance of the setting."

"How do you distinguish between them?" I asked.

"By examining the rest of the study for signs of struggle, which Lestrade has told us were absent, and by asking what message a tempo of forty-two beats per minute might convey to someone who would recognize it." He had already reached for his coat. "Forty-two is the catalogue number of a work by a composer with a particular connection to the Professor's field. The work is unpublished. The Professor is one of four people who knows of its existence." He paused at the door. "We are not looking for a missing man, Watson. We are looking for a man who has hidden himself, and who has left a direction for anyone intelligent enough to read it. Whether he hid himself voluntarily or under duress is the question that will determine whether we find him in a library or a cellar."
