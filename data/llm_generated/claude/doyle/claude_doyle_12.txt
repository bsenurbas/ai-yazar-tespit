The village of Stoke Marden was, as Holmes had predicted on the train, precisely the kind of place where nothing of consequence appeared to happen and where, for that reason, the things that did happen accumulated a significance they would not have had elsewhere. It was a village of perhaps four hundred souls, a Norman church, two public houses, and a post office that also served as a general store, and it had produced, in the space of three weeks, two unexplained deaths, one disappearance, and a level of official bafflement that had prompted the local constable to write to Holmes with a letter that managed, in three pages, to be simultaneously detailed and entirely unhelpful.

"He has given us the facts," Holmes said, folding the letter as our trap made its way from the station, "but not the shape of the facts. The two deaths are described as accidents. The disappearance is described as voluntary. What he has not told us, because it has not occurred to him to notice it, is that all three events took place within a quarter-mile radius of the old mill, and that the mill has been empty for eighteen months, since its previous tenant left without notice and without, apparently, taking anything with him."

"You think the mill is significant?"

"I think the mill is the only thing that is significant. The deaths and the disappearance are consequences. The mill is the cause, or rather, whatever has been happening at the mill is the cause." He looked out at the passing countryside. "I should have come sooner. When three apparently unrelated events cluster around a single location in a village this size, the location is never unrelated."
