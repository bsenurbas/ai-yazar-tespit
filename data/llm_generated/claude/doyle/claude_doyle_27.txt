Holmes had a habit, in the early stages of a case, of becoming disagreeable. Not deliberately—or not entirely deliberately—but in the manner of a man whose attention has been fully committed to one thing and who finds the ordinary demands of social existence an interruption he resents. I had learned to make allowances for this, and to interpret the sharpness of his remarks during these periods as a measure of his engagement rather than a reflection of his opinion of me.

He was particularly disagreeable during the Fenwick investigation, which told me that the Fenwick case was interesting him more than he wished to admit. He had accepted it, he said, because the alternative was the monograph on cigarette ash that had been waiting three months for his attention, and the monograph was beginning to seem less urgent than it once had. This was Holmes's way of saying that the case had caught him, which was always more reliable than his direct assessments.

"The body was found at the bottom of the stairs," he said on the third day, not looking up from the notes spread across his desk. "The official conclusion is that he fell. The fall is consistent with the injuries. What is not consistent with the injuries is the expression on his face, which the police surgeon noted and then ignored because expressions are not, in his view, evidence."

"What was the expression?"

"Surprise. Complete, unguarded surprise." Holmes looked up then. "A man who falls on his own staircase in his own house at eleven o'clock at night does not look surprised. He looks frightened, or pained, or simply blank. He looks surprised only if something caused the fall that he did not expect." He returned to his notes. "What was on the staircase that should not have been there, and who put it there, is the whole case. Everything else is furniture."
