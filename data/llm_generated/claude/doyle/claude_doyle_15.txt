The telegram arrived at a quarter past seven, and Holmes had read it, formed a preliminary hypothesis, and begun packing before I had finished my breakfast. I had learned not to ask questions at this stage; the answers would come on the train, or in the cab, or at whatever point Holmes determined that sharing his thinking would be useful rather than merely satisfying to my curiosity.

The answers came, on this occasion, as we crossed Waterloo Bridge in a hansom, with the Thames grey and cold below us and the city going about its morning business on either bank.

"The telegram is from a woman," Holmes said, "though it is signed with a man's name. The handwriting is a woman's—the particular formation of the capital letters is diagnostic—and she has signed it with a name that is not hers because she is either protecting herself or protecting whoever she is asking us to help. The address is a hotel in Bloomsbury, which means she is not local and did not wish to receive us at wherever she is actually staying."

"Why not?"

"Because wherever she is actually staying is connected to the matter she wishes to discuss, and she does not yet trust us sufficiently to reveal that connection. She will tell us the hotel is more convenient. It is not more convenient—the hotel is twenty minutes from Baker Street and her actual location, which I expect is somewhere in Southwark, would have been closer." He glanced at me. "The case concerns a missing person who is not missing voluntarily, and the person she has signed the telegram for is the one responsible for their disappearance. She knows this and cannot prove it. That is why she has come to us."

"You can deduce all that from a telegram?"

"I can deduce all that from the fact that she sent it at seven in the morning. Urgency of that kind comes from fear, not inconvenience."
