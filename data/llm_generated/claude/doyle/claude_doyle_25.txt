It was a point of some professional pride with Holmes that he did not, in general, make mistakes. He made incomplete observations, which he distinguished carefully from mistakes—an incomplete observation being one that could be corrected by the acquisition of additional data, whereas a mistake involved a reasoning error that additional data alone could not correct. In seventeen years of association, I had seen him make, by his own accounting, four mistakes. The Hartley affair was the fifth, and it was the one he spoke of least.

The error was not in his reasoning but in his premise. He had assumed, with a confidence that the available evidence appeared to justify, that the person who had commissioned the crime was the same person who had benefited from it. It was a reasonable assumption. It was wrong. The actual commissioner had not benefited at all, and had not intended to; the crime had been committed for reasons that had nothing to do with benefit in any material sense, and everything to do with a motive that Holmes later said he should have considered earlier and had not because it was, as he put it, the motive of a person who does not calculate.

He said this without self-pity and without the excessive self-criticism that would have been equally uncharacteristic. He said it in the manner of a man updating a working model with new information, which is what it was. Then he said, which was less characteristic: "There are people whose actions cannot be predicted from rational analysis of their interests, Watson. I have always known this in the abstract. I find I am still, occasionally, surprised by it in practice."

I did not comment. It seemed to me, and I believe it seemed to him, that the observation required no supplement.
