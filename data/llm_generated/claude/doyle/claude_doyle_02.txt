Holmes had been in one of his black moods for three days when the telegram arrived, and I confess I welcomed the interruption as much for its effect upon my friend's spirits as for any professional interest the case might offer. He read it twice, handed it to me, and reached for his pipe with the first sign of animation he had shown since Monday.

The telegram was from a Colonel Ashworth of the Gloucestershire constabulary, and it read: "BODY FOUND IN LOCKED BOATHOUSE STOP NO SIGNS OF ENTRY OR EXIT STOP DECEASED NOT KNOWN TO HAVE SWUM STOP ALL ASSISTANCE GRATEFULLY RECEIVED."

"A locked room," said I.

"Disappointing phrase," Holmes replied. "It tells us the policeman's conclusion before we have had the pleasure of forming our own. A room is never truly locked, Watson; it is merely closed in a way that has not yet been understood. What interests me is the last detail. A man who does not swim is found dead in a boathouse. Why was he in the boathouse at all?"

"To meet someone?"

"Or to retrieve something. Or to hide something. Three possibilities, each with its own implications for the lock. By the time we reach Gloucestershire, I expect I shall have reduced them to one." He was already reaching for his coat. "Wire Ashworth that we take the four-fifteen. And Watson—bring your revolver. The countryside is picturesque this time of year, but I have a feeling this particular corner of it contains something that has no interest in being picturesque."
