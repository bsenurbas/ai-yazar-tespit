It was Mrs. Hudson who first remarked that the gentleman had been sitting in the street below for the better part of two hours, and it was Holmes who, without troubling to rise from his chair or look out the window, said that the gentleman would come up within the next ten minutes, that he was debating whether his pride would survive the admission that he required help, and that when he did come up he would initially describe his problem in terms that concealed its actual nature, because the actual nature of his problem was something he found embarrassing rather than merely inconvenient.

The gentleman arrived in eight minutes. He was a solicitor of perhaps fifty, prosperous, accustomed to being the most capable person in most rooms he entered, and visibly uncomfortable in the position of supplicant. He gave his name as Mortlake and his problem, as Holmes had predicted, as a matter concerning a client's estate that had produced certain irregularities he wished to have clarified.

Holmes listened to this for approximately three minutes. Then he said: "Mr. Mortlake, the irregularities in your client's estate are not the reason you are here. You are here because you believe you have made an error of professional judgment that may have contributed to your client's death, and you wish to know whether that belief is correct before you decide what to do about it. The estate irregularities are real, but they are a symptom. The question you actually wish to ask me is whether the death was natural." He paused. "Was it?"

Mortlake was quiet for a long moment. Then he said: "That is precisely what I do not know."

"Good," said Holmes. "Uncertainty is a more honest starting point than a conclusion. Tell me about the death."
