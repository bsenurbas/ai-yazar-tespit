The theft from the Alderton Gallery was discovered on a Wednesday morning and reported to Scotland Yard by noon, and Lestrade brought it to Baker Street by two o'clock with the air of a man who has already formed a theory and wants it confirmed. Holmes listened to him with the patient courtesy he extended to official theories he found inadequate, asked three questions, and then told Lestrade that his theory was wrong in its identification of the thief but correct, interestingly, in its identification of the method.

"The method is the point," Holmes said, when Lestrade had gone with the slightly dazed expression that tended to follow these interviews. "Whoever planned this theft understood the gallery's security arrangements in considerable detail. That level of knowledge could be obtained in three ways: inside information, prior reconnaissance, or documentation. The first is the most common explanation for this type of theft and is therefore what Lestrade has assumed. I think it is the least likely in this case."

"Why?"

"Because the theft was too clean for inside information. Inside information produces thefts that are efficient in the wrong places—where the informant's knowledge is strong—and careless in the right ones—where it is weak. This theft was uniformly precise, which suggests documentation rather than a human source. Someone has seen a plan of the gallery's security arrangements." He stood. "The question is who has access to such a plan, and the answer to that question will tell us considerably more about who commissioned this than about who carried it out. The thief is a professional and will be difficult to trace. The client is an amateur and will not."
