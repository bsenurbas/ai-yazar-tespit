It was during the bitter February of 1895 that Holmes first drew my attention to the peculiar affair of the Harwick inheritance, though at the time I had no reason to suppose it would occupy us for the better part of a fortnight and bring us into contact with one of the most audacious forgers that Holmes, in his considerable experience, had ever encountered. I had called at Baker Street on a Thursday evening to find my friend standing at the window, his pipe unlit, his eyes fixed upon the street below with that quality of focused inattention which I had learned to associate with a problem already half-solved in his mind.

"You are thinking of taking a cab," said he, without turning.

"I had considered it," I admitted, somewhat startled.

"Don't. The particular cab you are watching has been standing at the corner for forty minutes. Its driver reads his newspaper with the page held too high, and he has not turned it in a quarter of an hour. He is watching this building, Watson, not waiting for a fare."

I looked again and saw that Holmes was right. "Who is he?"

"That," said Holmes, finally turning from the window with the swift movement that marked the transition from observation to action, "is precisely what makes this interesting. He is wearing the livery of the Harwick household—I recognize the particular shade of burgundy and the double-breasted cut—and the Harwick coachman, unless I am very much mistaken, died in a riding accident last November. Fetch your coat. I think we had better have a conversation with a dead man before he decides we have seen too much of him."
