"The curious thing about this case," Holmes remarked, as our train pulled out of Paddington into the grey October afternoon, "is not the crime itself, which is straightforward enough, but the effort that has been expended in making it appear complicated. Someone has worked very hard to give us too many suspects, Watson. It is the criminal's equivalent of a conjurer's misdirection—look at the variety of hands, and you will not notice which hand holds the card."

"And which hand does?" I asked.

"We shall see it more clearly when we have spoken to the groundskeeper. He is the one person in this affair who has not volunteered information, and that is always significant. Innocent men are eager to explain themselves. Guilty men are silent. Men who know something without having done anything are merely careful." He gazed out at the passing fields. "The victim was known to three people in that house well enough to have admitted them to his study after ten o'clock at night. One is dead, one has an alibi I have not yet tested, and one has given us an account so precise in its detail that it could only have been rehearsed. Rehearsed accounts have the ring of truth, which is exactly why they should be trusted least."

I had long since learned not to press Holmes when he was in this particular vein of thought—half conclusion, half provocation, designed as much to organize his own reasoning as to inform mine. I settled back in my seat and watched the countryside darken toward evening, confident that by the time we reached our destination he would have arranged the facts into an order that would seem, in retrospect, to have been obvious all along.
