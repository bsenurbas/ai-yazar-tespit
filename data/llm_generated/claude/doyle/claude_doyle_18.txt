Of the many singular cases that occupied Holmes during the winter of 1896, none illustrated more clearly his maxim that the most dangerous criminals are those who understand the difference between appearing guilty and being guilty than the matter of the Twickenham forgeries. The accused, a mild-mannered archivist named Fenner, had been found with every piece of incriminating evidence one could reasonably hope to find in the possession of a forger: the materials, the exemplars, the completed documents, and, most damningly, the payment records. The prosecution considered the case closed before it had properly opened.

Holmes considered it interesting precisely for that reason.

"When evidence is this complete," he remarked, as we reviewed the files that Fenner's solicitor had sent around, "it is almost always because it has been assembled rather than discovered. A genuine forger accumulates materials gradually, imperfectly, with the gaps and inconsistencies of a human life. This—" he indicated the inventory of items found in Fenner's rooms "—is a collection. Someone has been very thorough."

"You think he was set up?"

"I think the question of whether he was set up is less interesting than the question of who had sufficient knowledge of forgery to plant convincing evidence of it. To frame a forger convincingly, you must know what forgery looks like from the inside." Holmes set down the file. "Which means our actual forger is not in custody. He is somewhere comfortable, watching the proceedings with the satisfaction of a craftsman who has done his best work and had it appreciated, however indirectly."
