The case had one of those beginnings that Holmes particularly relished: a client who withheld the essential fact not from dishonesty but from the genuine conviction that it was irrelevant. Miss Hartley was a sensible woman of perhaps thirty-five who had spent twenty minutes describing the theft of her employer's correspondence before Holmes interrupted her with the question that mattered.

"The correspondence that was taken—was any of it in a language other than English?"

She paused. "There were several letters in French. But I don't see how—"

"And the French letters were taken while the English ones were left?"

Another pause, longer this time. "Yes. How did you know?"

"Because the English letters are on your employer's desk and the French ones are not, and you described the thief's movements as too precise for random selection. Someone knew exactly what they wanted, which means they knew exactly what existed, which means they have had access to information about your employer's correspondence before last Tuesday." Holmes stood. "The theft is recent; the planning is not. Watson, I believe we are looking for someone who has been in this house at a minimum of two previous occasions—socially, professionally, or in a capacity that gave them access to the study without raising comment. Miss Hartley, I should like a list of every person who has visited the house in the past six months. Not guests—everyone. Tradesmen, repairmen, casual callers. Everyone."

She looked at the scale of the request with visible dismay.

"Take your time," said Holmes, not unkindly. "Accuracy is more useful than speed."
