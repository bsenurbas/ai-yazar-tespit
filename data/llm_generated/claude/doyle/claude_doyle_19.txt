"The difficulty with this case," Holmes said, as we made our way back through the evening streets toward Baker Street, "is that I have been given too many suspects and too much motive. In my experience, when a crime scene contains evidence pointing clearly to four different people, the crime was committed by a fifth."

I had heard variations of this principle before, but the Ashwood affair seemed to me to present it with unusual clarity. We had spent the afternoon at a country house where a valuable document had disappeared from a locked safe, and where four members of the household had, severally and independently, both means and motive. The local inspector was delighted with the abundance of suspects. Holmes was not.

"Consider what it requires," he continued. "Four people, each with a plausible motive, each with a plausible opportunity, each leaving just enough evidence to be considered without quite enough to be conclusive. That is not the natural state of a crime. That is choreography." He paused at a crossing. "Someone in that house is not a suspect. Someone in that house has arranged for everyone else to be a suspect, which means someone in that house is either invisible to us or has made themselves appear to be above suspicion."

"The host?"

"Possibly. More likely someone whose position in the household makes their presence unremarkable and their access unquestioned." He hailed a cab. "Tomorrow I should like to speak with whoever is responsible for the household accounts. Not because I suspect them—I don't, yet—but because the person who manages money always knows where the bodies are buried, metaphorically speaking." A pause. "In this particular case, I am not entirely certain it is metaphorical."
