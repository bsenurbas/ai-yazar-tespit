We had been in Dartmoor for two days when Holmes found the thing he had not been looking for. We had come for the Brentwood matter—a straightforward affair of a disputed boundary and an allegation of survey fraud—and Holmes had been, by his own assessment, bored. The boredom ended on the second afternoon, when he paused on the moor half a mile from the Brentwood estate and stood very still for a long moment, looking at a patch of ground that appeared, to my eye, to be identical to every other patch of ground we had crossed.

"Someone has been here regularly," he said. "For several years. The path is faint but consistent, and it comes from the direction of the old quarry rather than from any of the farms." He crouched and examined the ground with his lens. "Boots of a particular make, worn to a particular pattern. Not a local man—the wear is on the wrong side for someone who walks this terrain habitually. A visitor, then, but a regular one." He stood. "The Brentwood survey can wait an hour."

The quarry was abandoned and had been for thirty years. At its center, in a shallow depression that was invisible from the rim, Holmes found a tin box secured with a lock that he opened in under a minute and that contained, along with a number of documents of considerable interest to the Brentwood matter, a correspondence that had nothing to do with it—a correspondence that turned out to be the loose end of a case Holmes had considered closed for four years, and whose resolution occupied him for the better part of the following month and produced, I will only say, consequences for several people in positions of some eminence that were, in Holmes's judgment, both just and overdue.
