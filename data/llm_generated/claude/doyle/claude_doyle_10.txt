Of all the cases that Holmes and I investigated during our long association, few taught me more about the nature of his methods than the apparently simple matter of the Colworth cipher. It arrived on a Wednesday—a single sheet of paper covered in numbers, sent without a covering letter or a return address, with only Holmes's name on the envelope—and it occupied him for less than four minutes.

"Beautifully simple," he said, setting it aside with the slight disappointment of a man who has been offered a puzzle that turned out to be a picture. "A substitution cipher with a key word of five letters, the key word being the sender's Christian name. The message reads: come alone tonight Aldgate nine o'clock the matter concerns Milverton."

I sat forward. "Milverton? But Holmes—"

"Yes, the name still carries weight, even at some remove from his death. Whoever sends this message knows that and has used it deliberately." He picked up the paper again and examined it with fresh attention. "What is interesting is not the cipher—that is straightforward—but the choice of key word. They have encoded it with their own name, which is either a sign of remarkable confidence or a very deliberate invitation to be identified. In either case, they wish me to know who they are." He set the paper down with decision. "We will go, Watson, but not alone, and not at nine o'clock. I have found, as a general principle, that meeting someone on their terms when they have taken such care to specify those terms is the surest way to ensure that the conversation goes entirely as they have planned."
