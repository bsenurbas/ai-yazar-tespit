Holmes received three visitors that Tuesday, and dismissed two of them within minutes—one because his problem was, as Holmes put it, a matter for an accountant rather than a detective, and one because Holmes had solved it before the man had finished describing it and saw no value in prolonging the interview. The third visitor, a pale woman of perhaps forty-five who gave her name as Mrs. Eldridge and her occupation as a schoolteacher in Hertfordshire, he listened to for the better part of an hour without interruption, which I had learned to recognize as a sign that she had said something in the first five minutes that he found genuinely unusual.

When she had finished, he sat for a long moment with his eyes closed and his fingers pressed together, in the posture that meant he was not resting but arranging.

"You have told me everything except the thing that frightened you into coming here," he said at last, without opening his eyes. "The facts you have described would not, in themselves, produce the level of distress I observe in you. There is a fact you have withheld, either because you believe it is not relevant or because you believe I will not credit it. Given that you have come to me rather than to the police, I suspect the latter."

Mrs. Eldridge was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "The man who was following me—I recognized him. He died four years ago. I attended the funeral. I saw the coffin lowered."

Holmes opened his eyes. "Now we are getting somewhere," he said. "Watson, I think we shall need the afternoon train to Hertfordshire. Mrs. Eldridge, I should be grateful if you would describe the funeral in as much detail as you can recall."
