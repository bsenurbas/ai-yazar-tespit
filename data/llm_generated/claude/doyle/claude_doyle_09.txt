Holmes's methods were, as I have had occasion to remark in these pages, frequently uncomfortable for those subjected to them, and never more so than when his conclusions were correct. The Pemberton affair was a case in point. We had been in the drawing room of Thornfield House for perhaps eight minutes when Holmes turned to the assembled family—two brothers, a sister, and a family solicitor of thirty years' standing—and said, in the pleasant conversational tone he used for his most devastating observations, that the will had not been forged, the signature was genuine, and the question was not what the late Mr. Pemberton had signed but why he had been persuaded to sign it six weeks before the date it bore.

The silence that followed had several distinct qualities. The elder brother's silence was the silence of a man recalculating. The sister's was the silence of relief. The solicitor's was the silence of professional alarm. And the younger brother's was the silence of someone who had just understood that the thing he had most carefully hidden was the first thing that had been found.

"The paper," Holmes continued, addressing his remarks to the younger brother with the focused attention of a man who has decided that courtesy requires directness, "was purchased in Edinburgh, where you spent four days in September on what you described to your family as a shooting holiday. The ink is of a variety not commercially available in England before October of this year. The witnesses whose signatures appear below your father's are both employed by a firm of inquiry agents with offices in that city." He paused. "I mention these details not to distress you, but because you will find it considerably easier to tell me why before Lestrade arrives to ask how."
