Lestrade had the expression of a man who has been proved wrong so many times that he has developed a philosophical relationship with the experience. He met us at the gate of the Fenwick property with his notebook already open and a report that managed, in the space of three paragraphs, to be simultaneously thorough and entirely beside the point.

"The household staff have all been interviewed," he said. "The butler confirms that Mr. Fenwick retired at half past ten. The cook heard nothing unusual. The parlour maid saw no one on the stairs after eleven. We have the murder weapon—a letter opener from the study desk—and the fingermarks on it belong to Mr. Fenwick himself, which suggests—"

"That someone put it in his hand," Holmes said. "Or that he was struck from behind first and the letter opener placed afterward to suggest a struggle. The fingermarks are on the handle?" Lestrade nodded. "Naturally. A man who picks up a letter opener in the normal course of his evening places his fingers on the handle. A man who uses it as a weapon places his fingers differently—toward the blade end, for control. Was there any bruising on the palm?"

Lestrade consulted his notes with the slightly defeated air of a man who had not thought to ask. "It doesn't say."

"Then we must look. And while we are looking, I should like to examine the fireplace in the study. Something was burned there last night—I can smell the particular residue of vellum from here—and whatever it was, I suspect it is more relevant than the letter opener." He moved past Lestrade with the focused energy of a man for whom a crime scene is a text to be read rather than a spectacle to be managed. "Come, Watson. The evidence is cooling."
