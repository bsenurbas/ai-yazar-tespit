Holmes had been silent for the better part of an hour, which in my experience meant either that he had solved the case and was arranging his conclusions into the order in which he intended to present them, or that he had encountered something that genuinely puzzled him. The distinction was visible, for those who knew him well, in the set of his shoulders: relaxed in the first instance, slightly forward in the second. His shoulders were forward.

"The difficulty," he said at last, "is that this crime has been committed by someone who knows how I think."

I looked up from my newspaper. "Surely that narrows the field considerably."

"On the contrary, it widens it to an uncomfortable degree. A great many people have read your accounts of our cases, Watson, and a considerable subset of those people have drawn conclusions about my methods from them. Whoever planned this has read those accounts carefully and has constructed a situation in which each piece of evidence points clearly to one conclusion, and in which that conclusion is almost certainly wrong." He stood abruptly and crossed to the window. "They have given me the solution they want me to find. Which means the actual solution is somewhere I have not yet thought to look."

"Where?"

"In whatever they have been most careful not to mention." He turned. "Go back through your notes on everything the client told us. Not what she said—what she did not say. The significant omission will be the thing she described as irrelevant when she introduced it."
