The peculiarity of the Wharton case lay not in its solution, which Holmes arrived at with his customary economy, but in the reaction of the client upon hearing it. Most clients, in my experience, received Holmes's conclusions with some mixture of relief, surprise, and the slightly deflated sensation of having paid for the obvious. Wharton received his with something that I can only describe as grief—a genuine, unperformed grief that sat oddly with the nature of what had been revealed.

"You knew," Holmes said, watching him. It was not a question.

"I suspected," Wharton said. "I came to you hoping you would find something I had not. Something that made a different explanation possible."

"There is no different explanation."

"No." He was quiet for a moment. "How long have you known?"

"Since the second interview. The third confirmed it." Holmes's voice had lost its usual crispness; there was something in it that I had heard only rarely, a quality that was not quite sympathy but was adjacent to it. "You could not have prevented it. The arrangement was made before you were aware of the relevant facts."

"That is not the same as not being responsible."

"No," Holmes agreed. "It is not." He stood. "What you do with the information is your own affair. I can tell you what happened. I cannot tell you what it means or what it requires of you. Those are questions outside my competence." He moved to the window, giving Wharton a moment that the man clearly needed. "Watson will see you out when you are ready. There is no hurry."
