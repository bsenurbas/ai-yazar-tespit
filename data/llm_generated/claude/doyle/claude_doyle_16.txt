Holmes examined the walking stick for perhaps ninety seconds, turning it in the lamplight with the focused attention he brought to objects that interested him, and then set it on the table with the air of a man who has extracted everything an object has to offer and is ready to share the proceeds.

"Your client is left-handed," he said, "has a dog of medium size, and walks this particular stick on uneven ground rather than on pavements—the wear pattern on the ferrule is diagnostic. He is also, unless I am mistaken, considerably taller than the stick's length would suggest appropriate, which means he acquired it from someone shorter than himself and has continued to use it from habit or sentiment rather than utility."

Inspector Gregson, who had brought the stick to Baker Street with the air of a man presenting evidence he expected to be found insufficient, looked from Holmes to the stick and back again with an expression that had moved, over the course of our acquaintance, from skepticism to a kind of resigned acceptance. "The dog we knew about," he said. "The left-handedness we did not."

"The grip wear is on the wrong side for a right-handed man. Elementary, though I grant you it requires knowing where to look." Holmes stood. "What you have not told me, Gregson, and what I suspect is the reason you have brought me a walking stick rather than simply describing it, is that your client claims he was not carrying this stick on the night in question, and yet it was found at the scene."

"He says he hasn't seen it in a month."

"Then someone took it from him a month ago, used it on the night in question, and either left it by accident or by design. The question is which." Holmes reached for his coat. "I rather think by design. Someone wishes your client to appear to have been present. That suggests he has an enemy with patience and access. Where does he keep his walking sticks?"
