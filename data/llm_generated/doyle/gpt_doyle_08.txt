The adventure of the brass key began upon one of those melancholy London mornings when the fog had crept so low that the hansoms seemed to swim rather than roll along Baker Street. Holmes was seated at the breakfast table, spreading marmalade upon his toast with the absent air of a man whose thoughts were far from domestic matters, when a telegram was brought up by Mrs. Hudson. He read it, smiled, and passed it across to me. “‘Come at once. Library locked. Master missing. Key in my possession. —Bramwell.’” I looked up in surprise. “A locked room, Holmes?” “No, Watson,” said he. “A room made to appear locked. The distinction is the whole case.”

By noon we were in a sombre house near Regent’s Park, where Mr. Bramwell, the late gentleman’s steward, awaited us with a face as grey as the sky. “The library door was bolted from within,” he said, “and yet Sir Julian was gone. We found this key upon the carpet.” Holmes took it in his palm and examined it through his lens. “A handsome object,” said he. “Too handsome for its duty. No scratches upon the stem, no wear at the teeth, and yet you say it opened the library door daily? Impossible. This key was never used for that lock.” Bramwell shifted uneasily. “Then how did it come there?” “Placed there by a person who knew the value of a symbol but not the habits of metal.”

Holmes crossed to the fireplace, stooped, and drew from the ashes a sliver of green wax. “Here is our true key,” said he. “The bolt was drawn from outside by means of a cord passed through the ventilator and sealed afterward with wax to hide the mark. Sir Julian left this room alive, and he left with someone he trusted.” He turned toward the portrait of a stern-faced woman above the mantel, then to a young nephew standing pale beside the desk. “You sent the note in your aunt’s name, brought him out through the conservatory, and returned to stage the miracle of the locked door. But you forgot one small fact: a brass key grows dull by service. Yours shines like a lie told in haste.”
