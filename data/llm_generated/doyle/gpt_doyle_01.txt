The affair began upon a raw November morning, when the fog lay so thick over Baker Street that the gas lamps glimmered like dull yellow coins seen through smoked glass. Holmes had been silent for nearly an hour, his long fingers pressed together and his eyes half closed, when Mrs. Hudson announced a visitor. “Show him up,” said my friend, without changing his position. A moment later there entered a nervous gentleman of perhaps forty, well dressed but mud-stained at the cuffs. “Mr. Holmes,” he cried, “I fear I am being followed.” Holmes opened one eye. “Not followed, Mr. Ellery. Watched. There is a difference, and in your case a most instructive one.”

The gentleman stared. “You know my name?” “It is written inside your hat,” said Holmes, taking it from the table. “You came from Hampstead by cab, though not directly, for the left wheel splashed river mud upon your trouser-leg, while the right bears the clay of the northern road. You stopped at a pawnbroker’s, not to pawn, but to inquire, since your watch-chain remains but the watch is gone. You are a banker’s clerk, recently entrusted with something of value, and your terror began last night.” Mr. Ellery sank into the chair. “By heaven, sir, it is true. A sealed packet was given into my care, and this morning I found the wax broken, though the packet never left my desk.”

Holmes sprang up with that sharp energy which marked the turn of his mind from observation to action. “Then the matter is plain enough to be dangerous. Watson, your revolver may be useful, though I trust not necessary. Mr. Ellery, you will return to your office, speak to no one, and place the packet where it was found. The man who opened it did not remove it because he had not yet learned what he sought. He will come again, and this time we shall be waiting.” He drew on his coat, his thin face alive with purpose. “Come, Watson. The game is not deep, but it has the merit of haste.”
