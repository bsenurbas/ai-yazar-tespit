The machine was completed in a shed behind the observatory, where its brass ribs and glass cylinders gave it the appearance of some delicate animal preserved for demonstration. Mr. Verrall did not call it a time machine, for he distrusted theatrical names. He described it instead as an apparatus for altering the rate of duration within a confined field. A clock placed beneath its dome lost twenty minutes while the world outside gained only three. A fly, imprisoned with the clock, lived out its brief afternoon in what seemed to us a matter of seconds.

At first the invention promised only practical inconveniences and commercial temptations. Meat might be aged in an hour; messages could be delayed without storage; a prisoner, some argued, might serve a year while the state lost only a week. Verrall received these suggestions with a professional sadness. He had not sought to make a servant for bankers, jailers, or hotel kitchens. He wished to know whether time, like air or heat, was a condition to be manipulated rather than a sovereign law.

On the night of the first human trial, he seated himself beneath the dome and set the regulator to a ratio no instrument had yet endured. We watched his face sharpen, pale, and grow strangely remote, as though he had moved a little farther from us without changing place. Then the glass clouded. When it cleared, the chair was empty, and upon the floor lay a gray dust arranged in the exact outline of a man. The clock inside the dome had advanced, not by hours, but by something more than nine hundred years.
