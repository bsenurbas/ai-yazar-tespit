It began, as many great disturbances begin, with a correction in a learned journal. Dr. Ingleby of Cambridge had observed that certain spores, preserved in a sealed glass tube since the expedition to the Antarctic plateau, had not merely survived their long imprisonment, but had increased in weight. The increase was slight, scarcely measurable by any commercial balance, but it persisted under repeated trials. To the ordinary reader, the matter suggested only an error of weighing; to Ingleby, it suggested that life might draw nourishment from sources not yet admitted by chemistry.

Within six weeks the green stain had appeared upon the inside of the tube, then upon the sealing wax, and finally upon the mahogany shelf beneath it. It did not spread like mould. It advanced with a deliberation almost architectural, laying down tiny hexagonal plates, each one clear at the edges and faintly luminous at the centre. Heat did not check it. Cold merely slowed it. Acids clouded it for a while, but by morning it resumed its pale and orderly expansion. Ingleby wrote to the Royal Society that the growth seemed less like a plant than an instrument, converting light, air, and perhaps some more general radiation into organized matter.

By autumn, panes of glass in half the laboratories of Europe had begun to bloom with the same quiet geometry. Men argued over quarantine, over sterilization, over priority of discovery, while the thing itself climbed window by window into the daylight. It made no attack upon mankind. It issued no poison, displayed no appetite, and showed no malice. Yet there was something more terrible in its indifference. It had found the earth suitable, and that was all.
