The change in the tides was first attributed to a misprint in the Admiralty tables. At Portsmouth the water rose seven inches above its predicted mark; at Dover it fell short by five. Such discrepancies, though inconvenient to pilots and harbour clerks, did not at once disturb the public mind. It required three days of repeated measurements, taken from ports as distant as Liverpool, Lisbon, and Alexandria, before the more thoughtful men of science began to suspect that the sea had not erred. Something had altered the earth’s balance.

The explanation came from young Bellairs, an unregarded mathematician at Greenwich, who had spent two years calculating the paths of bodies too faint for the telescope. He maintained that a dark planet, cold and almost without reflected light, had entered the outer marches of the solar system and was drawing, by its silent mass, upon the oceans of the earth. The newspapers treated the matter with their usual appetite for alarm, but Bellairs spoke of it plainly. There would be no immediate end of the world, he said. There would only be a gradual rearrangement of coasts, climates, harvests, and human confidence.

It was this last item which proved the most delicate. Men could raise embankments, abandon marshes, and move warehouses from the quays; they could not so readily abandon the belief that the world was built for their convenience. By winter, when the Thames came twice in one month over the lower steps of Westminster, the city had learned a new humility. The dark planet remained invisible, yet every wet cellar and flooded road testified to its passage, as a hand unseen may move a compass needle from across a room.
