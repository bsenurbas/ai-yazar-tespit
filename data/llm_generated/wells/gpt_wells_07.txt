The affair of the glass rain was first noticed in the provinces, where unusual weather is recorded with less theory and more care. A schoolmaster near Bath reported that, after a brief shower, the puddles in the road glittered with minute transparent flakes, each thinner than paper and cut into exact triangles. They melted neither in water nor in flame. Under the microscope they showed fine markings, not unlike print, though no alphabet on earth could be made of them.

For several days the phenomenon remained a curiosity. Children collected the flakes in bottles; jewellers tested them; one newspaper suggested that they were fragments of a passing comet. But when similar falls occurred in Vienna, Cairo, and Bombay, always at the same hour by Greenwich time, the matter became less amusing. Professor Elphinstone of the Royal Observatory found that the flakes were not falling from the clouds at all. They entered the atmosphere vertically, from beyond it, and slowed only in the last few miles, as if guided by some intelligence unwilling to waste its message upon impact.

The interpretation, when it came, was more disturbing than any invasion. The markings were not words but diagrams: maps of pressure, heat, mineral density, and nerve tissue. The earth had been surveyed, layer by layer, creature by creature, with a patience that made human science look like guesswork. No army appeared in the sky. No cylinder struck the common. Yet every roof in England now bore, in glittering silence, a fragment of the report that some remote observer had made of us.
