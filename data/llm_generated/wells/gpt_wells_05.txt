The first reports came from the coal districts, and were therefore received in London as one receives news of subsidence, damp, and labour unrest: with regret, but without imagination. Miners in Northumberland had broken into a chamber where no chamber should have been. Its walls were not rock, but a smooth black substance harder than steel and warm to the touch. In the centre stood a row of cylinders, each taller than a man, and within each cylinder could be seen, suspended in a yellow fluid, the folded shape of a creature unlike any animal known to natural history.

Sir Edmund Kyne, who was sent by the Geological Office, believed at first that the objects were fossils preserved by some extraordinary mineral process. This belief lasted until one of the cylinders opened. There was no explosion, no drama fit for the illustrated papers. A seam appeared, widened, and discharged its contents upon the floor with the soft precision of a valve. The creature within did not die in our air. It unfolded itself slowly, revealing limbs too numerous for comfort and eyes set not in a face but along the rim of its body, like instruments placed for a complete survey of the world.

It made no hostile movement. Indeed, its first act was one of measurement. From beneath its carapace it extended a filament, touched Kyne’s boot, the lamp, the stone wall, and finally the exposed hand of a miner who had been too astonished to retreat. Then, with a faint mechanical clicking, the other cylinders began to open. It became clear that mankind had not discovered a tomb beneath England, but a depot; not relics of a former age, but passengers waiting for conditions to become favourable.
