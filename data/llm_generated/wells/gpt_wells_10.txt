The disturbance was first observed in the museum collections. Beetles pinned and labelled half a century before began, not to move, but to alter. Their wing cases lengthened; their jaws took on new curves; one specimen of a common ground beetle developed, over three successive nights, a transparent membrane such as no entomologist had seen. The keepers suspected damp, then fraud, then some chemical action in the preservatives. But the changes continued in sealed drawers, in glass cases, and even in fossils embedded in stone.

Dr. Harcourt, who had spent his career among the patient dead of natural history, proposed an explanation so unsettling that he delivered it in the driest possible language. Evolution, he said, might not be fixed in the past as men supposed. It might be a force distributed through time, capable under certain conditions of acting backwards upon the record of life. Some new pressure, perhaps cosmic, perhaps terrestrial, had touched not living bodies alone but the whole sequence by which living bodies had come to be. The museum was not preserving history; it had become an index of history being revised.

The consequences were not immediate enough to frighten the public, which is often most alarmed by trifles and least by transformations too large for the day’s imagination. Yet in the following months, farmers reported lambs with unfamiliar bones, fishermen drew up blind silver fish from shallow rivers, and children were born with eyes that adapted to darkness in seconds. It was not that a new species had appeared among us. It was rather that mankind, having believed itself the latest sentence in nature’s book, discovered that the sentence was still being written.
